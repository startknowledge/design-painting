In an era where art and design are increasingly intertwined with experiential storytelling, the concept of an artist-curated space has gained unprecedented traction. Since its inception, the Shaker Outpost has become a pilgrimage site for design enthusiasts, historians, and artists alike, proving that even the most unconventional spaces can become cultural landmarks.

Who Is Maira Kalman, and Why Is Her Work So Influential?

Before diving into the Shaker Outpost, it’s essential to understand the philosophy and artistic sensibilities that shape Maira Kalman’s work. Born in 1949 in New York City, Kalman is a multi-disciplinary artist, illustrator, and writer whose career spans children’s books, editorial illustrations, and large-scale public art projects.

Kalman’s Signature Style: Playfulness Meets Depth

Kalman’s work is often described as whimsical yet profound, blending vibrant colors, hand-drawn elements, and layered narratives. Her collaborations with institutions like The New Yorker, The New York Times, and MoMA have cemented her reputation as an artist who bridges high and low culture.

One of her most famous projects is "The Principles of Uncertainty" (2010), a book and exhibition that explores existential questions through illustrated essays. This project exemplifies her ability to make complex ideas accessible—a skill she later applied to the Shaker Outpost.

The Shaker Outpost: A Fusion of History, Art, and Design

The Shaker Outpost is not just an exhibition—it’s a living, evolving dialogue between 19th-century Shaker heritage and contemporary art. Located in Chatham, New York, a town steeped in Shaker history, the project transforms a historic Shaker building into a modern gallery space where visitors can engage with art, history, and design in an interactive way.

Kalman’s curatorial approach here is radically inclusive—she doesn’t just display art; she invites participation, reflection, and even playfulness. This makes the Shaker Outpost a model for how artists can reimagine heritage sites without erasing their original purpose.

8 Actionable Strategies for Creating an Artist-Curated Space (Like the Shaker Outpost)

If you’re inspired by Kalman’s work and want to curate your own space—whether a gallery, workshop, or public installation—here are eight proven strategies used in the Shaker Outpost and other successful projects.

1. Blend History with Contemporary Art (Without Erasing the Past)

Kalman’s Approach: The Shaker Outpost doesn’t just use Shaker history—it dialogues with it. Original Shaker furniture, tools, and architectural elements are recontextualized alongside modern art, creating a layered narrative.

How to Apply This:

  • Research deeply—understand the original context of the space before making changes.
  • Use original artifacts (if possible) as conversation starters rather than relics.
  • Pair historical objects with contemporary works that challenge or complement the past.

Example: In London’s "The Design Museum’s ‘Designs of the Year’ exhibition, curators often place vintage designs alongside modern innovations, showing evolution rather than replacement.

2. Make the Space Interactive (Not Just Passive Viewing)

Kalman’s Approach: The Shaker Outpost encourages touch, movement, and participation. Visitors might be asked to write on walls, rearrange objects, or engage in guided meditations—all while reflecting on the art and history around them.

How to Apply This:

  • Include interactive elements like touchscreens, QR codes, or hands-on activities.
  • Host workshops or talks where artists and visitors can collaborate.
  • Design "zones"—some for quiet reflection, others for group discussions.

Example: The Museum of Modern Art’s "The Art of the Moving Image" exhibition included interactive stations where visitors could edit short films using MoMA’s digital archives.

3. Use Color and Light as Curatorial Tools

Kalman’s Approach: Kalman is famous for her bold, saturated colors, and the Shaker Outpost leverages this in lighting design and color palettes to guide the viewer’s experience. Warm tones in one room might contrast with cool, muted hues in another, creating emotional shifts.

How to Apply This:

  • Choose a dominant color scheme that reflects the theme of the exhibition.
  • Experiment with lighting—spotlights for key pieces, ambient glow for immersive sections.
  • Use color to create "journeys"—e.g., a calm blue room leading to a vibrant red space.

Example: The Tate Modern’s "Sensation" exhibition (1997) used dramatic lighting and color blocking to heighten the emotional impact of the art.

4. Curate for Emotional Resonance (Not Just Aesthetics)

Kalman’s Approach: The Shaker Outpost isn’t just about looking at art—it’s about feeling it. Kalman’s illustrations and chosen works evoke nostalgia, wonder, and even melancholy, making the experience deeply personal.

How to Apply This:

  • Select art that tells a story—even if it’s fragmented or abstract.
  • Include personal anecdotes or artist statements to humanize the work.
  • Design spaces that evoke specific emotions—e.g., a quiet, textured room for contemplation.

Example: The Whitney Museum’s "The World Goes Pop" exhibition used **pop art’s boldness to contrast with the seriousness of social commentary, creating a layered emotional experience.

5. Incorporate Local Community and Heritage

Kalman’s Approach: Since the Shaker Outpost is in Chatham, New York, Kalman actively engaged with the local Shaker community—interviewing descendants, incorporating regional folklore, and even featuring local artists.

How to Apply This:

  • Partner with local historians, artists, or cultural groups.
  • Host community events—workshops, lectures, or open studios.
  • Use local materials in installations (e.g., wood from the region, handmade textiles).

Example: The High Museum of Art’s "Souls Grown Deep" exhibition featured African American folk art, many pieces created by Southern artists, and included community talks with descendants of the artists.

6. Make the Space Multisensory (Beyond Just Visual Art)

Kalman’s Approach: The Shaker Outpost isn’t just for the eyes—it engages the ears, touch, and even smell. Visitors might hear Shaker hymns played softly in the background or feel textured fabrics that tell a story.

How to Apply This:

  • Add soundscapes—ambient music, nature sounds, or recorded interviews.
  • Include tactile elements—textured walls, movable objects, or scent diffusers.
  • Design for "slow looking"—encourage visitors to stay longer by engaging multiple senses.

Example: The Cooper Hewitt’s "Designing the World" exhibition included interactive sound installations where visitors could compose their own soundtracks based on the exhibits.

7. Use Humor and Playfulness (Without Undermining the Serious)

Kalman’s Approach: Kalman’s work is known for its wit, and the Shaker Outpost balances serious history with playful elements—like whimsical labels, unexpected juxtapositions, and even humor.

How to Apply This:

  • Find the "funny" in the serious—e.g., a humorous label for a solemn artifact.
  • Use unexpected pairings—e.g., a Shaker chair next to a modern sculpture of a dancing figure.
  • Encourage laughter—but ensure it enhances, not distracts from the core message.

Example: The Victoria & Albert Museum’s "Art & Design of the 1960s" exhibition included satirical posters and pop art that commented on the era’s politics, making history both educational and entertaining.

8. Create a "Living" Exhibition (Not a Static One)

Kalman’s Approach: The Shaker Outpost is not a one-time event—it’s an ongoing project with rotating exhibits, new installations, and evolving narratives. Kalman and her team constantly refine the space, adding new works and perspectives.

How to Apply This:

  • Plan for long-term engagement—seasonal changes, artist residencies, or evolving themes.
  • Gather feedback from visitors and adjust the space accordingly.
  • Invite artists to contribute—not just as exhibitors, but as collaborators.

Example: The Museum of Contemporary Art (MCA) Chicago’s "The Art of the Moving Image" is constantly updated, with new film installations and digital experiences added regularly.

Real-World Examples: How Other Artists and Institutions Are Doing It

While the Shaker Outpost is a standout example, many other projects follow similar principles. Here are three notable cases that demonstrate how artist-curated spaces can transform public engagement with art and design.

1. "The Studio Museum in Harlem’s ‘Soul of a Nation’ (2018)

Curator: Thelma Golden Concept: This landmark exhibition explored Black artists’ responses to the Civil Rights Movement, but what made it special was how it was presented.

Key Takeaways:

  • Golden curated the space like a "living room"—intimate, conversational, and emotionally charged.
  • She used color and lighting to create "moods"—e.g., warm tones for hope, cool blues for struggle.
  • The exhibition included interactive elements, like a "sound wall" where visitors could listen to Civil Rights speeches and music.
  • It was not just a museum show—it was a community experience, with workshops, film screenings, and live performances.

Why It Works: Like the Shaker Outpost, "Soul of a Nation" didn’t just display art—it made visitors feel part of the story.

2. "The Broad Museum’s ‘The Broad Experience’ (2019–Present)

Curator: The Broad’s team (with rotating artist collaborators) Concept: The Broad Museum in Los Angeles is famous for its contemporary art collection, but its interactive "Broad Experience" takes curation to the next level.

Key Takeaways:

  • The museum uses "sensory stations"—touchscreens, AR filters, and even a "sound lab" where visitors can compose music inspired by the art.
  • Artists are invited to "play" with the space—e.g., Yayoi Kusama’s "Infinity Mirror Rooms" are not just viewed—they’re experienced.
  • The museum’s website and app extend the experience, with virtual tours and behind-the-scenes content.
  • It’s a "choose-your-own-adventure" gallery, where visitors can navigate based on their interests.

Why It Works: The Broad treats art as an experience, not just an object—much like Kalman’s approach in the Shaker Outpost.

3. "The Design Museum’s ‘Designs of the Year’ (Ongoing)

Curator: The Design Museum’s team (with annual artist collaborators) Concept: This annual exhibition celebrates the best in global design, but what makes it special is how it’s curated.

Key Takeaways:

  • Each year, a different artist or designer is invited to "reimagine" the exhibition.
  • **2022’s curator, Ai Weiwei, turned the space into a political commentary, using design as a tool for activism.
  • The museum uses "immersive storytelling"—videos, interactive displays, and even a "design lab" where visitors can test prototypes.
  • It’s not just about showcasing objects—it’s about sparking conversations about design’s role in society.

Why It Works: The Design Museum proves that curation is a dialogue, not a monologue—similar to how Kalman engages visitors in the Shaker Outpost.

Common Mistakes in Artist-Curated Spaces (And How to Avoid Them)

While artist-curated spaces can be transformative, they also come with pitfalls. Here are five common mistakes and how to steer clear of them.

1. Mistake: Overloading the Space with Too Much

What Happens: Some curators try to include every idea, every artist, every historical artifact—resulting in a cluttered, overwhelming experience.

How to Fix It:

  • Curate with intention—every object should serve a purpose.
  • Use "negative space"—let some areas breathe.
  • Prioritize quality over quantity.

Example: The Guggenheim’s "Frank Lloyd Wright: Architecture as Art" was criticized for being too dense, but MoMA’s "The Art of the Moving Image" succeeded by focusing on a few key works with deep context.

2. Mistake: Ignoring the Original Space’s Character

What Happens: Some artists completely transform a historic space in a way that erases its original purpose, making it feel inauthentic.

How to Fix It:

  • Respect the building’s history—don’t just cover it up.
  • Use original materials where possible.
  • Find a balance between old and new.

Example: **The Shaker Outpost succeeds because it doesn’t hide the Shaker elements—it lets them speak alongside the new art.
