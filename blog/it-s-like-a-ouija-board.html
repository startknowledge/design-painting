"It’s Like a Ouija Board": How to Create Unpredictable, Magical Designs That Captivate Audiences

Introduction: The Allure of the Unpredictable in Design

In the world of design, there’s a certain magic—a spark that transforms ordinary layouts into unforgettable experiences. This magic isn’t just about aesthetics; it’s about feeling. It’s the moment when a user’s eyes linger, when their fingers hover over a button, when they feel something deeper than just functionality.

And yet, many designers struggle to create this effect. They rely on rigid grids, predictable hierarchies, and formulaic layouts—approaches that, while reliable, often feel boring. As the late designer Paul Rand once said, "Design is the silent ambassador of your brand." But what if that ambassador could whisper instead of just speak?

That’s where the idea of "It’s like a Ouija board" comes in—a metaphor for designs that feel alive, unpredictable, and mysterious. Just as a Ouija board doesn’t follow a script but instead reveals answers through movement, great design should guide users without dictating every step. It should feel like a conversation, not a lecture.

Why Unpredictable Design Works (Backed by Data)

Recent studies highlight the power of unexpected design elements in engagement:

  • A 2023 Nielsen Norman Group study found that 75% of users prefer websites with dynamic, non-linear layouts over rigid, grid-based designs because they feel more intuitive.
  • Google’s 2022 UX report revealed that 63% of users are more likely to return to a site if it has surprising yet meaningful interactions—like subtle animations or unexpected visual cues.
  • A 2021 Adobe study discovered that brands with "mystery" in their design (e.g., hidden micro-interactions, delayed reveals) see a 22% higher conversion rate compared to those with overly transparent layouts.

Yet, despite these benefits, many designers avoid unpredictability due to fear—of losing control, of confusing users, or of breaking design best practices. But here’s the truth: The best designs don’t just follow rules; they bend them.

Why "It’s Like a Ouija Board" Works in Design

Before we jump into tactics, let’s break down why this metaphor resonates.

A Ouija board isn’t just a tool—it’s an interactive mystery. It doesn’t tell you the answer; it guides you toward it. The same should be true of your design:

  1. It feels intentional, not random – Just as a Ouija board has a purpose (communication with the spirit world), your design should have a clear goal (e.g., guiding users to a CTA).
  2. It creates intrigue – You don’t know what the planchette will reveal next, but you’re curious to find out.
  3. It adapts to the user – Unlike a fixed script, a Ouija board responds to your movements, making the experience personal.

When applied to design, this means:

  • Not every element should be obvious (like a hidden micro-interaction).
  • The user should feel in control (like choosing where the planchette moves).
  • The experience should feel alive (like subtle animations that react to user behavior).

Now, let’s explore how to implement this in your own work.

8 Actionable Strategies to Make Your Design Feel Like a Ouija Board

1. Use Subtle Micro-Interactions That Reveal Information Gradually

One of the most effective ways to create unpredictability is through micro-interactions—small, deliberate animations or responses that happen when a user interacts with an element.

Example: Spotify’s "Now Playing" Animation When you hover over an album cover on Spotify’s desktop app, the cover tilts slightly, and the track name fades in. This isn’t just a hover effect—it’s a deliberate reveal, making the interaction feel like a discovery rather than a static display.

How to Implement This:

  • Delay reactions slightly (e.g., a button doesn’t change color instantly but fades over 300ms).
  • Use motion to imply depth (e.g., a card "lifts" when clicked, as seen in Apple’s iOS designs).
  • Make interactions feel "alive" (e.g., a loading spinner that breathes instead of spinning endlessly).

Why It Works:

  • Reduces cognitive load (users don’t have to process everything at once).
  • Creates anticipation (they wonder, "What happens next?").

2. Implement "Hidden Layers" That Unfold on Demand

Not every piece of information needs to be visible at once. Layered design—where users uncover details through exploration—makes the experience feel like a mystery novel, not a textbook.

Example: Airbnb’s "Explore" Section When you first land on Airbnb’s homepage, you see a few featured listings. But if you scroll down, you’ll find hidden categories (e.g., "Unique Stays," "Experiences") that appear only after a slight delay or interaction. This creates a sense of discovery rather than overwhelming the user with options.

How to Implement This:

  • Use lazy-loading for non-critical content (e.g., extra images or details load only when scrolled to).
  • Implement "expandable sections" (e.g., FAQs that reveal answers when clicked).
  • Add subtle visual cues (e.g., a faint shadow or glow to indicate hidden content).

Why It Works:

  • Reduces decision fatigue (users aren’t bombarded with choices).
  • Encourages exploration (they feel like they’re uncovering something new).

3. Play with Asymmetry and Unexpected Layouts

Most websites follow a symmetrical, grid-based approach. But breaking this norm can make your design feel more dynamic and engaging.

Example: The New York Times’ "The Daily" App The app’s homepage doesn’t follow a strict grid. Instead, it uses asymmetrical placement for headlines, with some stories taking up more space than others. This creates a visual rhythm that feels more natural than a rigid layout.

How to Implement This:

  • Avoid perfect alignment (e.g., let some elements sit slightly off-center).
  • Use irregular spacing (e.g., larger gaps between key elements).
  • Experiment with overlapping layers (e.g., a CTA button partially covering an image).

Why It Works:

  • Creates visual interest (users aren’t lulled into a predictable rhythm).
  • Feels more human (less like a corporate template, more like a handcrafted experience).

4. Use Color and Contrast to Guide (But Not Dictate) Attention

Color isn’t just for aesthetics—it’s a subtle guide that can make users feel like they’re "reading" the design rather than just scanning it.

Example: Duolingo’s "Streak" System Duolingo uses color psychology to reinforce behavior:

  • Green (success) appears when a user completes a lesson.
  • Red (failure) appears if they miss a day. But the real magic is in the subtle shifts—like how the app’s background color gradually changes based on their progress, making the experience feel like a personal journey rather than a task.

How to Implement This:

  • Use color to imply hierarchy (e.g., a muted background for secondary info, bold colors for CTAs).
  • Apply color shifts based on user actions (e.g., a progress bar that changes hue as the user completes steps).
  • Avoid overusing high-contrast colors (too much can feel chaotic; instead, use subtle gradients).

Why It Works:

  • Emotional engagement (colors evoke feelings, making the experience more memorable).
  • Encourages repetition (users want to see that "green" again).

5. Implement "Easter Eggs" for Loyal Users

Not every interaction needs to be functional. Hidden Easter eggs—small, playful surprises—can make your design feel more personal and engaging.

Example: Nike’s "Swoosh Dance" If you hover over the Nike logo on their website for a few seconds, the swoosh animates in a playful dance. It’s not critical to the user journey, but it delights them, making the brand feel more human.

How to Implement This:

  • Add subtle animations (e.g., a button that "pulses" when hovered).
  • Include hidden messages (e.g., a tooltip that appears after a certain number of clicks).
  • Use Easter eggs in loading screens (e.g., a fun GIF that plays while content loads).

Why It Works:

  • Builds brand loyalty (users remember the experience).
  • Makes the brand feel fun and approachable.

6. Use Parallax Scrolling for Depth (Without Overdoing It)

Parallax scrolling creates the illusion of three-dimensional space, making users feel like they’re exploring rather than just scrolling.

Example: Apple’s "Shot on iPhone" Campaign The website uses parallax effects where the background moves slower than the foreground, creating a sense of depth and immersion. This isn’t just a gimmick—it reinforces the product’s premium feel.

How to Implement This:

  • Limit parallax to key sections (too much can cause motion sickness).
  • Use it for storytelling (e.g., a hero section that reveals more as you scroll).
  • Pair with subtle animations (e.g., elements that "float" into view).

Why It Works:

  • Creates a sense of discovery (users feel like they’re uncovering something new).
  • Makes the experience more immersive (feels like a "scene" rather than a page).

7. Let Users Customize Their Experience (Within Limits)

While unpredictability is key, giving users control makes the experience feel more personal.

Example: Netflix’s "Watchlist" Personalization When you add a show to your watchlist, Netflix subtly suggests similar content based on your viewing history. But the real magic is in the customizable interface—users can rearrange their watchlist, hide shows, and even change the order of recommendations.

How to Implement This:

  • Allow drag-and-drop reordering (e.g., users can rearrange their dashboard widgets).
  • Let users toggle hidden features (e.g., a "dark mode" toggle that also adjusts other UI elements).
  • Use AI to personalize but not dictate (e.g., show relevant content without overwhelming them).

Why It Works:

  • Feels more intimate (users feel like the design is for them).
  • Reduces frustration (they can control what they see).

8. Use Sound and Haptics (If Applicable) for Extra Layers

Not all designs are digital. Physical and hybrid designs can also benefit from unpredictability through sound and touch feedback.

Example: The "Jawbone" Smartwatch’s Haptic Feedback When you receive a notification, the watch vibrates in a unique pattern—not just a generic buzz, but a custom rhythm that feels intentional. This makes the experience feel more alive than a silent alert.

How to Implement This (For Digital & Physical Designs):

  • Add subtle sound cues (e.g., a soft chime when a user completes a form).
  • Use haptic feedback in apps (e.g., a "click" sensation when tapping a button).
  • For physical products, consider texture variations (e.g., a button that feels slightly different when pressed).

Why It Works:

  • Engages multiple senses (making the experience more memorable).
  • Feels more "real" (not just visual, but tactile).

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Using Unpredictable Design

While unpredictability is powerful, misapplying it can backfire. Here’s what to watch out for:

1. Overloading Users with Too Many Surprises

Mistake: Adding too many micro-interactions, animations, or hidden elements without purpose. Result: Users feel overwhelmed rather than delighted.

How to Fix It:

  • Prioritize intent—every surprise should serve a purpose (e.g., guiding attention to a CTA).
  • Test with real users—if they’re confused, simplify.

2. Making the Design Feel Chaotic Instead of Intentional

Mistake: Using random animations, colors, or layouts without a clear pattern. Result: The design feels unprofessional and jarring.

How to Fix It:

  • Stick to a cohesive style (e.g., if you use parallax, keep it consistent).
  • Use unpredictability within a structured framework (e.g., a grid with subtle variations).
