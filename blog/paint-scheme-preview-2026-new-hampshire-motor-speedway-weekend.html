The Evolution of NHMS Paint Schemes: A Decade of Design Innovation

Before diving into the 2026 predictions, it’s essential to understand how race car paint schemes have evolved over the past decade. The New Hampshire Motor Speedway has always been a hotbed for bold, high-contrast designs, but recent years have seen a shift toward storytelling, cultural relevance, and digital integration.

2016-2020: The Era of Minimalism & High-Contrast Blocking

In the mid-2010s, NASCAR teams leaned heavily on high-contrast, block-based designs—think bold primary colors with sharp geometric lines. The 2017 Toyota Camry of Kyle Larson (a black and white checkered scheme) became iconic, symbolizing speed and precision. Meanwhile, Joe Gibbs Racing’s Ford F-150s often featured deep blues and reds, creating a visually striking contrast against the NHMS track’s green and brown surroundings.

Why it worked:

  • Easy to read from a distance (critical for TV broadcasts).
  • Strong brand recognition—teams like Gibbs and Hendrick Motorsports used consistent color palettes across multiple cars.
  • Digital integration—these schemes were optimized for social media and streaming, making them shareable and memorable.

2021-2025: The Rise of Narrative & Cultural Themes

The post-pandemic era brought a shift toward more meaningful designs. Teams began incorporating:

  • Historical references (e.g., Dale Earnhardt Jr.’s 88 car featuring retro NASCAR liveries).
  • Community ties (e.g., New Hampshire-themed schemes celebrating local culture).
  • Sustainability messaging (e.g., eco-friendly paint finishes and recycled material accents).

Notable Examples:

  1. Martin Truex Jr.’s 77 Ford (2023 NHMS) – A deep teal and silver scheme with New Hampshire-inspired motifs, including mountain silhouettes and local landmarks.
  2. William Byron’s 22 Chevrolet (2024 NHMS) – A matte black and white design with subtle red accents, inspired by classic race car aesthetics but with a modern twist.
  3. Ross Chastain’s 31 Ford (2025 NHMS) – A high-gloss blue and gold scheme celebrating NASCAR’s 100th anniversary, with digital holographic elements for a futuristic touch.

Why it resonated:

  • Emotional connection—fans felt more invested when designs told a story.
  • Social media dominance—Instagram-worthy schemes drove engagement and sponsorship deals.
  • Trackside visibility—glossy, reflective finishes made cars stand out even in bright daylight.

2026: The Future of NHMS Paint Schemes – What to Expect

10 Actionable Strategies for Achieving Professional NHMS-Level Paint Schemes

If you’re a painter, designer, or automotive enthusiast looking to replicate or improve upon these high-end race car designs, here are 10 proven strategies to elevate your work.

1. Master the Art of High-Contrast Blocking (Like a NASCAR Pro)

What it is: The foundation of most NHMS schemes—bold, solid colors with sharp edges to ensure maximum visibility on track.

How to do it:

  • Use a color wheel to select complementary colors (e.g., blue and orange, red and green).
  • Test in different lighting—NHMS has bright sunlight and shaded areas; ensure contrast remains visible under all conditions.
  • Avoid gradients—flat, solid blocks are easier to read and apply.

Real-World Example: The 2024 Hendrick Motorsports #24 Chevrolet featured a deep purple and white scheme with thin black stripes—the high contrast made it unmistakable even at high speeds.

Pro Tip:

  • Use masking tape for precise edges—NASCAR teams use specialty automotive tape that won’t peel paint.
  • Test on a scrap piece first—race car paint is thicker and more durable than standard automotive paint.

2. Incorporate Cultural & Local Themes (Like Martin Truex Jr.’s 2023 Scheme)

What it is: Designing with regional pride—NHMS has a strong connection to New Hampshire, and teams are increasingly tying schemes to local culture.

How to do it:

  • Research local symbols—mountains (White Mountains), forests, lakes, and historical landmarks.
  • Work with local artists—some teams commission NH-based designers for exclusive motifs.
  • Use subtle references—silhouettes, textures, or color palettes inspired by New Hampshire’s natural beauty.

Real-World Example: Martin Truex Jr.’s 2023 #77 Ford featured:

  • Deep teal (representing New Hampshire’s lakes and forests).
  • Silver accents (inspired by local manufacturing and industrial heritage).
  • A subtle mountain silhouette on the sidepods.

Pro Tip:

  • Avoid overloading—one strong motif is more effective than multiple competing designs.
  • Check with local tourism boards—some regions have guidelines for cultural representation.

3. Experiment with Matte & Glossy Finishes (The 2025 Byron Trend)

What it is: Mixing textures—some areas glossy, others matte—to create depth and visual interest.

How to do it:

  • Start with a glossy base (easier to clean and maintain).
  • Apply matte accents (e.g., wheel wells, sidepods, or sponsor logos) for contrast.
  • Use a wet-sand finish for a smooth, velvety texture in matte areas.

Real-World Example: William Byron’s 2025 #22 Chevrolet had:

  • Matte black hood and roof (for a modern, aggressive look).
  • High-gloss white and red accents (to stand out against the matte sections).
  • Subtle metallic silver stripes (adding a futuristic touch).

Pro Tip:

  • Test durability—matte finishes show dirt more easily; ensure easy cleaning methods.
  • Use a primer before applying matte paint to prevent peeling.

4. Leverage Digital & AR Integration (The 2026 Tech Trend)

What it is: Using technology to enhance paint schemes—digital overlays, holograms, or color-shifting pigments.

How to do it:

  • Partner with AR developers—some teams are testing augmented reality decals that change based on viewer location.
  • Use electrochromic paint—pigments that shift color with temperature or electrical current.
  • Embed QR codes—linking to team social media, sponsor pages, or fan engagement content.

Real-World Example (Future Prediction): A 2026 Hendrick Motorsports car might feature:

  • A digital "speed stripe" effect that changes color based on real-time lap times.
  • AR decals that display sponsor messages when viewed through a smartphone app.
  • A "ghost car" hologram projected onto the side of the vehicle during pit stops.

Pro Tip:

  • Work with tech partners early—AR and smart paint require specialized suppliers.
  • Keep it simple—too many digital elements can distract from the core design.

5. Use Sustainable & Eco-Friendly Materials (The 2026 Green Shift)

What it is: Reducing environmental impact while maintaining high-performance aesthetics.

How to do it:

  • Switch to water-based paints—less VOCs (volatile organic compounds) than traditional paints.
  • Use recycled fabrics for interiors and decals.
  • Opt for biodegradable decals—some teams are testing plant-based inks.
  • Choose low-reflective finishes to reduce glare and heat absorption.

Real-World Example: The 2025 Toyota Racing #2 Camry featured:

  • A "green tech" scheme with recycled aluminum accents.
  • Water-based paint for lower emissions.
  • Solar-reflective panels on the roof to reduce heat buildup.

Pro Tip:

  • Check with regulators—some tracks have eco-friendly paint requirements.
  • Market the sustainability—fans love supporting green initiatives.

6. Incorporate Retro & Nostalgia Elements (Like the 2024 Earnhardt Jr. Car)

What it is: Blending modern design with classic NASCAR aesthetics—a growing trend in 2026.

How to do it:

  • Revisit old-school color schemes (e.g., 1970s and 1980s block colors).
  • Add vintage sponsor logos (e.g., Budweiser, Pepsi, or Goodyear from past eras).
  • Use retro typography for team names and numbers.

Real-World Example: Dale Earnhardt Jr.’s 2024 #88 Ford featured:

  • A 1990s-inspired color scheme—bright orange and black.
  • Classic NASCAR stripes (similar to the 1990s Richard Childress Racing cars).
  • Retro sponsor decals (e.g., a vintage Goodyear logo).

Pro Tip:

  • Don’t overdo it—a few nostalgic elements are enough; too much retro can look dated.
  • Research historical accuracy—some old designs had specific rules (e.g., minimum stripe width).

7. Play with Unconventional Color Palettes (Like the 2025 Chastain Car)

What it is: Moving beyond primary colors—using unexpected hues to stand out.

How to do it:

  • Try jewel tones (e.g., emerald green, sapphire blue, ruby red).
  • Use pastels (e.g., mint green, lavender, baby blue) for a softer look.
  • Combine metallic and matte (e.g., gold and black, silver and white).

Real-World Example: Ross Chastain’s 2025 #31 Ford featured:

  • A deep navy blue base (uncommon in NASCAR).
  • Gold and white accents (for a luxury feel).
  • A subtle holographic stripe (adding a futuristic touch).

Pro Tip:

  • Test in different lights—some colors look great in daylight but wash out at night.
  • Consider sponsor preferences—some brands have color restrictions.

8. Optimize for Trackside Visibility (The NASCAR Rulebook Secret)

What it is: Designing for maximum visibility—critical for TV broadcasts and fan experience.

How to do it:

  • Follow NASCAR’s color guidelines—minimum contrast ratios for TV and digital viewing.
  • Avoid small, intricate details—big, bold shapes are easier to see.
  • Use reflective materials (e.g., chrome, holographic, or mirror-like finishes) for high-visibility.
  • Test under different conditions—sunlight, shadows, and night racing.

Real-World Example: The 2023 Joe Gibbs Racing #20 Ford had:

  • A bright yellow and black scheme (high contrast for TV).
  • Reflective silver accents (to catch the light in different conditions).
  • Large, readable sponsor logos (to avoid blurring on screens).

Pro Tip:

  • Work with a NASCAR design consultant—they know the best practices.
  • Simulate TV viewing—record test runs to see how the design appears on broadcast.

9. Add Personal Touches (Like Drivers’ Signature Styles)

What it is: Incorporating the driver’s personality into the design—a fan-favorite trend.

How to do it:

  • Include driver logos or nicknames (e.g., "The King" for Dale Earnhardt Jr.).
  • Use inside jokes or personal symbols (e.g., a favorite animal, sports team, or hobby).
  • Let the driver approve final designs
