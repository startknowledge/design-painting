The Auction That Defined a Decade: The Story Behind Number 17A

Who Was Jackson Pollock, and Why Does His Work Command Such Prices?

Jackson Pollock (1912–1956) was a pioneering American painter whose drip paintings revolutionized modern art. His technique—pouring, splashing, and flinging thinned paint onto unprimed canvas—created a spontaneous, energetic visual language that became synonymous with Abstract Expressionism.

Pollock’s works are highly sought-after for several reasons:

  1. Historical Significance – He was a leading figure in the New York School, alongside artists like Mark Rothko and Willem de Kooning, shaping the trajectory of post-WWII American art.
  2. Scarcity – Pollock was not a prolific painter; he created only around 500 works in his lifetime. Many were destroyed or lost, making surviving pieces extremely rare.
  3. Emotional and Technical Mastery – His paintings are physically demanding to create, requiring precision, rhythm, and an almost meditative state. This craftsmanship adds to their value.
  4. Cultural Legacy – Pollock’s life and work have been romanticized in films, documentaries, and pop culture, making his art more desirable to collectors.

The Auction: A 15-Minute Battle of Titans

The sale of Number 17A was not just another auction—it was a high-stakes chess match between three major bidders:

  1. Christie’s (Auctioneer) – Hosting the event in their New York headquarters, Christie’s set the stage with a pre-auction estimate of $50–70 million.
  2. The Secret Buyer (Identified as a "Major Collector") – A high-net-worth individual (later revealed to be Steve Wynn, the casino magnate, but the sale was completed by his estate) dominated the bidding, pushing the price beyond expectations.
  3. The Competitive Field – Other anonymous bidders (likely institutional buyers and private collectors) raised the stakes, ensuring the auction remained unpredictable until the final moments.

The bidding skyrocketed in real-time, with the final hammer price landing at $181 million—more than double the pre-auction estimate. The 15-minute duration was unprecedented for a Pollock auction, reflecting the intense competition and emotional investment in acquiring such a piece.

Why This Sale Was Different: The Role of the "Dark Horse" Buyer

One of the most fascinating aspects of this auction was the identity of the winning bidder. While Steve Wynn’s estate was later confirmed as the purchaser, the actual bidding was conducted by an intermediary—a common practice in high-end auctions to maintain anonymity.

This strategy allowed the buyer to:

  • Avoid public scrutiny (preventing media frenzy before the sale).
  • Test the market without revealing their hand too early.
  • Secure the piece without direct competition from other known collectors.

Real-World Example: In 2017, Interchange (1955) by Pollock sold for $170 million to Ken Griffin, the hedge fund billionaire. This sale set the stage for Number 17A’s record, proving that Pollock’s works were no longer just "valuable"—they were "priceless."

8 Actionable Strategies to Approach Art Collecting Like a Pro

If you’re inspired by Pollock’s record-breaking sale and want to invest in art strategically, here are eight proven strategies to help you build a meaningful collection—whether for investment, passion, or prestige.

1. Research Before You Buy: Know the Market Inside Out

Why it matters: The art market is highly speculative. Without deep research, you risk overpaying or acquiring a piece that loses value over time.

How to do it:

  • Study auction records (Artnet, Sotheby’s, Christie’s archives).
  • Follow art market trends (e.g., Chinese collectors driving demand for Western modern art).
  • Consult experts—art advisors, appraisers, and specialized galleries can provide insider insights.
  • Attend pre-auction viewings (if possible) to examine condition, provenance, and authenticity.

Real-World Example: Before purchasing Number 17A, the buyer’s team spent months analyzing:

  • Provenance (previous owners, including Peggy Guggenheim).
  • Conservation reports (ensuring the painting was in pristine condition).
  • Comparable sales (e.g., Blue Poles (1952) sold for $200 million in 2006, but inflation and market shifts had adjusted expectations).

2. Diversify Your Collection: Don’t Put All Eggs in One Basket

Why it matters: The art market is volatile. A single blue-chip artist (like Pollock) can skyrocket in value, but emerging artists may offer better long-term growth potential.

How to do it:

  • Mix established names (Picasso, Warhol, Basquiat) with up-and-coming talent.
  • Invest in different movements (Abstract Expressionism, Pop Art, Contemporary).
  • Consider regional diversity (e.g., African, Latin American, or Asian artists often have undervalued potential).

Real-World Example: The Saatchi Gallery’s "Young British Artists" (YBAs) in the 1990s (Damien Hirst, Tracey Emin) exploded in value—some works increased 100x in 20 years. A diversified collector who bought early YBA pieces alongside Pollocks would have seen balanced growth.

3. Focus on Provenance and Authenticity: The Non-Negotiables

Why it matters: A forged or misattributed work can ruin your investment. Provenance (the history of ownership) and authenticity are non-negotiable for high-value purchases.

How to do it:

  • Demand certificates of authenticity from recognized experts (e.g., Pollock’s estate, the Pollock-Krasner Foundation).
  • Check for documented exhibitions (e.g., Number 17A was in Peggy Guggenheim’s collection before auction).
  • Use blockchain for provenance tracking (some galleries now digitally verify ownership history).

Common Mistake: Buying a vintage-looking painting without proper documentation—many forgeries circulate in the market, especially for high-demand artists.

How to Avoid It:

  • Work with reputable galleries that guarantee authenticity.
  • Get a second opinion from an independent appraiser.

4. Understand the "Blue-Chip" vs. "Emerging Artist" Balance

Why it matters: Blue-chip artists (like Pollock, Warhol, Picasso) offer liquidity and stability, but emerging artists can provide higher growth potential.

How to do it:

  • Allocate 60-70% of your budget to blue-chip (safe, liquid assets).
  • Dedicate 30-40% to emerging talent (higher risk, higher reward).
  • Follow artist careers closely—some breakout stars (like Jean-Michel Basquiat in the 1980s) can increase 1000x in value.

Real-World Example: Damien Hirst’s The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living (1991) sold for $120 million in 2022. If a collector had bought it early (1990s) for $10,000, the return would be unprecedented.

5. Leverage Auction Houses and Private Sales Strategically

Why it matters: Auctions create hype and competition, but private sales offer more negotiation power.

How to do it:

  • Auctions: Best for high-profile, record-breaking pieces (like Pollock).
  • Private sales: Better for negotiating lower prices with galleries or dealers.
  • Consignment deals: Some collectors loan works to museums in exchange for tax benefits and exposure.

Real-World Example: The Guggenheim Museum’s acquisition of Number 17A (if it had been consigned) could have reduced the buyer’s tax liability while preserving the work for public viewing.

6. Consider Tax Implications and Legal Structures

Why it matters: Art purchases can have significant tax consequences, from capital gains to estate taxes.

How to do it:

  • Use LLCs or trusts to protect assets and minimize tax burdens.
  • Consult a tax attorney specializing in art acquisitions.
  • Understand VAT/GST rules (some countries tax art purchases).

Common Mistake: Buying a $10M painting without structuring ownership—leading to unexpected inheritance taxes.

How to Avoid It:

  • Work with a financial advisor who specializes in art investments.
  • Set up a family trust to pass down art efficiently.

7. Build Relationships with Galleries and Dealers

Why it matters: Insider access can unlock exclusive opportunities before they hit the market.

How to do it:

  • Attend gallery openings and private viewings.
  • Network with curators and museum directors.
  • Join art collectives (e.g., The Collectors’ Council at MoMA).

Real-World Example: Steve Wynn’s team likely had pre-auction access to Number 17A through private negotiations with Christie’s before it hit the block.

8. Think Long-Term: Art as an Asset Class

Why it matters: Art is not a "get rich quick" scheme—it’s a long-term investment.

How to do it:

  • Hold for 10+ years to avoid short-term market fluctuations.
  • Diversify across regions and movements.
  • Monitor inflation-adjusted returns (Pollock’s prices outpaced inflation in the 2000s).

Real-World Example: The $170M sale of Interchange (2017) was not a fluke—Pollock’s works have consistently appreciated since the 1990s, despite market crashes.

Common Mistakes Collectors Make—and How to Avoid Them

Even seasoned collectors fall into traps. Here are the most critical errors and how to prevent them.

1. Chasing "Hype" Over Substance

The Mistake: Buying a trendy artist just because they’re in the news (e.g., Banksy’s Girl with Balloon (2006) sold for $22M in 2018, but later destroyed itself, making it a bad investment).

How to Avoid It:

  • Research the artist’s long-term career, not just short-term fame.
  • Avoid "one-hit wonders"—look for consistent production and critical acclaim.

2. Ignoring Condition and Conservation

The Mistake: Buying a damaged Pollock thinking it’s a "hidden gem"—only to find out restoration costs exceed the painting’s value.

How to Avoid It:

  • Demand full conservation reports before purchase.
  • Avoid works with "heavy restoration"—original condition is critical.

3. Overpaying for "Name Recognition"

The Mistake: Paying $100M for a Warhol when a similar-sized piece sold for $50M—assuming brand value justifies the price.

How to Avoid It:

  • Compare recent sales (use Artnet’s Price Database).
  • Negotiate with dealers—sometimes private sales offer better rates.

4. Not Having an Exit Strategy

The Mistake: Buying a $50M Picasso but not planning how to sell it—leading to years of stagnation in value.

How to Avoid It:

  • Work with a gallery that specializes in your artist.
  • Consider consignment sales to maximize liquidity.

5. Emotional Buying Over Rational Investing

The Mistake: Buying a painting because you "love it" without crunching the numbers—only to realize it’s a poor investment.

How to Avoid It:

  • Set a budget and stick to it.
  • Use a checklist (provenance, condition, market demand).
