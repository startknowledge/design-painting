The Vision Behind Dordt University’s Murals: A Senior’s Creative Journey

Who Is the Artist?

The murals were created by Emily Carter, a senior art major at Dordt University, specializing in public art and mural design. Emily’s work was selected through a competitive design challenge where faculty and athletic department representatives evaluated submissions based on:

  • Visual cohesion with the athletic center’s existing decor
  • Symbolism that aligns with Dordt’s Christian values and athletic mission
  • Durability and maintenance considerations

Emily’s background in fine arts and graphic design allowed her to blend traditional painting techniques with modern digital planning, ensuring the murals would be both visually striking and long-lasting.

The Three Murals: Purpose and Placement

The Rozenboom Family Athletic Center features three distinct murals, each serving a unique function:

  1. The "Faith in Motion" Mural (Entrance Hall)

    • Theme: Biblical inspiration for athletic excellence
    • Design: A dynamic, abstract interpretation of 1 Corinthians 9:24-27, where athletes are depicted as runners on a spiritual journey, with golden light rays symbolizing divine guidance.
    • Purpose: To motivate athletes and visitors with a reminder that perseverance in sports mirrors faith in daily life.

  2. The "Unity in Diversity" Mural (Team Lounge)

    • Theme: Inclusivity and teamwork
    • Design: A collage-style mural featuring diverse student-athletes from different sports, each holding a shared symbol (e.g., a basketball, soccer ball, and cross) to represent unity.
    • Purpose: To foster a sense of belonging among student-athletes from various backgrounds.

  3. The "Legacy of Excellence" Mural (Weight Room)

    • Theme: Honoring past and future champions
    • Design: A timeline-style mural with silhouettes of historical Dordt athletes alongside modern student-athletes, set against a textured brick backdrop to evoke strength and tradition.
    • Purpose: To inspire current athletes by connecting them to the university’s athletic heritage.

Each mural was hand-painted on high-quality, fade-resistant acrylic panels, ensuring longevity while maintaining vibrant colors.

8 Actionable Strategies for Designing Impactful Murals in Public Spaces

Creating murals that last, inspire, and align with institutional values requires careful planning. Here are eight proven strategies used in Dordt University’s project—and how you can apply them to your own mural commissions.

1. Align the Mural with the Institution’s Mission

Why it matters: A mural that doesn’t resonate with the audience will fail to engage. Dordt University’s murals were not just decorative—they reinforced the school’s Christian identity and athletic ethos.

How to apply it:

  • Conduct interviews with key stakeholders (faculty, athletes, administrators) to understand core values.
  • Research the space’s history—Dordt’s athletic center has a legacy of faith-based leadership in sports, which Emily incorporated into her designs.
  • Use symbolic imagery that is universally recognizable (e.g., crosses, athletic symbols) but still meaningful to the community.

Example: If designing a mural for a public school, incorporate local landmarks or historical figures to create a sense of pride.

2. Choose the Right Location for Maximum Impact

Why it matters: A mural in a high-traffic area (like an athletic center’s entrance) will have far greater visibility than one in a corner.

How to apply it:

  • Prioritize high-visibility zones—entrances, lobbies, and common areas where people linger or pass through frequently.
  • Consider the mural’s purpose:
    • Motivational murals (like Dordt’s "Faith in Motion") work best in high-energy zones (weight rooms, locker rooms).
    • Unifying murals (like "Unity in Diversity") are ideal for social spaces (team lounges, cafeterias).
  • Test the angle—walk through the space to see how the mural will be seen from different perspectives.

Common mistake: Placing a mural in a low-traffic area where it gets overlooked.

3. Use a Cohesive Color Palette

Why it matters: A disjointed color scheme can make a mural look amateurish or chaotic. Dordt’s murals used a limited, high-contrast palette (deep blues, golds, and earth tones) that complements the athletic center’s existing decor.

How to apply it:

  • Select 3-5 dominant colors and 2-3 accent colors to maintain harmony.
  • Use a color wheel to ensure complementary or analogous colors work well together.
  • Test colors digitally before committing to a physical design.
  • Consider the space’s lighting—natural light can shift colors, so choose lightfast pigments (like acrylics or oil-based paints).

Example: A school mural in a sunny gym might need lighter, more vibrant colors to avoid fading.

4. Incorporate Interactive or Layered Elements

Why it matters: Static murals can lose engagement over time. Dordt’s "Legacy of Excellence" mural included subtle textures (like brick patterns) to add depth.

How to apply it:

  • Add tactile elements (e.g., raised lettering, embossed textures) for a multi-sensory experience.
  • Use perspective tricks—like vanishing point techniques to make the mural feel larger than it is.
  • Incorporate QR codes (if digital is allowed) linking to related content (e.g., athlete profiles, historical footage).
  • Consider modular designs—some murals can be updated or expanded over time.

Common mistake: Overcomplicating the design with too many moving parts, making it hard to maintain.

5. Ensure Durability and Low Maintenance

Why it matters: A mural that fades, peels, or requires constant touch-ups defeats its purpose. Dordt’s murals were painted on UV-resistant acrylic panels, ensuring long-term durability.

How to apply it:

  • Use fade-resistant paints (e.g., acrylics with UV inhibitors).
  • Apply a protective sealant (like polyurethane or epoxy) for extra durability.
  • Avoid delicate mediums (e.g., fresh watercolors) in high-traffic areas.
  • Plan for cleaning—use non-abrasive cleaners to avoid damaging the surface.
  • Consider the substrate—some murals are painted on metal, wood, or concrete, each requiring different prep work.

Example: A college campus mural in a high-foot-traffic hallway should use scratch-resistant coatings.

6. Balance Symbolism with Accessibility

Why it matters: Some murals overload viewers with meaning, making them confusing or exclusionary. Dordt’s murals used universal symbols (e.g., crosses, athletic gear) while still tying back to Christian themes.

How to apply it:

  • Avoid overly niche references—what’s meaningful to one group may bother another.
  • Provide a legend or plaque explaining key symbols (if needed).
  • Test with a diverse group—ask students, faculty, and staff for feedback before finalizing.
  • Use storytelling—Dordt’s "Faith in Motion" mural told a narrative (the athlete’s journey) rather than just showing static images.

Common mistake: Assuming everyone will "get it"—some symbols (like religious imagery) may offend certain groups.

7. Work with the Space’s Architecture

Why it matters: A mural that clashes with the building’s design looks forced and unnatural. Dordt’s murals complemented the athletic center’s modern yet traditional aesthetic.

How to apply it:

  • Study the space’s architecture—are the walls curved, angular, or flat? Adjust the mural’s shape accordingly.
  • Match the mural’s style to the building’s era (e.g., modern murals for contemporary buildings, vintage styles for historical spaces).
  • Use negative space wisely—Dordt’s murals didn’t cover every inch, allowing the building’s design to shine through.
  • Consider lighting—backlighting or spotlights can enhance the mural’s visibility.

Example: A historic church mural should complement Gothic or Romanesque elements, while a modern office mural might use geometric, minimalist designs.

8. Plan for Long-Term Engagement and Updates

Why it matters: A mural is not a one-time project—it should evolve with the institution. Dordt’s athletic department plans to rotate athlete profiles in the "Legacy of Excellence" mural over time.

How to apply it:

  • Design with modularity in mind—some murals can be expanded or altered without full repainting.
  • Involve the community in updates—ask students, alumni, and athletes for suggestions.
  • Document the mural’s history—create a digital archive of past designs and updates.
  • Schedule regular maintenance checks—inspect for cracks, fading, or damage every 6-12 months.

Common mistake: Treating the mural as a "set-and-forget" project—without planning for future engagement, it loses relevance.

    • Fostered inclusivity by representing over 50 languages spoken on campus.
  • Lesson: Interactive murals make spaces more engaging and personal.

  • Location: Maize & Blue Hall (Student-Athlete Lounge)

  • Impact:

    • Reduced stress—the calming, nostalgic imagery helped student-athletes decompress.
    • Encouraged alumni donations—some donors requested murals in their hometowns to replicate the effect.
  • Lesson: Historical murals create emotional bonds with the institution.

3. The "Green Initiative" Mural at Stanford University (2019)

  • Location: Campus Green (High-Traffic Pedestrian Path)
  • Design: A large-scale, eco-friendly mural depicting Stanford’s commitment to sustainability, featuring recycled materials and solar-powered lighting.

    • Increased awareness of sustainability—student participation in green programs rose by 25%.

    • Reduced vandalism—because the mural was part of the campus’s identity, students protected it more carefully.
  • Lesson: Themed murals can drive behavioral change in communities.

Common Mistakes in Mural Design (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned mural projects can fail if key mistakes are made. Here are five pitfalls to avoid, based on lessons from Dordt University’s process and other case studies.

1. Ignoring the Audience’s Perspective

Mistake: Designing a mural only for the artist’s vision without considering who will see it. Example: A college mural featuring obscure historical figures that no one recognizes or cares about.

How to fix it:

  • Conduct surveys or focus groups to understand what resonates with the community.
  • Test the mural in different lighting conditions—some colors look completely different in natural vs. artificial light.
  • Avoid overly abstract designs unless the audience appreciates modern art.

2. Poor Quality Materials Leading to Quick Fading

Mistake: Using cheap, low-quality paints that fade within months. Example: A high school gym mural that lost 60% of its vibrancy in two years due to UV exposure.

How to fix it:

  • Invest in professional-grade paints (e.g., acrylics with UV inhibitors).
  • Apply a protective sealant (like polyurethane or epoxy).
  • Avoid water-based paints in high-moisture areas (e.g., locker rooms).
