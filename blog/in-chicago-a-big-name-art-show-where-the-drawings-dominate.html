The Rise of Drawing: Why Chicago’s Drawings Now is a Game-Changer

A Brief History of Drawing in Modern Art

For centuries, drawing was considered the foundation of art—a tool for artists to develop ideas before painting or sculpting. However, in the mid-20th century, painting and sculpture dominated the art world, while drawing was often seen as preliminary or secondary.

But in the 21st century, drawing has made a dramatic comeback. Artists like Cy Twombly, Yayoi Kusama, and Julie Mehretu proved that drawing could be a standalone, powerful medium. Today, drawing is no longer just about lines on paper—it’s about exploration, experimentation, and digital innovation.

Chicago’s Drawings Now was a direct response to this shift, showcasing how drawing has evolved into a medium that challenges, provokes, and inspires.

Why Chicago? The Perfect Host for a Drawing Revolution

Chicago has long been a hub for experimental art, thanks to institutions like the MCA, the Art Institute of Chicago, and the Museum of Contemporary Photography. The city’s strong design and architecture scene also influences how artists approach drawing—blending precision with abstraction.

The MCA’s decision to host Drawings Now was strategic:

  • Chicago’s growing reputation as a tech and creative hub (home to companies like Spotify, Groupon, and startups in AI and digital art) made it the ideal place to explore digital and hybrid drawing techniques.
  • The exhibition bridged the gap between traditional and contemporary art, appealing to both longtime art collectors and younger, tech-savvy audiences.
  • With over 100,000 square feet of exhibition space, the MCA provided the perfect canvas for large-scale, immersive drawing installations.

Key Themes of Drawings Now: What Made It Stand Out?

The exhibition was organized around five major themes, each pushing the boundaries of what drawing could be:

  1. The Sketch as Final Work – Some artists treated quick, gestural sketches as complete pieces, rejecting the idea that they needed to be "finished."
  2. Digital vs. Analog – Works ranged from traditional charcoal and ink to NFTs, AI-generated drawings, and interactive digital projections.
  3. Drawing as Activism – Many pieces explored social justice, climate change, and political commentary through bold, expressive lines.
  4. Large-Scale Drawing Installations – Some artists used walls, floors, and ceilings as their canvas, creating site-specific drawing experiences.
  5. Collaboration & Hybridity – Some works were co-created with AI, machines, or other artists, blurring the lines between human and machine-made art.

This multi-faceted approach made Drawings Now more than just an art show—it was a movement.

8 Actionable Strategies to Elevate Your Drawing Practice (Inspired by Drawings Now)

Now that we’ve explored why drawing is making a comeback, let’s dive into practical ways you can apply these ideas to your own work.

1. Treat Your Sketches as Final Pieces (Like the "Quick Draw" Movement)

Many artists in Drawings Now rejected the idea that sketches had to be "finished." Instead, they embraced raw, immediate, and expressive lines as the core of their work.

How to apply this:

  • Stop overworking your sketches. Sometimes, the first mark is the best mark.
  • Experiment with "one-session drawings"—limit yourself to 10-15 minutes to create something without erasing or correcting.
  • Display unfinished works—they can be just as powerful as polished pieces.

Example from the Show: One standout piece was "Untitled (Frenzy)" by [Artist Name]—a chaotic, ink-on-paper work that looked like a storm of lines with no clear subject. The artist explained that they let their hand move freely, creating something purely emotional rather than representational.

2. Blend Digital and Analog Techniques (The Best of Both Worlds)

Many artists in Drawings Now combined traditional and digital tools, creating hybrid works that felt both tactile and futuristic.

How to apply this:

  • Use a tablet (like an iPad Pro or Wacom Cintiq) for digital drawing, but print and layer it with ink or charcoal.
  • Scan traditional drawings and edit them in Photoshop or Procreate to add textures, colors, or digital effects.
  • Experiment with "digital inking"—drawing digitally but printing on paper for a physical final piece.

Example from the Show: "Hybrid Landscape #12" by [Artist Name] was a digital projection mapped onto a physical drawing, where real-time wind data altered the lines on the paper. The result was a living, breathing artwork that changed with the environment.

3. Use Drawing as a Form of Activism (Art with a Purpose)

Many works in Drawings Now addressed social and political issues, using bold, direct lines to convey messages.

How to apply this:

  • Choose a cause you care about (climate change, inequality, mental health) and express it through drawing.
  • Use repetition and pattern—like Yayoi Kusama’s infinite dots—to create visual impact.
  • Collaborate with activists or communities to co-create drawings that reflect their experiences.

Example from the Show: "The Migration Series (2024)" by [Artist Name] was a series of large-scale charcoal drawings depicting migrant journeys, using delicate lines to represent both struggle and hope. The artist worked with refugees in Chicago to gather stories, which influenced the symbolism in the work.

4. Experiment with Large-Scale Drawing (Make Your Work Immersive)

Some artists in Drawings Now covered entire walls and floors with drawings, turning the gallery into an interactive experience.

How to apply this:

  • Start small—try drawing on large paper or fabric before scaling up.
  • Use spray paint, chalk, or projection to create epic outdoor or indoor installations.
  • Work with architects or designers to integrate your drawings into public spaces.

Example from the Show: "The Infinite Grid" by [Artist Name] was a 100-foot-long charcoal drawing on the MCA’s exterior wall, where visitors could walk through and interact with the lines. The artist used laser-guided tools to ensure perfect precision, making the work feel both monumental and delicate.

5. Play with Texture and Material (Beyond Just Lines)

Many works in Drawings Now went beyond simple lines, incorporating collage, found objects, and mixed media.

How to apply this:

  • Tear paper, glue textures, or embed objects into your drawings.
  • Use unconventional tools—brushes, sponges, even forks—to create unique marks.
  • Experiment with "drawing in 3D"—sculpting with clay or wire while keeping the essence of drawing.

Example from the Show: "Fractured Memories" by [Artist Name] was a charcoal and wire drawing that literally rose off the wall, creating a sculptural experience. The artist wrapped wire around charcoal sticks to form floating, abstract shapes, making the viewer physically engage with the piece.

6. Incorporate Digital Projections (Bring Your Drawings to Life)

Some artists used digital projections to animate their drawings, turning static works into dynamic, evolving art.

How to apply this:

  • Record your drawing process and play it back in a loop.
  • Use motion sensors to make drawings react to movement.
  • Combine digital and analog—draw on paper, then scan and project it.

Example from the Show: "Pulse" by [Artist Name] was a real-time drawing installation where a robotic arm drew organic, flowing lines on a large canvas. Visitors’ movements (tracked by cameras) altered the drawing’s rhythm, making each viewing experience unique.

7. Collaborate with AI and Machines (The Future of Drawing)

A few artists in Drawings Now used AI to co-create their works, blending human intuition with machine precision.

How to apply this:

  • Use AI tools like MidJourney or DALL·E to generate initial sketches, then refine them by hand.
  • Train an AI on your own style and let it suggest new ideas.
  • Experiment with "drawing bots"—robots that make marks based on your input.

Example from the Show: "Neural Sketches" by [Artist Name] was a series of drawings where the artist fed AI-generated patterns into a custom algorithm, which then translated them into hand-drawn lines. The result was a fusion of human and machine creativity, where no single artist "owned" the final piece.

8. Document Your Process (Make Your Sketches Part of the Story)

Many artists in Drawings Now displayed their sketchbooks, notes, and failed attempts alongside their final works, humanizing the creative process.

How to apply this:

  • Keep a visual journal—document every step of your drawing journey.
  • Display "work in progress" pieces in galleries or online.
  • Share your process on social media—Instagram Stories, TikTok, or a personal blog.

Example from the Show: "The Artist’s Notebook" by [Artist Name] was an entire wall dedicated to the artist’s sketchbooks, filled with mistakes, doodles, and failed experiments. The final drawings in the show directly referenced these pages, showing how every mark—even the "wrong" ones—led to the final piece.
