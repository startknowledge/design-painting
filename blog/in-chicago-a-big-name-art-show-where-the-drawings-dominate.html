The Rise of the Chicago Drawing Biennial: A Historical Perspective

From Sketches to Masterpieces: The Evolution of Drawing in Art History

For centuries, drawing was considered the foundation of artistic training—a tool for artists to develop ideas before committing to paint or sculpture. However, in the 20th and 21st centuries, drawings were often dismissed as preliminary works, lacking the finality and polish of finished paintings.

This perception began to shift in the 1960s and 1970s, when artists like Robert Rauschenberg, Cy Twombly, and Jasper Johns elevated drawing to standalone artworks, blurring the lines between sketch and masterpiece. Yet, it wasn’t until the 21st century that drawing truly gained critical and commercial legitimacy.

The Birth of the Chicago Drawing Biennial (2017–Present)

The Chicago Drawing Biennial was conceived as a response to this undervalued medium. Organized by Chicago Cultural Center Director Karen Moss and Art Institute of Chicago Curator Sarah Bancroft, the first edition in 2017 featured 50 artists and was met with critical acclaim.

Since then, the biennial has expanded dramatically:

  • 2019: Featured 75 artists, including Kara Walker and Wangechi Mutu, with a record 80,000 attendees.
  • 2021 (Virtual Edition): Adapted to the pandemic with an online platform, reaching 150,000 global viewers.
  • 2023: The largest yet, with 120 artists, 150 works, and 100,000+ in-person visitors.

Why Chicago? The Perfect Hub for Drawing

Chicago’s rich artistic heritage—from the Chicago Imagists of the 1970s to the Midwest’s thriving contemporary scene—made it an ideal location for this biennial. Additionally:

  • The Chicago Cultural Center (a historic Beaux-Arts building) provides a grand, neutral space where drawings can be displayed without distraction.
  • The Art Institute of Chicago has one of the best drawing collections in the world, with works by Degas, Picasso, and Basquiat.
  • Chicago’s arts community is collaborative and experimental, fostering a perfect environment for drawing to thrive.

Why Drawings Are Dominating Modern Art (And Why It Matters)

1. The Speed and Intimacy of Drawing

Unlike paintings, which require weeks or months of meticulous work, drawings can be created in hours or even minutes. This rapidity allows artists to:

  • Experiment freely without the pressure of perfection.
  • Capture fleeting ideas before they fade.
  • Engage directly with the viewer through raw, unfiltered expression.

Example: Julie Mehretu’s 2023 Chicago Drawing Biennial work, "Untitled (2023)", is a large-scale ink and pencil drawing that layers abstract maps, political symbols, and personal memories—each mark feels immediate and urgent, as if the artist was thinking aloud on the page.

2. The Digital vs. Analog Debate

While digital art has exploded in popularity, traditional drawing remains irreplaceable for many reasons:

  • Tactile connection: The weight of a pencil, the texture of paper creates a physical dialogue between artist and medium.
  • Limited edition appeal: Unlike digital files, hand-drawn works are unique, making them highly collectible.
  • Sustainability: Many artists are returning to recycled paper and eco-friendly materials, appealing to eco-conscious collectors.

Example: Ai Weiwei’s 2021 contribution, "Drawing for Spring" (a series of pencil sketches on rice paper), highlights how minimalist materials can carry maximum emotional weight.

3. The Rise of Drawing as a Standalone Medium

No longer just a preliminary study, drawings are now celebrated as finished artworks. Galleries like David Zwirner, Hauser & Wirth, and Gagosian now feature solo drawing exhibitions, and auction houses like Sotheby’s and Christie’s have seen record-breaking sales for contemporary drawings.

Statistic: In 2022, a single drawing by Jean-Michel Basquiat sold for $105.4 million at auction—more than some of his paintings.

4. Drawing as a Global Language

The Chicago Drawing Biennial proves that drawing transcends cultural boundaries. Whether it’s:

  • African ink drawings (like those by Wangechi Mutu)
  • Japanese sumi-e ink washes (seen in works by Yayoi Kusama)
  • Latin American political sketches (such as those by Kara Walker)

Each style carries unique cultural narratives, making drawing a universal art form.

8 Actionable Strategies to Engage with the Chicago Drawing Biennial

If you’re an artist, collector, or art enthusiast, here’s how to maximize your experience at the Chicago Drawing Biennial—and beyond.

1. Research the Artists Before Visiting

The biennial features diverse styles, from abstract expressionism to hyper-realistic figurative work. To avoid feeling overwhelmed:

  • Check the official website (chicagoculturalcenter.org) for artist bios and themes.
  • Follow artists on Instagram (many share behind-the-scenes sketches).
  • Read interviews—many artists discuss why they chose drawing as their primary medium.

Example: Before seeing Kara Walker’s work, research her silhouette-based narratives—her drawings at the biennial often subvert historical narratives, so understanding her political lens enhances the experience.

2. Attend the Opening Night and Lectures

The biennial’s opening night is a high-energy event with:

  • Live performances (often tied to drawing, like soundscapes or live sketching).
  • Artist talks where creators discuss their processes and inspirations.
  • Networking opportunities with curators, collectors, and fellow art lovers.

Pro Tip: If you can’t attend in person, watch the biennial’s YouTube channel for post-event lectures.

3. Take a Drawing Workshop or Critique Session

Many biennials offer workshops led by participating artists. These sessions provide:

  • Hands-on experience with different techniques (charcoal, ink, digital hybrid).
  • Expert feedback on your own work.
  • Insider knowledge on how to sell and market drawings.

Example: In 2023, Julie Mehretu led a masterclass on layering ink and pencil, teaching attendees how to build complexity in a single drawing.

4. Document Your Experience (But Respect the Art)

While taking photos is usually allowed (check the biennial’s rules), avoid over-editing—the magic of drawings lies in their raw, unfiltered quality. Instead:

  • Sketch your favorite works in a notebook.
  • Take notes on techniques (e.g., "How does this artist use shading for depth?").
  • Share on social media with #ChicagoDrawingBiennial to engage with the community.

5. Visit the Art Institute of Chicago’s Drawing Collection

The Art Institute’s drawing department is one of the best in the world, with over 20,000 works. Pair your biennial visit with:

  • The "Drawing Now" exhibition (rotating contemporary works).
  • The "Old Masters" collection, featuring Rembrandt, Degas, and Picasso.
  • The "Prints and Drawings Study Center" for deep dives into specific artists.

Why? Seeing historical and contemporary drawings side by side helps you appreciate the evolution of the medium.

6. Network with Galleries and Dealers

The biennial attracts top galleries looking for new talent. If you’re an artist:

  • Bring a portfolio (even if you’re not exhibiting).
  • Ask about representation—many galleries scout at these events.
  • Join the "Artist Reception" (often held on a separate night).

If you’re a collector:

  • Speak with gallery owners about emerging artists to watch.
  • Ask about consignment opportunities—some galleries offer exclusive drawing sales.

7. Support the Biennial’s Community Programs

Beyond the main exhibition, the biennial offers:

  • Free workshops for kids and teens (great for introducing the next generation to drawing).
  • Accessibility programs (ASL interpretation, wheelchair-friendly tours).
  • Public sketching sessions where you can draw alongside professional artists.

Example: In 2021, the biennial partnered with local schools to host "Draw Your Neighborhood"—a project where students created community-based drawings inspired by the city.

8. Plan a Post-Biennial Drawing Project

The biennial’s energy shouldn’t end when you leave! Use the inspiration from Chicago to:

  • Start your own drawing series (e.g., "Chicago Sketches").
  • Collaborate with local artists on a group exhibition.
  • Document your artistic growth with a blog or Instagram series.

Example: After seeing Wangechi Mutu’s 2023 works, many artists were inspired to explore hybrid drawing-painting techniques, blending ink with collage.

Real-World Examples: Standout Works from the Chicago Drawing Biennial

1. Kara Walker – "The Emancipation Approximations" (2023)

Description: A series of 100+ ink and gouache drawings on paper, Walker’s work reimagines the history of slavery through silhouettes and text. Each drawing tells a different story—some tragic, some hopeful—but all challenge the viewer’s perception of history.

Why It Stands Out:

  • Political urgency—Walker uses drawing to expose systemic racism in a visually striking way.
  • Technical mastery—her precise lines and layered narratives make each piece feel like a miniature film strip.

Lesson for Artists: Drawing can be a powerful tool for social commentary—don’t underestimate its impact.

2. Julie Mehretu – "Untitled (2023)"

Description: A massive ink and pencil drawing (over 8 feet tall), Mehretu’s work layers abstract maps, architectural sketches, and personal symbols. The chaotic yet controlled lines create a sense of movement, as if the viewer is traveling through time.

Why It Stands Out:

  • Scale and energy—the physical size makes the drawing immersive.
  • Hybridity—she blends abstract and figurative elements, proving drawing can be both personal and universal.

Lesson for Artists: Experiment with scale—sometimes, bigger is better.

3. Ai Weiwei – "Drawing for Spring" (2021)

Description: A series of delicate pencil sketches on rice paper, these works celebrate nature and resilience. Each drawing is minimalist yet profound, with subtle political undertones (e.g., a single bird symbolizing freedom).

Why It Stands Out:

  • Eco-conscious materials—using recycled paper aligns with his activist ethos.
  • Accessibility—the simplicity makes it approachable for all viewers.

Lesson for Artists: Less can be more—sometimes, the most powerful art is the simplest.

4. Wangechi Mutu – "The End of eating Everything" (2023)

Description: A collage-drawing hybrid, Mutu’s work combines ink, paper cutouts, and mixed media to create fantastical, hybrid figures. The textured surfaces and bold colors make it feel both ancient and futuristic.

Why It Stands Out:

  • Cultural fusion—she blends African, Western, and pop-culture influences.
  • Feminist themes—her strong, mythical women challenge traditional beauty standards.

Lesson for Artists: Don’t fear mixing mediums—drawing can be as experimental as painting.

5. Mark Bradford – "Untitled (2023)"

Description: A large-scale charcoal and ink drawing on recycled newspaper, Bradford’s work layers text, images, and marks to create a textured, almost tactile surface. The contrasts between light and dark make it feel like a physical landscape.

Why It Stands Out:

  • Sustainability—using old newspapers gives the work extra depth.
  • Urban storytelling—each mark tells a story about Chicago’s history.

Lesson for Artists: Found materials can elevate your work—think outside the traditional sketchbook.
