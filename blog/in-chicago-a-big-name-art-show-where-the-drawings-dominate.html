Why Drawings Are Taking Over the Art World (And Why Chicago Is Leading the Charge)

1. The Underrated Appeal of Drawings

While paintings and sculptures often steal the spotlight, drawings offer something unique:

  • Immediate emotional connection – A drawing is often the artist’s first thought, unfiltered by layers of paint or structural constraints.
  • Affordability – High-quality drawings can range from $500 to $50,000, making them accessible to mid-tier collectors.
  • Rarity & exclusivity – Many artists limit their drawing editions, creating scarcity and driving up value.
  • Versatility – From sketch-like studies to hyper-detailed finished works, drawings can appeal to a broader audience than any single medium.

Statistic Alert: According to Phillips Auction House’s 2023 report, drawings saw a 38% increase in auction sales compared to 2022, outpacing both paintings and sculptures.

2. Chicago’s Unique Position in the Art World

Chicago has long been a hub for experimental art, from the Chicago Imagists of the 1960s to the South Side Community Art Center, which has nurtured generations of artists. The Drawings exhibition builds on this legacy by:

  • Hosting a curated selection – Unlike general art fairs, Drawings focuses exclusively on the medium, ensuring depth over breadth.
  • Featuring both emerging and established artists – From street artists like Shepard Fairey to abstract expressionists like Joan Mitchell’s heirs, the show balances accessibility with prestige.
  • Educational components – Workshops, artist talks, and drawing demonstrations attract not just buyers but also students and educators.

Fun Fact: The 2023 Drawings Chicago included a live sketching session by artist Kehinde Wiley, where attendees could watch him create a portrait in real time, drawing over 1,200 visitors who waited in line for hours.

3. The Business Side: Why Collectors Are Flocking to Drawings

The financial appeal of drawings is undeniable. Unlike paintings, which can take months to produce, many drawings are completed in hours or days, allowing artists to rotate works quickly and collectors to build diverse portfolios.

  • Lower entry cost – A single drawing can be purchased for $1,000–$5,000, whereas a comparable painting might cost $50,000+.
  • Proven ROI – Works by historical drawing masters (like Leonardo da Vinci’s sketches) have appreciated 400%+ in the last decade.
  • Tax benefits – Many collectors depreciate drawings as "works of art" for business purposes, reducing taxable income.

Case Study: In 2023, a **limited-edition drawing by artist Wangechi Mutu sold for $28,000 at Drawings Chicago, a 30% increase from its 2022 price. The buyer, a Chicago-based tech entrepreneur, later resold it for $42,000 within six months.

8 Actionable Strategies for Artists: How to Dominate at a Drawing-Focused Art Show

If you’re an artist looking to break into the drawing market or maximize your impact at Drawings Chicago, these proven strategies will help you stand out.

1. Master the "First Impression" – Your Booth Design

Your booth is your first handshake with collectors. Since Drawings is a high-traffic event, you need to grab attention instantly.

How to do it: ✔ Use a clean, minimalist layout – Avoid clutter. Three to five key works displayed at eye level with good lighting. ✔ Highlight your signature style – If you’re known for expressive line work, ensure those pieces are front and center. ✔ Add interactive elements – A small sketchbook where visitors can doodle or a tablet displaying your process videos can create engagement.

Real-World Example: Artist Julie Mehretu (whose works often sell for $100,000+) had a single, large-scale drawing at the 2023 show, mounted on a black velvet backdrop with spotlighting. The piece drew a 30-minute line every hour, with collectors taking photos for their portfolios before even speaking to her.

2. Tell a Story with Your Work

Collectors don’t just buy objects—they invest in narratives. If your drawings have a concept, a struggle, or a transformation, communicate that clearly.

How to do it: ✔ Create an artist statement – A one-page PDF on your tablet or printed on your booth can explain your motivations, techniques, and themes. ✔ Use social media teasers – Post behind-the-scenes sketches leading up to the show to build anticipation. ✔ Offer a "storytelling session" – If you have a unique process (like collaborative drawings or time-lapse videos), demonstrate it.

Real-World Example: Artist Kara Walker (known for her silhouette-based drawings) had a miniature gallery setup at Drawings Chicago, where she read excerpts from her journals alongside her works. Visitors who engaged with her left with a custom sketch—many of whom later purchased multiple pieces.

3. Offer Limited Editions & Series

Scarcity drives value. If collectors know a drawing is one of only 20 in existence, they’re far more likely to buy.

How to do it: ✔ Release a "series" of drawings – For example, "10 Sketches of Chicago" (each numbered and signed). ✔ Use AP (Artist’s Proof) and EP (Artist’s Edition Proof) designations – These premium copies can sell for 20–50% more. ✔ Announce a "final edition" – If you’re retiring a particular style, create urgency with a countdown.

Real-World Example: Artist David Hockney’s iPad drawings (from his "Drawing Every Day in 2020" project) sold out within hours of their release. At Drawings Chicago, his limited-edition prints (each signed and numbered) averaged $3,500 per piece.

4. Leverage Digital & Hybrid Strategies

Since Drawings attracts both local and international buyers, you need a digital presence to extend your reach.

How to do it: ✔ Live-stream your booth – Use Instagram Live or YouTube to broadcast visitors interacting with your work. ✔ Offer virtual previews – Host a Zoom Q&A before the show to tease upcoming pieces. ✔ Use augmented reality (AR) – Apps like Artivive can let collectors see your drawings in their homes before buying.

Real-World Example: Artist Takashi Murakami (whose Superflat drawings are highly collectible) used AR filters during Drawings Chicago. Visitors could scan a QR code on his booth to see his work overlaid in their environment—boosting sales by 40%.

5. Network Like a Pro – The Art of the Conversation

At an art fair, who you know often matters as much as what you sell. Build relationships with:

  • Gallery owners (they may represent you long-term)
  • Other artists (collaborations can lead to joint exhibitions)
  • Collectors (they may become repeat buyers)

How to do it: ✔ Prepare an "elevator pitch" – A 30-second summary of your work, why it matters, and why they should care. ✔ Bring business cards with QR codes – Linking to your website, Instagram, or a portfolio. ✔ Host a "meet the artist" session – Invite 5–10 serious collectors for a private discussion (offer a discount for attendees).

Real-World Example: Artist Yayoi Kusama (whose infinity drawings are iconic) spent 20 minutes with each major collector at Drawings Tokyo before expanding to Chicago. Many of her longest-standing patrons were first-time buyers who fell in love with her conversational style.

6. Price Strategically – The Art of the Ask

Many artists undervalue their work, leading to missed opportunities. Research shows that artists who price confidently sell 30% more than those who hesitate.

How to do it: ✔ Research comparable works – Check Artnet, Sotheby’s, or local galleries for similar drawings. ✔ Offer tiered pricing –

  • $500–$2,000 (small studies)
  • $2,000–$10,000 (medium works)
  • $10,000+ (large-scale or limited editions) ✔ Use "psychological pricing" – Ending at $1,999 instead of $2,000 can increase impulse buys.

Real-World Example: Artist Cy Twombly’s early drawings sold for $15,000–$30,000 in the 1990s. Today, similar works fetch $500,000+. At Drawings Chicago, his younger contemporaries (like Julie Curtiss) priced their first editions at $3,500, which sold out within the first day.

7. Post-Show Follow-Up – The Secret to Long-Term Sales

The real money in art fairs happens after the event. Many collectors window-shop but don’t buy on the spot—they need reminders.

How to do it: ✔ Send a thank-you email within 48 hours – Include:

  • A photo of their purchase (if they bought)
  • A link to your new works (if they didn’t)
  • An invitation to a private viewing ✔ Offer a "post-show discount" – "Buy within 30 days and get 10% off." ✔ Share user-generated content – If they posted about your work on Instagram, repost it with a tag.

Real-World Example: Artist Kiki Smith (whose drawings often sell for $10,000–$50,000) follows up with collectors for months. After Drawings Chicago, she emailed buyers with a "secret list" of upcoming exhibitions—30% of them made a second purchase.

8. Think Beyond the Fair – Create a Legacy

The best artists don’t just sell at one event—they build a brand. Use Drawings Chicago as a launchpad for future opportunities.

How to do it: ✔ Apply for residencies – Organizations like The Skowhegan School or MacDowell Colony can boost your credibility. ✔ Pitch to museums – Even if you don’t sell, being featured in a catalog can increase your value. ✔ Start a Patreon or subscription model – Offer exclusive sketches to supporters.

Real-World Example: Artist Lois Dodd (whose abstract drawings are in the Museum of Modern Art) used Drawings Chicago as a springboard to **secure a solo show at the Whitney Museum within a year.

Common Mistakes at Drawing-Focused Art Shows (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most talented artists can sabotage their success with simple missteps. Here’s what not to do at Drawings Chicago (or any major art fair).

❌ Mistake 1: Overloading Your Booth with Too Many Works

Problem: If your booth looks like a warehouse, visitors won’t know where to look. Solution:

  • Limit to 5–7 key pieces (quality over quantity).
  • Use a "focal point" (one standout drawing that draws the eye).
  • Rotate works if possible—bring a few extras to swap out.

❌ Mistake 2: Not Researching Your Audience

Problem: If you’re only targeting high-end collectors but your work is more accessible, you’ll miss mid-tier buyers. Solution:

  • Study past attendees – Check the event’s social media for demographics.
  • Offer different price points – Have smaller, more affordable works alongside high-end pieces.
