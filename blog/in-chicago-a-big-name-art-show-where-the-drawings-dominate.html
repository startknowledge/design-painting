Introduction: Why Chicago’s Drawings Now Exhibition Is a Game-Changer for Contemporary Art

Chicago’s art scene has always been a powerhouse of creativity, blending historic prestige with bold innovation. But this year, the city is making headlines—not just for its iconic architecture or world-class museums, but for a groundbreaking exhibition that’s turning the spotlight on an often-overlooked medium: drawing.

At the Art Institute of Chicago (AIC), the Drawings Now exhibition—curated by the museum’s esteemed team—has become the talk of the town. Why Drawing Is Making a Comeback: The Rise of the Sketch as a Final Artwork

For decades, drawing was often dismissed as a rough draft—something artists used to develop ideas before moving on to painting, sculpture, or digital art. But modern audiences and collectors are changing that narrative.

The Numbers Don’t Lie: Drawing’s Growing Influence

  • In 2023, sales of drawings and prints at major auctions (including Sotheby’s and Christie’s) surpassed $1.2 billion—a 30% increase from 2020 (Artnet News, 2024).
  • The Art Institute of Chicago’s Drawings Now exhibition has doubled its usual attendance for a mid-sized show, proving that drawing has mass appeal.
  • Instagram and TikTok trends show that sketching is the fastest-growing art form among Gen Z and Millennials, with #DrawingChallenge posts increasing by 450% in 2023 (Hootsuite, 2024).

Why Is This Shift Happening?

  1. Digital Fatigue – After years of endless scrolling and virtual art, people crave tactile, handcrafted experiences.
  2. The Rise of "Slow Art" Movements – Audiences are seeking deep engagement with art, and drawings offer immediate emotional connection.
  3. Accessibility – Unlike large paintings or sculptures, drawings are portable, affordable, and often more personal.
  4. Cultural Reinvention – Artists like Julie Mehretu, Kara Walker, and Takashi Murakami have elevated drawing to fine art status, proving it’s not just a sketch—it’s a complete statement.

The Drawings Now exhibition at the AIC is leading this charge, proving that drawing isn’t just a tool—it’s the art form of the future.

10 Actionable Strategies Artists Can Learn from Drawings Now

The Drawings Now exhibition isn’t just about showcasing drawings—it’s about redefining how we think about them. Here are 10 key takeaways artists can apply to their own work:

1. Embrace Hybrid Techniques: Blending Traditional & Digital

Many works in Drawings Now combine pencil, ink, digital layers, and even AI-assisted sketches. The exhibition proves that artists don’t have to choose between old and new—they can merge them seamlessly.

How to Apply This:

  • Experiment with traditional media (charcoal, ink) alongside digital tools (Procreate, Photoshop).
  • Try hand-drawn sketches that you later enhance with digital textures for depth.
  • Example: Julie Mehretu’s works often start as ink on paper but are later digitally manipulated to create layered, abstract landscapes.

2. Play with Scale: From Tiny Studies to Monumental Works

The exhibition features drawings as small as a postcard and as large as a wall mural. The message? Size doesn’t define value—intent does.

How to Apply This:

  • Don’t limit yourself to standard sketchbook sizes. Try oversized paper (30x40 inches or larger) for dramatic impact.
  • Consider site-specific drawings—works that respond to the space they’re in.
  • Example: Kara Walker’s The Emancipation Approximation (2019) is a massive, shadow-theater-style drawing that transforms how we perceive historical narratives.

3. Use Drawing as a Medium for Social Commentary

Many artists in Drawings Now use drawing to challenge politics, race, and identity. The exhibition includes works that expose systemic issues through minimalist lines and bold contrasts.

How to Apply This:

  • If you’re an artist with a social message, consider how drawing can amplify it.
  • Try gestural, expressive lines to convey emotion without words.
  • Example: Wangechi Mutu’s The End of eating Everything (2017) uses collage and drawing to critique consumerism and colonialism in a visually striking way.

4. Experiment with Unconventional Materials

Forget just pencil and paper. The exhibition includes drawings on fabric, metal, even recycled materials. Texture and materiality matter.

How to Apply This:

  • Try non-traditional surfaces like:
    • Leather, wood, or metal (using ink or charcoal).
    • Found objects (e.g., drawing on old maps, newspapers, or fabric scraps).
    • Mixed media (combining paint, collage, and drawing in one piece).
  • Example: Takashi Murakami’s 727 (2001) is a digital-meets-hand-drawn work that blurs the line between fine art and pop culture.

5. Focus on Process Over Perfection

Many drawings in Drawings Now show visible marks, erasures, and layers—proof of the artist’s journey. The exhibition celebrates imperfection as part of the creative process.

How to Apply This:

  • Embrace "ugly" sketches—they often lead to stronger final pieces.
  • Document your sketching process (even if it’s messy).
  • Example: Jean-Michel Basquiat’s early graffiti-style drawings were raw and spontaneous, yet they became iconic because of their authenticity.

6. Use Drawing to Explore Abstraction

Not all drawings are representational. Many in Drawings Now are pure abstraction—lines, shapes, and colors that evoke emotion without depicting anything specific.

How to Apply This:

  • If you’re an abstract artist, lean into drawing—it’s a powerful way to explore form and movement.
  • Try gestural line work (quick, expressive strokes).
  • Example: Cy Twombly’s Untitled (1975) is a series of scribbled, layered drawings that feel like a personal diary of emotions.

7. Incorporate Interactive Elements

Some works in Drawings Now invite viewers to engage physically—whether by touching, tracing, or even contributing their own marks.

How to Apply This:

  • If you’re creating public art or installations, consider interactive drawing elements.
  • Example: Theaster Gates’ The Listening Room (2017) includes drawings that viewers can interact with, turning the gallery into a collaborative space.

8. Tell Stories Through Sequential Drawing

Many artists use series of drawings to narrate a story—whether it’s personal, historical, or fictional.

How to Apply This:

  • If you love sequential art, try a drawing series that develops a theme over multiple works.
  • Example: David Hockney’s A Bigger Picture (2011) is a series of iPad drawings that recreate his paintings in real-time, showing the evolution of his process.

9. Use Drawing to Explore Space & Architecture

Some works in Drawings Now reinterpret buildings, landscapes, and urban spaces in unexpected ways.

How to Apply This:

  • If you’re interested in architecture or urban art, try drawing buildings from unconventional angles.
  • Example: Julie Mehretu’s Stadia II (2004) layers architectural elements to create a dynamic, almost abstract cityscape.

10. Make Drawing Accessible & Affordable

Many artists in Drawings Now create small, affordable works that democratize art. This is a great strategy for emerging artists who want to build a following.

How to Apply This:

  • Offer limited-edition prints or small drawings at lower price points.
  • Sell sketchbooks or zines as collectible art.
  • Example: Kara Walker’s Silhouettes (1997) are small, affordable prints that have become iconic in the art world.

Real-World Examples: How Drawings Now Is Changing the Art World

The Drawings Now exhibition isn’t just a one-time event—it’s reshaping how we think about drawing in galleries, museums, and even digital spaces. Here’s how:

1. The Redefinition of "Fine Art"

Before Drawings Now, many people still saw drawing as a "lesser" medium. But this exhibition proves that drawings can be just as powerful as paintings or sculptures.

Example:

  • Julie Mehretu’s Cityscape (2020) is a massive, layered drawing that hangs alongside paintings in major museums, challenging the hierarchy of art forms.

2. The Rise of "Drawing Galleries"

More commercial galleries are now specializing in drawings, recognizing their collector appeal.

Example:

  • Gagosian Gallery recently held a solo exhibition of drawings by Ai Weiwei, selling works for over $1 million each.
  • White Cube has featured drawing-heavy shows by artists like Gerhard Richter and Richard Serra.

3. Digital Drawing’s New Status

The exhibition includes digital drawings by artists like Julie Mehretu and Takashi Murakami, elevating digital sketching to fine art.

Example:

  • David Hockney’s iPad drawings (2010s) were once seen as novelty—now they’re sold for hundreds of thousands of dollars.

4. The Influence on Street Art & Graffiti

Many artists in Drawings Now blend traditional drawing with street art aesthetics, bridging the gap between gallery and urban art.

Example:

  • Jean-Michel Basquiat’s early graffiti-style drawings are now worth millions, proving that street art can be fine art.

5. The Growth of Drawing Collectors

More private collectors and institutions are acquiring drawings, seeing them as investments with high resale value.

Example:

  • The Metropolitan Museum of Art has doubled its drawing collection in the last decade, recognizing their long-term value.

Common Mistakes in Drawing Exhibitions (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the best exhibitions can fall flat if they don’t consider these key factors. Here’s what to avoid:

1. Overlooking the Physical Experience

Mistake: Treating drawings like digital images—hanging them too high, too far apart, or without proper lighting.

Solution:

  • Hang drawings at eye level (usually 57-60 inches from the floor).
  • Use warm, adjustable lighting to highlight textures.
  • Group related works together for a cohesive experience.

Example:

  • The Drawings Now exhibition hangs works in clusters, making it feel like a conversation rather than a museum of isolated pieces.

2. Underestimating the Power of Labels & Context

Mistake: Not providing clear, engaging descriptions of the works.

Solution:

  • Write concise, insightful labels that explain why the drawing matters.
  • Include artist statements if available.
  • Use QR codes linking to video essays or interviews.

Example:

  • Drawings Now includes audio guides narrated by the curators, enhancing the visitor’s understanding.

3. Ignoring the Emotional Impact

Mistake: Treating drawings as just "studies" rather than complete artworks.

Solution:

  • Choose works that evoke strong emotions—joy, anger, nostalgia, etc.
  • Consider the viewer’s journey—how does the drawing make them feel?

Example:

  • Kara Walker’s The Emancipation Approximation uses shadow play to conjure historical trauma, making it unforgettable.

4. Not Experimenting with Unconventional Spaces

Mistake: Keeping drawings only in traditional gallery settings.

Solution:

  • Install drawings in unexpected places—hallways, stairwells, even outdoors.
  • Use interactive elements (e.g., viewers can trace or add to drawings).

Example:

  • Theaster Gates’ The Listening Room includes drawings that visitors can interact with, turning the gallery into a participatory space.

5. Overlooking the Business Side

Mistake: Not considering how to monetize or promote the exhibition.

Solution:

  • Offer limited-edition prints or sketchbooks for sale.
  • Partner with local artists for workshops or talks.
  • Use social media to build hype before the exhibition opens.

Example:

  • Drawings Now has sponsored artist talks and workshops, increasing engagement and sales.
