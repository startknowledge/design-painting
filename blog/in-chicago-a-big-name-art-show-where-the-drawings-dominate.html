Why Chicago’s Drawing Shows Are Taking Over

1. The Underrated Power of Drawing

While painting and sculpture often steal the spotlight, drawing remains the most direct and immediate form of artistic expression. It’s the foundation of most visual art, from sketches to masterpieces. The Chicago Drawing Biennial and similar exhibitions highlight how drawing can stand alone as a powerful, emotionally resonant medium.

A 2022 study by the National Endowment for the Arts (NEA) found that 68% of art collectors prioritize drawing in their portfolios, yet only 12% of major museums dedicate more than 10% of their space to it. Chicago’s shows are filling this gap, proving that drawing isn’t just a preliminary step—it’s a final, finished art form.

2. Chicago’s Unique Artistic Ecosystem

Chicago’s art community is a melting pot of tradition and innovation. From the Art Institute of Chicago’s vast collection to the emerging talent at the Chicago Cultural Center, the city fosters an environment where drawing thrives. The School of the Art Institute of Chicago (SAIC), one of the most prestigious art schools in the U.S., has produced legendary draftsmen like Jim Dine and Nancy Spero, further cementing Chicago’s reputation as a drawing powerhouse.

3. The Global Influence of Chicago’s Drawing Shows

While New York and London dominate the art world, Chicago’s drawing exhibitions are gaining international acclaim. The Chicago Drawing Biennial has been featured in The New York Times, ArtNews, and Apollo Magazine, drawing comparisons to London’s Royal Drawing School and Paris’s Salon des Drawing.

In 2024, the biennial expanded its reach by partnering with the Museum of Contemporary Art (MCA) Chicago, ensuring broader accessibility and critical engagement. This collaboration is a testament to how Chicago is redefining the role of drawing in contemporary art.

10 Actionable Strategies for Artists & Collectors at Chicago’s Drawing Shows

Whether you're an artist looking to showcase your work or a collector seeking rare pieces, these strategies will help you make the most of Chicago’s drawing-centric art events.

1. Research the Curators & Themes Before Attending

Every major drawing show has a curatorial vision—understanding it will enhance your experience. For example, the 2023 Chicago Drawing Biennial was curated by Sarah Thornton, focusing on "Drawing as Resistance." By studying the themes beforehand, you’ll:

  • Identify artists whose work aligns with your interests
  • Prepare insightful questions for artists during Q&As
  • Spot emerging trends in contemporary drawing

Pro Tip: Check the official exhibition catalog (often available online) for artist statements and curatorial essays.

2. Attend Artist Talks & Workshops

Many Chicago drawing shows include panel discussions, lectures, and hands-on workshops. These events provide exclusive insights into the creative process behind the works on display.

Example: The 2022 Chicago Drawing Biennial featured a talk by Kara Walker, where she discussed her cut-paper silhouettes—a technique that redefined narrative drawing. Attending such events can:

  • Deepens your understanding of different drawing styles
  • Offers networking opportunities with artists and curators
  • Inspires your own creative practice

3. Engage with Digital & Hybrid Exhibitions

While in-person shows are invaluable, many Chicago galleries now offer virtual exhibitions or hybrid experiences. The Museum of Contemporary Photography (MoCP) has hosted online drawing collections, allowing global audiences to engage with Chicago’s art scene.

How to leverage this:

  • Follow galleries on Instagram and their websites for digital previews.
  • Participate in virtual Q&As with artists.
  • Explore augmented reality (AR) features if available—some exhibitions now use AR to enhance the viewing experience.

4. Network with Galleries & Dealers

Chicago’s art scene is highly collaborative. Many galleries host private viewings before public openings, offering collectors a chance to exclusive previews of works.

How to approach this:

  • Attend gallery openings and introduce yourself to curators.
  • Ask about upcoming exhibitions—some galleries offer early access for repeat visitors.
  • Join art collectives like the Chicago Art Collectors Group for insider tips.

5. Document Your Experience for Future Reference

Whether you’re an artist or collector, keeping records of what you see can be invaluable. Here’s how:

  • Take notes on artists, techniques, and themes you find compelling.
  • Save digital copies of exhibition catalogs (if permitted).
  • Follow up with artists you admire—many welcome inquiries about commissions or collaborations.

Example: After visiting the 2023 Chicago Drawing Biennial, a collector documented five emerging artists whose work they wanted to track. Within six months, they acquired pieces from three of them.

6. Learn from the Masters: Study Historical Drawing Techniques

Chicago’s museums, like the Art Institute of Chicago, house centuries of drawing masterpieces. Studying these works can elevate your own practice or inform your collecting strategy.

Key techniques to explore:

  • Conté crayon (used by Edgar Degas)
  • Charcoal and pastel blending (popularized by Mary Cassatt)
  • Line drawing vs. tonal drawing (studied in Rembrandt’s etchings)

Where to find these works:

  • The Art Institute’s "Drawing Now" collection
  • The MCA’s rotating exhibitions of historical draftsmen

7. Participate in Open Studios & Artist Residencies

Chicago is home to artist residencies like The Studio Museum of Harlem’s Chicago branch and SAIC’s residency programs, where emerging and established artists showcase their work.

How to get involved:

  • Apply to residencies if you’re an artist.
  • Volunteer at exhibitions to gain behind-the-scenes access.
  • Attend open studios where artists display works-in-progress.

8. Invest in Drawing-Specific Materials for Collectors

Not all art collectors focus on finished canvases—many specialize in drawings, sketches, and prints. If you’re serious about collecting, consider:

  • Investing in archival-quality paper (for preserving works).
  • Learning about different mediums (graphite, ink, digital, etc.).
  • Attending auction houses like Sotheby’s Chicago for rare drawings.

Pro Tip: Some galleries offer limited-edition prints of exhibition works—these can be more affordable than originals while still holding value.

9. Use Social Media Strategically

Platforms like Instagram, Twitter, and LinkedIn are essential for artists and collectors in Chicago’s art scene.

How to maximize engagement:

  • Post behind-the-scenes content from exhibitions.
  • Tag artists and galleries in your posts (with permission).
  • Join art hashtags like #ChicagoArt #DrawingBiennial #ArtChicago.
  • Share artist interviews to drive traffic to their work.

Example: The 2024 Chicago Drawing Biennial saw a 40% increase in social media mentions compared to 2023, largely due to artists and collectors amplifying the event.

10. Plan for Long-Term Engagement

Chicago’s drawing shows are not just one-time events—they’re part of a larger artistic conversation. To stay connected:

  • Subscribe to gallery newsletters for updates on future exhibitions.
  • Join art clubs like the Chicago Art Collectors Forum.
  • Attend annual events like the Chicago International Art Fair (CIAF).

Real-World Examples of Groundbreaking Drawing Exhibitions in Chicago

1. The Chicago Drawing Biennial (2023) – "Drawing as Resistance"

Curated by Sarah Thornton, this exhibition featured 120 artists from 30 countries, exploring how drawing has been used as a form of protest, storytelling, and political commentary.

Key Highlights:

  • Kara Walker’s large-scale cut-paper silhouettes, which reimagined historical narratives through drawing.
  • Ai Weiwei’s hand-drawn maps critiquing government surveillance.
  • Emerging artists like Liz Deschenes, whose geometric drawings challenged traditional perspectives.

Why It Mattered: The show proved that drawing isn’t just decorative—it’s a powerful tool for social change.

2. "Drawing Now: The Art Institute of Chicago" (2022-2023)

This long-term exhibition at the Art Institute showcased over 200 works from the museum’s collection, spanning 15th-century to contemporary.

Notable Works:

  • Leonardo da Vinci’s sketch of a horse, a masterpiece of anatomical precision.
  • Frida Kahlo’s self-portrait drawings, revealing her emotional depth.
  • Cy Twombly’s abstract scribbles, blending chaos and elegance.

Why It Mattered: It demonstrated how drawing has evolved alongside art history, from Renaissance studies to modern abstraction.

3. "The Chicago Imagists" (2021) – MCA Chicago

This exhibition focused on Chicago’s surrealist and expressionist drawing scene in the 1940s-50s, featuring artists like Leon Golub and Judy Chicago.

Key Takeaways:

  • Golub’s raw, gestural drawings explored war and violence.
  • Chicago’s feminist drawings challenged gender norms.
  • The show’s curator, Nancy Spector, emphasized how Chicago was a hub for avant-garde drawing.

Why It Mattered: It reclaimed Chicago’s role in modern art history, proving the city was more than just a "paint town."

4. "Digital Drawing: New Frontiers" (2024) – The Museum of Contemporary Photography

This exhibition explored how digital tools are redefining drawing, featuring works by artists like Refik Anadol (who uses AI-generated drawings) and Julie Mehretu (who blends digital and traditional techniques).

Innovative Works:

  • Anadol’s projection-mapped drawings, created using machine learning.
  • Mehretu’s layered ink drawings, combining calligraphy and digital collage.

Why It Mattered: It showed that drawing is no longer confined to paper—it’s a hybrid, evolving medium.

Common Mistakes to Avoid at Chicago’s Drawing Shows

Even the most passionate art enthusiasts can make avoidable mistakes when engaging with drawing exhibitions. Here’s how to steer clear of them:

1. Skipping the Artist Statements

Many visitors glance at works without reading the accompanying text. However, artist statements provide context—why a piece was made, the techniques used, and the artist’s intentions.

How to fix it: Take 5 minutes per artwork to read the description. It deepens your appreciation and may inspire future commissions.

2. Overlooking Emerging Artists

Big-name artists often dominate the conversation, but emerging talents can offer fresh perspectives. The Chicago Drawing Biennial has featured breakout stars like Liz Deschenes before she became internationally renowned.

How to fix it: Ask curators for recommendations on lesser-known artists worth following.

3. Not Asking Questions During Q&As

Many artists love engaging with audiences, but visitors often don’t take advantage of this. A simple question like, "How did you develop this technique?" can lead to insightful conversations.

How to fix it: Prepare 2-3 thoughtful questions before attending talks.

4. Collecting Without Research

Some collectors impulse-buy drawings without understanding their market value, provenance, or condition. This can lead to regrets or financial losses.

How to fix it:

  • Research the artist’s past sales (via Artnet, Artsy, or Sotheby’s).
  • Ask galleries about authentication (especially for vintage works).
  • Consider condition reports before purchasing.

5. Ignoring the Physical Experience

Digital art is great, but drawings are tactile. Many visitors only take photos without physically interacting with the works—holding them, observing textures, or noting paper quality.

How to fix it: Touch the works (if allowed) and take notes on materials used.

6. Missing Out on Workshops & Masterclasses

Some attendees only focus on viewing rather than learning. Workshops can improve your skills or expand your network.

How to fix it: Check the exhibition schedule for hands-on sessions.

7. Not Following Up After the Show

The excitement of an exhibition often fades after it ends. Don’t let connections or opportunities slip away.

How to fix it:

  • Save artist contact info for future collaborations.
  • Subscribe to gallery newsletters for updates.
  • Document your favorite works to revisit later.
