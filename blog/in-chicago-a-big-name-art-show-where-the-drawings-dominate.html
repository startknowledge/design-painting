Why Drawings Are Taking Over the Art World (And Why Chicago’s Show Is the Epicenter)

1. The Resurgence of the Sketch: Why Drawings Are More Valuable Than Ever

For decades, paintings and sculptures dominated the art market. But in the last decade, drawings have surged in value and prestige. According to Artnet’s 2023 Market Report, sales of original drawings and prints increased by 42% from 2020 to 2023, outpacing traditional mediums.

Why?

  • Accessibility: Unlike large-scale paintings, drawings are lighter, more portable, and easier to collect.
  • Process Over Product: Buyers today value the artist’s thought process—drawings reveal ideas in their raw form.
  • Digital vs. Analog: While digital art grows, hand-drawn works carry a tactile, human authenticity that resonates with collectors.

Chicago’s Drawings exhibition capitalizes on this trend by highlighting both historic and contemporary works, proving that sketching is not just a preliminary step—it’s a final, powerful statement.

2. The Art Institute of Chicago’s Legacy in Supporting Drawings

The AIC has a century-long commitment to drawings, with its collection boasting over 100,000 works—one of the largest in the world. The annual Drawings exhibition is a curatorial celebration of this medium, featuring:

  • Rare masterpieces (e.g., Leonardo da Vinci’s Study of a Horse’s Head)
  • Contemporary breakthroughs (e.g., Kara Walker’s intricate cut-paper silhouettes)
  • Emerging artists pushing boundaries with new materials and techniques

This year’s show, titled “Unseen: Drawings from the Collection,” includes works never before displayed, making it a must-see for art lovers.

10 Actionable Strategies to Make Your Drawings Stand Out (Like the Ones in Chicago’s Show)

If you’re an artist looking to elevate your sketches, these strategies—inspired by the works in Chicago’s exhibition—will help you craft drawings that command attention.

1. Master the "Sketch-to-Finish" Mindset (Like Rembrandt)

Many artists treat drawings as preliminary studies, but the best works—such as Rembrandt’s The Three Crosses—are complete in themselves.

How to apply this:

  • Treat every drawing as a final piece, even if it’s small.
  • Experiment with layering (charcoal, ink, watercolor) to add depth.
  • Sign and date your works—this adds authenticity, just like the AIC’s historic sketches.

2. Play with Scale and Format (Inspired by Kara Walker’s Silhouettes)

Kara Walker’s large-scale cut-paper works in the AIC show prove that size doesn’t limit creativity.

How to apply this:

  • Try miniatures (e.g., 4x6 inches) for intimate, high-impact pieces.
  • Experiment with unconventional formats (e.g., scrolls, accordion books).
  • Use negative space to create visual tension, as Walker does in her narratives.

3. Incorporate Mixed Media (Like Cy Twombly’s Scribbles)

Cy Twombly’s chaotic, ink-stained drawings in the AIC collection show how abstraction + gesture can create emotional power.

How to apply this:

  • Combine pencil, ink, and paint for textural contrast.
  • Add collage elements (torn paper, fabric) for unexpected depth.
  • Embrace imperfection—Twombly’s "mistakes" are his signature.

4. Tell a Story (Like Francisco Goya’s Etchings)

Goya’s Los Caprichos in the AIC show are not just drawings—they’re social commentaries.

How to apply this:

  • Use drawings as narratives (e.g., sequential sketches, zines).
  • Explore dark or satirical themes (like Goya’s critiques of society).
  • Engage with politics or culture—contemporary buyers love meaningful work.

5. Experiment with Unconventional Materials (Like Yayoi Kusama’s Polka Dots)

Kusama’s ink-and-paper works in the AIC show prove that repetition and pattern can be visually hypnotic.

How to apply this:

  • Try non-traditional tools (e.g., ballpoint pen, coffee stains, sand).
  • Use stencils or repetitive motifs for rhythmic impact.
  • Play with texture (e.g., rubbing, scratching, erasing).

6. Focus on Line Work (Like Edgar Degas’ Pastels)

Degas’ delicate pastel drawings in the AIC collection show how line can be both delicate and powerful.

How to apply this:

  • Practice different line techniques (hatching, cross-hatching, stippling).
  • Use varying pressures for dynamic contrast.
  • Limit color—sometimes black-and-white is more striking.

7. Study Light and Shadow (Like Leonardo’s Studies)

Leonardo’s Vitruvian Man sketches in the AIC show mastery of chiaroscuro—the play of light and shadow.

How to apply this:

  • Use shading to create depth (even in small works).
  • Experiment with light sources (e.g., backlighting, side lighting).
  • Study anatomy through light—this adds dramatic realism.

8. Make Your Drawings Interactive (Like Julian Schnabel’s "Chopped" Canvases)

While not a drawing, Schnabel’s collage-like approach in the AIC’s broader collection shows how physical engagement can elevate art.

How to apply this:

  • Create "participatory" drawings (e.g., viewers can add marks).
  • Use erasable media (e.g., white chalk on black paper).
  • Experiment with kinetic elements (e.g., movable parts).

9. Document Your Process (Like David Hockney’s iPad Drawings)

Hockney’s digital sketches in the AIC show prove that documenting ideas can be art in itself.

How to apply this:

  • Sketch daily—even if it’s just quick studies.
  • Use a sketchbook as a journal (like Hockney’s A Bigger Picture).
  • Share your process online—this builds audience engagement.

10. Price and Market Your Work Strategically (Like the AIC’s Collectors)

The AIC’s Drawings exhibition attracts high-net-worth collectors, but even emerging artists can position their work for sales.

How to apply this:

  • Offer limited editions (e.g., 10/50 prints) to increase accessibility.
  • Sell "drawing sets" (e.g., a series of related sketches).
  • Partner with galleries that specialize in drawings and prints.

Common Mistakes in Drawing (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the best artists make beginner-friendly mistakes. Here’s how to steer clear of them:

1. Overworking the Drawing

Mistake: Smoothing out every line until the essence is lost. Fix: Embrace imperfection—sometimes roughness is more interesting.

2. Ignoring Composition

Mistake: Placing subjects randomly without balance. Fix: Use the rule of thirds or golden ratio for visual harmony.

3. Skipping the Sketch

Mistake: Jumping straight to final details without a rough draft. Fix: Always thumbnail first—this saves time and improves ideas.

4. Using Only One Medium

Mistake: Sticking to pencil only and missing textural variety. Fix: Experiment with charcoal, ink, watercolor, collage.

5. Not Signing or Dating Work

Mistake: Leaving drawings unsigned, making them harder to sell. Fix: Sign and date every piece—this adds value and authenticity.

6. Copying Instead of Observing

Mistake: Replicating photographs or other artists without personal input. Fix: Study, then transform—add your unique perspective.

7. Neglecting Lighting

Mistake: Sketching in poor lighting, leading to flat, dull tones. Fix: Use natural light or bright studio lamps for accurate shadows.

8. Not Protecting Your Work

Mistake: Leaving drawings unframed or exposed, risking damage. Fix: Use archival paper, acid-free mats, and UV-protective glass.

2. What are the best materials for professional drawings?

Answer: The best materials depend on your style and budget, but these are industry standards:

  • Paper: Arches, Fabriano, or Strathmore (acid-free, heavyweight).
  • Pencils: Graphite (H, 2B, 4B), charcoal (vine, compressed), pastels (soft or oil).
  • Ink: Sumi, India ink, or fountain pen ink for precision.
  • Mixed Media: Collage paper, sand, coffee stains, or gold leaf for texture.

3. How do I exhibit my drawings in a gallery?

Answer: Exhibiting requires strategy and persistence:

  1. Build a portfolio (digital and physical) of your best works.
  2. Research galleries that specialize in drawings, prints, or contemporary art.
  3. Submit to open calls (many galleries have emerging artist programs).
  4. Network at art fairs, residencies, and local events.
  5. Consider consignment—some galleries take 20-30% commission but handle sales.

4. Can digital drawings be considered "real" art?

Answer: Absolutely! Digital drawings are legitimate art, especially when:

  • They mimic traditional techniques (e.g., Procreate brushes that feel like charcoal).
  • They explore new concepts (e.g., AI-assisted sketches, interactive digital art).
  • They are signed and dated (just like analog works).

Pro Tip: Many contemporary galleries now accept digital prints as original works.
