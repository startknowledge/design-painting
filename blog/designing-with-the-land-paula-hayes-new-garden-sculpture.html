Understanding Paula Hayes’ Design Philosophy: "Designing With the Land"

Paula Hayes doesn’t just place sculptures in gardens—she collaborates with the land. Her work is rooted in three core principles:

  1. Respect for Natural Growth Patterns – Instead of forcing rigid shapes, Hayes observes how plants, water, and soil interact, then mirrors those movements in her designs.
  2. Functional Artistry – Every element serves a purpose, whether it’s drainage, shade, or wildlife habitat, while still being visually stunning.
  3. Evolution Over Time – Her sculptures are not static; they change with seasons, weather, and even human interaction, creating a living dialogue between art and nature.

"A garden sculpture should feel like an extension of the land itself," Hayes once said. "It shouldn’t just be there—it should become part of the ecosystem."

8 Actionable Strategies to Design With the Land (Like Paula Hayes)

Inspired by Hayes’ approach, here are practical ways to integrate organic artistry into your own garden or landscape projects:

1. Study the Microclimate Before Designing

Before placing any sculpture or structure, analyze how sunlight, wind, and moisture affect the space. Hayes often uses local soil composition and plant life to guide her designs.

How to apply this:

  • Conduct a 24-hour observation of the area—note where shade shifts, where water pools, and how wind patterns move debris.
  • Use digital tools like SunPath or Windrose to map solar exposure and airflow.
  • Example: In a coastal garden, Hayes might use driftwood and seashells to create a windbreak that also doubles as a seating area, allowing the sculpture to function as both art and utility.

2. Use "Soft" Materials That Blend Naturally

Hayes avoids harsh, man-made materials in favor of organic textures—stone, wood, clay, and even repurposed natural debris. This ensures the sculpture ages gracefully rather than looking out of place.

How to apply this:

  • Source materials locally—river rocks, fallen branches, or even petrified wood—to maintain an authentic feel.
  • Avoid painted finishes unless they’re earth-toned and weather-resistant (e.g., natural linseed oil on wood).
  • Example: In her "Whispering Pines" installation, Hayes stacked irregularly shaped boulders to form a seating nook. Over time, moss and lichen grew on them, making the piece part of the landscape’s natural cycle.

3. Design for Seasonal Change

Hayes’ sculptures are not static; they evolve with the seasons. Incorporating temporary elements (like dried grasses or seasonal flowers) keeps the design dynamic.

How to apply this:

  • Plan for "soft" transitions—use woven willow fences that can be adjusted or movable stone arrangements.
  • Incorporate plants that change color (e.g., purple coneflowers in summer, goldenrod in fall).
  • Example: Her "Autumn’s Palette" sculpture features a wooden frame filled with seasonal foliage, so the piece transforms from green in spring to fiery reds and oranges in fall.

4. Create Functional Art Spaces

The best garden sculptures serve a purpose. Hayes often designs seating, water features, or shade structures that also function as art.

How to apply this:

  • Combine a sculpture with a bench—use driftwood as armrests or slabs of stone as seats.
  • Integrate water elements—a stone-lined stream can double as a reflective mirror for light.
  • Example: In her "Moonlit Grove" project, Hayes carved a hollowed-out oak log into a fire pit and seating circle, ensuring the sculpture enhances social gatherings while remaining a focal point.

5. Work With Natural Water Flow

Water is a powerful design element in Hayes’ work. She never forces water—instead, she guides it through gentle curves and organic shapes.

How to apply this:

  • Use meandering paths for streams or fountains to mimic natural riverbends.
  • Add boulders or plants to slow water flow and create miniature waterfalls.
  • Example: Her "River’s Embrace" installation features a shallow, winding pond lined with smooth river stones, where frogs and dragonflies naturally gather, making the sculpture a thriving ecosystem.

6. Incorporate "Hidden" Art Elements

Hayes often buries or partially conceals sculptures, revealing them gradually as one moves through the garden.

How to apply this:

  • Use low walls or hedges to frame a sculpture from a distance.
  • Plant climbing vines (like clematis or wisteria) to partially obscure a stone feature.
  • Example: In her "Hidden Grove" project, a wooden archway leads to a secret garden where a stone owl sculpture sits atop a moss-covered boulder—only fully visible when approached.

7. Use Lighting to Highlight Organic Forms

Nighttime transforms Hayes’ sculptures into ethereal, glowing forms. She uses soft, warm lighting to enhance natural textures.

How to apply this:

  • Embed LED strips behind smooth stone slabs for a subtle glow.
  • Use solar-powered path lights to outline driftwood or boulders.
  • Example: Her "Nocturne" installation features a wooden spiral staircase wrapped in fairy lights, making the sculpture appear to float in the dark.

8. Involve the Community in the Design Process

Hayes believes the best garden art stories come from collaboration. She often works with local artisans, gardeners, and even children to co-create pieces.

How to apply this:

  • Host a "garden sculpture workshop" where participants collect natural materials and build a communal piece.
  • Feature local stories in the design—e.g., carve initials into a tree or use stones from a family heirloom.
  • Example: In a public park project, Hayes invited elderly residents to paint patterns on reclaimed wooden benches, turning the sculpture into a living mural.

Common Mistakes in Garden Sculpture Design (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned designs can go wrong. Here are five pitfalls and how to prevent them:

1. Ignoring Scale & Proportion

Mistake: Placing a massive boulder in a small courtyard or a tiny stone figure in a grand garden. Solution:

  • Measure your space before selecting materials.
  • Use the "rule of thirds"—divide your garden into sections and place sculptures at key focal points.
  • Example: Hayes once had to reposition a 3-foot-tall stone deer in a client’s backyard because it looked lost—she later paired it with a cluster of native shrubs to anchor it.

2. Overlooking Maintenance Needs

Mistake: Choosing delicate materials (like carved sandalwood) that require constant upkeep. Solution:

  • Opt for low-maintenance materials—cedar, slate, or concrete with a natural finish.
  • Design for self-sustaining ecosystems—e.g., a stone water feature that cleans itself with natural filtration.
  • Example: A painted metal sculpture in a humid climate may rust and peel—Hayes avoids this by using weather-resistant finishes like powder-coated steel.

3. Forcing Unnatural Shapes

Mistake: Creating perfectly symmetrical sculptures that clash with the garden’s organic flow. Solution:

  • Embrace imperfections—uneven stones, gnarled branches, and irregular curves look more natural.
  • Study local flora and fauna for inspiration—how do trees grow? How do rivers bend?
  • Example: Hayes’ "Wildflower Mandala" features asymmetrical stone circles that mimic the organic patterns of a beehive.

4. Neglecting Safety & Accessibility

Mistake: Placing sharp-edged sculptures near walkways or unstable structures in high-traffic areas. Solution:

  • Round edges on stone or wood to prevent injuries.
  • Ensure pathways are wide enough for wheelchairs and strollers.
  • Example: In a public garden, Hayes covered a jagged rock formation with soft moss and sand to make it safe for children to touch.

5. Disconnecting from the Site’s History

Mistake: Ignoring the cultural or historical significance of the land. Solution:

  • Research the area’s past—were there ancient stone walls? Indigenous land-use practices?
  • Incorporate local materials—reclaimed bricks, native stones, or traditional carving techniques.
  • Example: In a historic farmstead, Hayes used old barn wood to create a wind chime sculpture, preserving the site’s heritage while adding artistry.

1. "The Breathing Wall" (Residential Garden, Portland, Oregon)

This living sculpture features a curved stone wall that undulates like a wave, with small crevices where moss and ferns grow. The design guides airflow through the garden, creating a cool microclimate in summer while allowing sunlight to filter through in winter.

Key Features:

  • Materials: Locally quarried basalt (weather-resistant) and native ferns.
  • Function: Natural ventilation for the home + sound absorption (reduces noise from nearby traffic).
  • Evolution: Over time, ivy climbs the wall, softening its edges and changing its appearance seasonally.

2. "The Driftwood Bridge" (Coastal Retreat, Maine)

A delicate wooden bridge made from salvaged driftwood, spanning a shallow tidal pool. The design allows water to flow beneath it, creating a miniature waterfall effect when the tide rises.

Key Features:

  • Materials: Saltwater-resistant black locust (naturally rot-resistant).
  • Function: Seating area + wildlife habitat (frogs and crabs use it as a resting spot).
  • Evolution: Seaweed and barnacles grow on the underside, changing its texture over time.

3. "The Sunken Garden" (Botanical Conservatory, Chicago)

A depressed circular garden lined with smooth river stones, filled with water-loving plants like lotus and water lilies. The centerpiece is a stone lotus sculpture, partially submerged in the pond.

Key Features:

  • Materials: Limestone (for the pond edge) and copper (for the lotus petals, which patina over time).
  • Function: Rainwater collection system + a cooling oasis in the urban heat.
  • Evolution: Frogs lay eggs in the pond, and butterflies visit the flowers, making the sculpture a thriving mini-ecosystem.

4. "The Whispering Stones" (Public Park, New York)

A series of irregularly shaped boulders arranged in a semi-circle, where sound waves between them create a natural echo effect. Visitors can whisper into the stones, and their voices carry in a haunting, melodic way.

Key Features:

  • Materials: Granite (for durability) and sandstone (for sound absorption).
  • Function: Aural art installation + shade structure for park-goers.
  • Evolution: Moss grows between the stones, softening the sound over time.

5. "The Root System" (Urban Rooftop Garden, Los Angeles)

A vertical garden sculpture made from exposed tree roots (from a fallen oak) wrapped around a steel frame. The roots continue growing, twisting and turning as they climb the structure.

Key Features:

  • Materials: Live roots (from a transplanted tree) + recycled steel.
  • Function: Air purification (trees absorb CO₂) + a living art piece.
  • Evolution: The roots expand, changing the sculpture’s shape each year.
