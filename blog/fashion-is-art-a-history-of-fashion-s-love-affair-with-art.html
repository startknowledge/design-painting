‘Fashion is Art’: A History of Fashion’s Love Affair with Art

By Design & Painting | Updated May 2024

*Fashion is art*—a statement so iconic it’s been repeated by designers, critics, and celebrities for decades. But what does this really mean? Is it merely a catchphrase, or does it reflect a centuries-old relationship between two of humanity’s most expressive mediums? The answer lies in a rich tapestry of history, where artists and fashion designers have continuously inspired, challenged, and redefined each other.

Today, the fashion industry is worth a staggering **$3.3 trillion globally** (McKinsey & Company, 2023), with art influencing everything from runway aesthetics to streetwear trends. A 2022 report by the Business of Fashion found that **68% of consumers** now associate fashion with artistic expression, up from 42% in 2018. This shift isn’t accidental—it’s the result of a deep, evolving dialogue between fashion and art that spans centuries.

In this comprehensive guide, we’ll explore:

  • The earliest intersections of fashion and art in history
  • Key artistic movements that shaped fashion (Renaissance to Modernism)
  • Iconic collaborations between designers and artists
  • How contemporary fashion houses blend art with wearable design
  • Actionable strategies for artists and designers to collaborate effectively
  • Common pitfalls and how to avoid them
Whether you’re an artist looking to break into fashion or a fashion enthusiast curious about the roots of your favorite trends, this post will give you the insights you need.

From the Renaissance to the Runway: The Ancient Roots of Fashion as Art

1. The Renaissance: Where Fashion and Art First Danced

The Renaissance (14th–17th century) was a golden age for both fashion and art, where **clothing became a canvas for storytelling**. Artists like Leonardo da Vinci and Raphael didn’t just paint portraits—they designed garments that reflected their subjects’ status, personality, and even moral character.

Take, for example, the **elaborate robes of the Italian Renaissance**. These weren’t just practical garments; they were **three-dimensional paintings** that conveyed wealth and power. The **double-breasted tunics** worn by noblemen were often embroidered with intricate gold thread, mimicking the detailed brushwork of altarpieces. Meanwhile, **Venetian glasswork**—used in jewelry and accessories—echoed the opulence of Titian’s paintings.

One of the most famous examples is the **costume of Isabella d’Este**, a patron of the arts who commissioned portraits that highlighted her **lavish silks and velvets**. Her wardrobe was so iconic that artists like **Bellini and Mantegna** used her clothing as a visual shorthand for her influence.

2. The Baroque Era: Drama, Excess, and the Birth of Fashion Photography

The Baroque period (17th–18th century) took this further, with fashion becoming **a theatrical performance**. The **hoop skirts of the 1600s** weren’t just wider—they were **architectural statements**, inspired by the dramatic compositions of Caravaggio and Rubens. The **ruffled collars and powdered wigs** of the time mirrored the **tenebrism** (dramatic lighting) of Baroque art.

Even the **first fashion plates** (engravings of clothing) in the 17th century were designed to look like **miniature paintings**. Publishers like **Pierre Mignard** in France created illustrations that were so lifelike, they blurred the line between art and fashion. These weren’t just advertisements—they were **visual poetry**.

3. The 19th Century: Romanticism, Pre-Raphaelites, and the Rise of the Fashion Plate

The **Romantic era** (early 1800s) brought a new wave of artistic influence, with fashion embracing **dramatic silhouettes and symbolic motifs**. The **Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood**, a group of artists like **Dante Gabriel Rossetti and John Everett Millais**, had a profound impact on women’s fashion.

Their **dark, mystical themes** (think: long, flowing dresses with floral embroidery) were directly reflected in the **crinoline skirts** and **lace-trimmed gowns** of the time. Even the **hairstyles**—like the **Victorian "mourning wreath"**—were inspired by the **idealized beauty** of Pre-Raphaelite paintings.

Meanwhile, **Charles Frederick Worth**, often called the "father of haute couture," began using **live models** in his Parisian atelier, treating fashion like a **living art installation**. His clients, including **Empress Eugénie of France**, wore his designs as **walking canvases**, with each garment telling a story.

Art Movements That Shaped Fashion: From Cubism to Street Art

4. The Avant-Garde: When Fashion Became a Rebellion

The early 20th century was a **revolution** for both fashion and art. Movements like **Futurism, Dadaism, and Surrealism** rejected tradition, and fashion followed suit.

**Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel** was one of the first to embrace this spirit. Her **little black dress (1926)** wasn’t just a garment—it was a **visual rebellion** against the corseted silhouettes of the past. Meanwhile, **Paul Poiret**, another pioneer, used **abstract patterns** inspired by **Futurist art** in his designs.

The **Dada movement**, with its **anti-art philosophy**, found its way into fashion through **asymmetrical cuts and unexpected fabric choices**. **Christian Dior’s "New Look" (1947)** was a direct response to the **austerity of WWII**, but its **hourglass silhouette** had roots in the **idealized female forms** of **Renaissance art**, reimagined for a modern audience.

5. Pop Art: Andy Warhol and the Birth of the Fashion Icon

The **1960s Pop Art explosion** changed fashion forever. **Andy Warhol**, who famously said *"Fashion is just art that touches the body,"* became the bridge between high art and high fashion.

His **silkscreen portraits of Marilyn Monroe and Campbell’s Soup Cans** were directly translated into **print designs** by **Mary Quant** and **Courrèges**. The **mod aesthetic**—**bold colors, geometric patterns, and minimalist tailoring**—was a direct homage to Warhol’s **repetitive, mass-produced imagery**.

Even **Yves Saint Laurent’s "Mondrian Dress" (1965)** was inspired by **Piet Mondrian’s abstract compositions**, turning a painting into a wearable statement. The dress wasn’t just a fashion piece—it was a **living art installation**.

6. Minimalism and Conceptual Art: Less Is More (But Still Meaningful)

The **1970s and 80s** brought **minimalism** to fashion, influenced by artists like **Donald Judd and Agnes Martin**. Designers like **Helmut Lang** and **Rei Kawakubo (Comme des Garçons)** used **clean lines, neutral palettes, and deconstructed silhouettes** to create garments that felt like **sculptures**.

Kawakubo’s **1981 "Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body"** collection was a **conceptual masterpiece**, where clothing was **not just worn but experienced**. The **asymmetrical cuts and layered fabrics** mirrored the **abstract compositions** of **Minimalist painters**.

Meanwhile, **Jean-Paul Gaultier’s "Cone Bra" (1990)** was a **sculptural statement**, inspired by **modernist architecture** and **body art**. It challenged conventions, just as **Yoko Ono’s performance art** had done decades earlier.

7. Street Art and Graffiti: From Banksy to High Fashion

The **21st century** has seen **street art** become one of the most influential forces in fashion. **Banksy**, the anonymous British street artist, has collaborated with brands like **Supreme, Balenciaga, and Louis Vuitton**, proving that **graffiti can be as prestigious as a museum piece**.

**Virgil Abloh’s work at Louis Vuitton (2018–2023)** was a **masterclass in blending street art with haute couture**. His **"Louis Vuitton x Off-White" collections** featured **bold, stencil-like logos, distressed fabrics, and surreal imagery**—all inspired by **graffiti culture and pop art**.

Even **Nike’s "The Ten" sneakers (2020)**, designed in collaboration with **Shepard Fairey (the artist behind the Obama "Hope" poster)**, were a **fusion of streetwear and fine art**. The **distressed leather and political motifs** turned a sneaker into a **walking mural**.

10 Iconic Fashion-Art Collaborations That Redefined Creativity

8. **Yves Saint Laurent x Andy Warhol (1973)

One of the **first major fashion-art collaborations**, this partnership was a **love letter to Pop Art**. Warhol designed **silkscreen prints** for Saint Laurent’s **parfums and accessories**, including the iconic **"Lolita" perfume bottle**, which featured a **Warhol-style portrait of a woman**.

The collaboration wasn’t just about aesthetics—it was about **democratizing art**. Warhol’s **repetitive, mass-produced imagery** challenged the exclusivity of haute couture, making fashion **accessible to the masses**.

9. **Alexander McQueen x Damien Hirst (2008)

McQueen’s **"Shark" jacket** (2008) was a **bold statement** on the intersection of fashion and art. The **shark-shaped fur collar** was inspired by Hirst’s **"The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living" (1991)**, a **shark preserved in formaldehyde**.

The piece was **both horrifying and beautiful**, much like Hirst’s work. It forced viewers to question **ethics in art and fashion**, making it one of the most **controversial yet influential** collaborations in history.

10. **Balenciaga x Takashi Murakami (2003–Present)

Murakami, the **godfather of Superflat art**, has had a **long-standing relationship with Balenciaga**. Their collaborations feature **bright, cartoonish motifs** that **blur the line between high art and pop culture**.

The **"Rainbow Tribal" collection (2019)** was a **visual explosion** of **Murakami’s "Happy Trees" and Balenciaga’s avant-garde tailoring**. The **oversized silhouettes and bold colors** made even the most **high-fashion garments feel like streetwear**.

11. **Chanel x Jean-Paul Goude (1990s)

Goude, a **French photographer and painter**, became Chanel’s **official photographer in the 1990s**. His **surreal, dreamlike images** influenced **Karl Lagerfeld’s designs**, particularly the **"Chanel x Goude" collections** of the late '90s.

Goude’s **use of **gold leaf, intricate embroidery, and theatrical lighting** translated directly into **Chanel’s "Ballet Russes" collection (1999)**, where **ballet-inspired dresses** were adorned with **Goude’s signature motifs**.

12. **Dior x Jeff Koons (2017)

Koons, known for his **hyper-realistic sculptures**, collaborated with **Maria Grazia Chiuri (Dior’s creative director at the time)** to create **"Dior x Jeff Koons: The Flower"**.

The collection featured **giant, sculptural floral embroideries** inspired by Koons’ **"Puppy" sculptures**. The **three-dimensional flowers** on the dresses were **so intricate** that they looked like **living art installations**.
