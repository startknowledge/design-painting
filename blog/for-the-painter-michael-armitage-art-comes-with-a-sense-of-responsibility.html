For the Painter Michael Armitage, Art Comes With a ‘Sense of Responsibility’: How to Approach Painting with Depth and Purpose

Introduction: The Weight of Creation in Modern Art

In an era where digital art dominates galleries and social media feeds, the act of painting—with its tactile, deliberate, and often labor-intensive process—remains a deeply meaningful pursuit. Among contemporary painters, Michael Armitage stands out not just for his technical skill but for the philosophical weight he carries in his work. His paintings, often sprawling and layered with symbolic narratives, reflect a sense of responsibility toward art as a form of storytelling, critique, and emotional engagement.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel and UBS Global Art Market Report, over 60% of contemporary art collectors prioritize works that carry social, cultural, or philosophical depth over purely aesthetic appeal. This shift underscores a growing demand for art that doesn’t just look good but also means something. For painters like Armitage, this means approaching each canvas with intentionality, ethical awareness, and a willingness to challenge the viewer.

  • Why a "sense of responsibility" matters in painting
  • Eight actionable strategies to infuse your work with depth and purpose
  • Real-world examples of artists who embody this mindset
  • Common mistakes that dilute meaning in painting
  • FAQs to help you navigate ethical and creative challenges

By the end, you’ll have a clear roadmap for creating paintings that resonate beyond the surface—just like Armitage’s.

Why a ‘Sense of Responsibility’ Defines Great Painting

Michael Armitage’s work is often described as dense, surreal, and politically charged, yet it also carries a quiet humanity. His paintings—such as The World Is Not Enough (2018) and The Last Supper (2020)—don’t just depict scenes; they comment on power, history, and the human condition. This isn’t accidental. It’s the result of a deliberate approach where the artist sees themselves as a storyteller, a critic, and a keeper of cultural memory.

The Psychological and Ethical Dimensions of Painting

A 2022 study in The Journal of Aesthetic Education found that 72% of viewers remember artworks that evoke emotional or intellectual engagement more vividly than those that are purely decorative. This suggests that meaningful art isn’t just about skill—it’s about connection.

When Armitage paints, he doesn’t just apply brushstrokes; he weighs the impact of his choices. Here’s why this matters:

  1. Art as a Mirror of Society – Your paintings reflect the world around you. If you ignore systemic issues (racism, environmental collapse, political corruption), your work risks becoming complicit in silence.
  2. The Viewer’s Experience – A painting that makes someone pause, question, or feel has a lasting effect. Without depth, art becomes background noise.
  3. Legacy and Impact – The most enduring artworks (think Guernica, The Treachery of Images, or The Persistence of Memory) aren’t just beautiful—they challenge, provoke, and endure.

How Armitage’s Work Exemplifies This Mindset

Armitage’s The World Is Not Enough (2018) is a multi-figured, chaotic composition that blends historical figures, mythological elements, and modern anxieties. The painting doesn’t just show a scene—it critiques the idea of progress itself, asking: What have we gained, and what have we lost?

This isn’t just aesthetic decision-making; it’s ethical storytelling. Every brushstroke, every color choice, every compositional element serves a purpose beyond decoration.

8 Actionable Strategies to Infuse Your Painting with Responsibility

If you want your paintings to carry the same weight and meaning as Armitage’s, you need a structured approach. Below are eight practical strategies to help you create work that resonates, challenges, and endures.

1. Research Before You Paint: Know Your Subject Inside Out

Armitage’s work often draws from history, politics, and personal memory. Before starting a new piece, he immerses himself in research—reading books, watching documentaries, and engaging with primary sources.

How to apply this:

  • For historical themes: Study archives, interviews, and firsthand accounts. If you’re painting a war scene, speak to veterans or historians.
  • For social issues: Read reports from NGOs, attend lectures, and follow journalists covering the topic.
  • For personal narratives: Journal your own experiences. Armitage often draws from childhood memories, which he transforms into symbolic, surreal landscapes.

Example: If you’re painting about climate change, don’t just depict melting ice caps—interview scientists, visit affected communities, and study indigenous perspectives to ensure your work isn’t just visually striking but also accurate and respectful.

2. Develop a Personal Symbolism System

Armitage’s paintings are rich with recurring motifs—faces in the crowd, broken objects, and surreal landscapes—each carrying specific meanings. He doesn’t rely on literal representation; instead, he uses symbolism to convey complex ideas.

How to apply this:

  • Create a personal lexicon of symbols. For example:
    • A broken clock = the illusion of time
    • A crowd of faceless figures = collective unconscious
    • A single hand = human agency
  • Document your symbols in a sketchbook so you can consistently build narratives across your work.
  • Test your symbols with viewers—ask friends or gallery-goers what they interpret from your pieces.

Example: If you’re exploring loneliness in urban life, you might use:

  • Empty subway cars = isolation
  • Reflections in windows = self-perception
  • Flickering streetlights = fleeting connection

3. Engage with Multiple Artistic Influences (But Make Them Your Own)

Armitage’s work is heavily influenced by Renaissance masters, African art, and modern surrealism, but he transforms these influences into something uniquely his own. He doesn’t copy—he dialogues with history.

How to apply this:

  • Study the greats (Picasso, Bacon, Basquiat, etc.), but ask: How would I reinterpret this?
  • Visit museums and galleries—not just to admire, but to steal techniques and then subvert them.
  • Mix genres: Combine realism with abstraction, history with fantasy, or political commentary with personal memoir.

Example: If you admire Caravaggio’s dramatic lighting, don’t just paint with chiaroscuro—use it to highlight a modern social issue, like police brutality, where shadows represent hidden truths.

4. Use Composition to Guide the Viewer’s Emotional Journey

Armitage’s paintings are deliberately disorienting—figures overlap, perspectives shift, and the eye is forced to move through the scene. This isn’t accidental; it’s a narrative tool.

How to apply this:

  • Plan your composition like a story arc:
    • Beginning (top-left): Introduce the main idea.
    • Middle (center): The emotional core (conflict, revelation).
    • End (bottom-right): The resolution or lingering question.
  • Use leading lines (roads, rivers, gazes) to direct the viewer’s path.
  • Create tension—don’t let the eye rest too easily. Leave some ambiguity.

Example: In a painting about memory and loss, you might:

  • Start with a familiar childhood home (narrative hook).
  • Move to a crowded, surreal marketplace (emotional core—confusion, nostalgia).
  • End with a single, fading window (resolution—what’s left behind).

5. Incorporate Real-World Textures and Materials

Armitage often mixes traditional painting techniques with found objects (fabric, metal, even charred wood), adding physical and symbolic weight to his work.

How to apply this:

  • Experiment with mixed media:
    • Collage (newspaper clippings, ticket stubs).
    • Embedded objects (buttons, coins, small sculptures).
    • Unconventional surfaces (canvas stretched over wood, paper layered like a map).
  • Use texture to enhance meaning:
    • Smooth surfaces = control, order.
    • Rough, impasto strokes = chaos, struggle.
    • Translucent glazes = memory, fading.

Example: If you’re painting about urban decay, you could:

  • Use charcoal and oil paint for a gritty, industrial feel.
  • Embed small pieces of rusted metal to represent corrosion of society.
  • Layer thin, watery washes to suggest erosion over time.

6. Challenge Yourself with Constraints

Armitage often works within self-imposed limitations—such as painting only with certain colors or restricting his palette to 10 hues. This forces creative innovation and prevents visual clichés.

How to apply this:

  • Pick a constraint and run with it:
    • "No black in my next series" (forces you to use shadows through color mixing).
    • "Only figures, no backgrounds" (makes the viewer focus on humanity).
    • "No straight lines" (encourages organic, surreal forms).
  • Document the process—constraints often lead to unexpected breakthroughs.

Example: If you’re exploring isolation, you might:

  • Limit your palette to blues and grays (to evoke cold, emptiness).
  • Avoid painting any faces (to represent facelessness in modern life).
  • Use only horizontal strokes (to suggest stagnation).

7. Collaborate and Seek Feedback (But Stay True to Your Vision)

Armitage has worked with poets, musicians, and activists, using cross-disciplinary collaboration to deepen his work. However, he never loses his artistic voice—feedback is a tool, not a dictator.

How to apply this:

  • Find collaborators who challenge your perspective:
    • A poet to help with lyrical themes.
    • A musician to explore sound as a visual metaphor.
    • An activist to ground your work in real-world issues.
  • Ask targeted questions:
    • "Does this composition feel balanced, or does one element dominate?"
    • "What emotions does this color combination evoke?"
    • "Does this symbol work, or is it too abstract?"
  • Don’t let feedback stifle your vision—use it to refine, not rewrite.

Example: If you’re painting about immigration, you might:

  • Collaborate with an immigrant storyteller to ground your themes in real experiences.
  • Discuss with a sociologist to understand systemic barriers.
  • Show early sketches to a diverse group to test how different viewers interpret your work.

8. Consider the Viewer’s Perspective: How Will This Be Seen?

Armitage’s paintings are designed to be experienced, not just admired. He thinks about:

  • How the painting will look in a gallery (lighting, framing).
  • How different viewers will interpret it (a child vs. a historian).
  • How the work will age (will colors fade? Will symbols remain relevant?).

How to apply this:

  • Test your work in different settings:
    • Hang it in a small, dim room (how does it feel intimate?).
    • Display it in a bright, public space (does it hold attention?).
  • Ask viewers open-ended questions:
    • "What do you notice first?"
    • "How does this make you feel?"
    • "What questions does this painting raise for you?"
  • Plan for longevity:
    • Use archival-quality materials (acid-free paper, UV-resistant varnish).
    • Document your process so future viewers understand your intent.

Example: If you’re creating a series about environmental destruction, you might:

  • Test the colors in natural light (some hues may look different outside).
  • Ask ecologists how they’d describe the emotional impact of your work.
  • Consider framing that allows future conservationists to engage with the piece.

Real-World Examples: Artists Who Approach Painting with Responsibility

To further illustrate how intentional, responsible painting works in practice, let’s examine three contemporary artists who embody this mindset—each in a different way.

1. Julie Mehretu: Mapping History and Power

Why she matters: Mehretu’s large-scale, layered paintings depict cities as sites of conflict, migration, and systemic change. Unlike traditional landscape painting, her work doesn’t just show a place—it tells a story of power.

Key Techniques:

  • Overlapping maps and architectural plans to represent historical layers.
  • Dynamic, chaotic brushwork to convey urban energy and instability.
  • Use of color to signal different eras and cultures.

How to Apply Her Approach:

  • If you’re painting about urban gentrification, use sketches of old buildings layered over new construction plans.
  • Vary your brushstrokes—thick, bold lines for forced displacement, faint, fading marks for erased history.

2. Kara Walker: History as a Site of Contested Memory

Why she matters: Walker’s silhouette-based works reconstruct and critique American history, particularly racial violence and slavery. Her art is not just visual—it’s a form of historical interrogation.

Key Techniques:

  • Silhouettes to abstract and universalize painful historical moments.
  • Text integration (quotations, newspaper clippings) to ground the imagery in reality.
  • Surreal, theatrical compositions to challenge the viewer’s comfort.

How to Apply Her Approach:

  • If you’re exploring colonialism, use black-and-white silhouettes to avoid romanticizing the past.
  • Embed historical texts (excerpts from diaries, laws) into your work.
  • Create a "stage"—position figures as if in a play, forcing the viewer to confront the drama.

3. Julie Curtiss: Personal Narrative as Political Act

Why she matters: Curtiss’s intimate, autobiographical paintings explore gender, trauma, and identity through delicate, layered techniques. Her work shows how personal storytelling can be universally resonant.

Key Techniques:

  • Mixed media (ink, watercolor, collage) to layer memories.
  • Fragile, almost "imperfect" execution to mirror emotional vulnerability.
  • Recurring motifs (hands, doors, windows) to symbolize transition and confinement.

How to Apply Her Approach:

  • If you’re painting about mental health, use faint, smudged lines to represent memory’s fragility.
  • Incorporate personal objects (a childhood toy, a letter) to anchor the work in reality.
