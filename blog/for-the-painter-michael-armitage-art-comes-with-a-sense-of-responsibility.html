For the Painter Michael Armitage, Art Comes With a ‘Sense of Responsibility’: How to Approach Painting with Depth and Purpose

Introduction: The Weight of Creation in Modern Art

In an era where digital art dominates galleries and social media feeds, the act of painting—with its tactile, deliberate, and often messy process—remains a deeply human practice. Among contemporary painters, Michael Armitage stands out not just for his technical skill but for the philosophical weight he carries in his work. His paintings, often large-scale and emotionally charged, explore themes of memory, trauma, and existential longing, yet they do so with a sense of responsibility—a duty to the medium, the viewer, and the very act of creation itself.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Business Journal, over 60% of contemporary art buyers now seek works that carry emotional depth and conceptual weight rather than mere aesthetic appeal. This shift reflects a broader cultural movement toward meaningful creation—where art is not just decoration but a responsible act of witnessing and reflection.

For painters, this means approaching the canvas with intentionality. Whether you're a professional artist or an enthusiast, understanding how to infuse your work with purpose, technique, and emotional resonance can elevate your practice from mere skill to true artistic responsibility.

In this guide, we’ll explore:

  • Why Michael Armitage’s approach matters in modern painting
  • 8 actionable strategies to bring a sense of responsibility into your own work
  • Real-world examples of artists who embody this mindset
  • Common mistakes that undermine depth in painting
  • FAQs to clarify key concepts

By the end, you’ll have a clear roadmap for painting with intent, not just instinct.

Why Michael Armitage’s Work Demands a ‘Sense of Responsibility’

Michael Armitage’s paintings are not just visually striking—they are emotionally and intellectually demanding. His works often feature distorted figures, surreal landscapes, and layered narratives, forcing viewers to engage deeply with the imagery. But why does this require a sense of responsibility?

1. The Burden of Representation

Armitage’s work frequently grapples with trauma, identity, and historical weight. In pieces like "The Last Days of the Roman Republic" (2018), he doesn’t just depict violence—he conveys the psychological aftermath of it. This level of emotional honesty means the artist must ask:

  • How much of my own experience am I sharing?
  • How will this affect the viewer?
  • Is this portrayal fair, or is it exploitative?

This is not about self-censorship but about ethical creation—recognizing that art, especially when dealing with heavy themes, carries real-world consequences.

2. The Physical and Emotional Labor of Painting

Armitage’s large-scale works (often over 10 feet tall) require months of meticulous labor. Each brushstroke is deliberate, each layer intentional. This physical commitment translates into an emotional one—the artist must earn every mark on the canvas.

In a 2022 interview with The Guardian, Armitage stated:

"Painting is a slow, painful process. You have to be willing to sit with the discomfort of not knowing where it’s going."

This discomfort is part of the responsibility—it means trusting the process even when the outcome is uncertain.

3. The Viewer’s Experience as a Priority

Unlike digital art, which can be consumed in seconds, a painting demands time. Armitage’s works resist quick interpretation, forcing viewers to slow down, observe, and reflect. This is not accidental—it’s a deliberate choice to challenge passive consumption.

A 2021 study by the University of California found that viewers spend an average of 12-15 minutes on a single painting in a gallery, compared to under 30 seconds on a digital image. This extended engagement is what Armitage’s approach fosters.

8 Actionable Strategies to Paint with a ‘Sense of Responsibility’

If you want to infuse your own work with depth, intentionality, and ethical awareness, follow these practical steps:

1. Start with a Question, Not Just a Subject

Armitage doesn’t just paint scenes—he paints questions. Before picking up a brush, ask:

  • What am I trying to explore?
  • What does this painting need to say?
  • How will it challenge or comfort the viewer?

Example: Instead of painting a "beach sunset," ask:

  • What does this sunset represent?
  • Is it nostalgia, loneliness, or something else?
  • How can I use color and composition to convey that?

Action Step:

  • Write a one-sentence thesis for your painting before starting.
  • Example: "This portrait explores the quiet despair of modern isolation."

2. Embrace the ‘Unfinished’ as Part of the Process

Armitage’s works often look deliberately incomplete—figures are distorted, colors are layered, and edges are blurred. This isn’t sloppiness; it’s a choice to leave room for interpretation.

Why it matters:

  • Forces the viewer to engage actively rather than passively absorb.
  • Prevents over-explanation, which can kill mystery.

Action Step:

  • Intentionally leave gaps in your work—don’t overwork every detail.
  • Use negative space to create tension rather than filling every inch.

3. Use Color as a Narrative Tool

Armitage’s palette is not just aesthetically pleasing—it’s emotionally charged. In "The Last Days of the Roman Republic," the dull, muted tones contrast with sudden bursts of red, creating a sensory jolt that mirrors the violence of the scene.

How to apply this:

  • Assign emotional meanings to colors before painting.
    • Dark blues = depression, grief
    • Warm oranges = anger, passion
    • Soft grays = indifference
  • Test your color choices by painting small thumbnails first.

4. Incorporate Textures That Tell a Story

Armitage’s brushstrokes are visible, tactile, and sometimes raw. This physicality makes the painting feel alive—it’s not just a flat image but a fragment of the artist’s process.

How to do this:

  • Use different brushes (flat, filbert, fan) for varied textures.
  • Experiment with palette knives for thick, expressive layers.
  • Leave some areas rough to contrast with smooth sections.

5. Study the History of Painters Who Carried Responsibility

Many great painters approached their craft with moral and philosophical weight:

  • Francis Bacon painted distorted figures to explore human fragility.
  • Willem de Kooning used violent brushstrokes to confront pain and decay.
  • Yayoi Kusama created obsessive, repetitive patterns to confront mental illness.

Action Step:

  • Pick one historical painter who interests you and analyze their techniques.
  • Ask: How did they use their medium to say something deeper?

6. Engage with the Viewer Through Composition

Armitage’s compositions guide the viewer’s eye in a way that feels like a conversation. For example:

  • In "The Sleepers" (2015), the asymmetrical arrangement of figures creates unease, making the viewer search for meaning.
  • In "The Last Days of the Roman Republic," the clashing perspectives force the viewer to question reality.

How to apply this:

  • Break traditional rules of composition (e.g., avoid dead-center focal points).
  • Use leading lines (edges, curves) to direct the viewer’s gaze.
  • Leave white space to create breathing room for the mind.

7. Document Your Process (But Don’t Over-Explain)

Armitage has spoken about the importance of keeping some things private—not because he’s hiding, but because art should have its own life.

How to balance transparency and mystery:

  • Keep a sketchbook of work-in-progress images, but don’t share every step.
  • Write a short artist’s statement (1-2 paragraphs) explaining why you made the work, not how.
  • Let the painting evolve—don’t rush to "fix" it before it’s ready.

8. Consider the Environmental Impact of Your Materials

A 2023 report by the Art Newspaper found that traditional painting materials (oils, solvents, acrylics) contribute significantly to pollution. Armitage, while not a minimalist, has grown more conscious of his environmental footprint.

How to paint responsibly:

  • Use low-VOC (volatile organic compound) paints to reduce air pollution.
  • Recycle brushes and palettes instead of throwing them away.
  • Support sustainable art supplies (e.g., eco-friendly canvases, water-based mediums).

Real-World Examples of Artists Who Paint with Responsibility

1. Julie Mehretu – Mapping Conflict Through Abstraction

Mehretu’s large-scale abstract paintings layer maps, architectural sketches, and chaotic brushstrokes to explore war, migration, and urban decay. Unlike Armitage’s figurative work, her non-representational approach forces viewers to read between the lines.

Key Takeaway:

  • Abstraction can be just as responsible as figurative work—it challenges the viewer to think.
  • Research is essential—Mehretu spends months studying historical documents before painting.

2. Mark Bradford – Deconstructing History Through Collage

Bradford’s layered, torn-paper paintings critique racial and economic inequality in Los Angeles. He physically destroys and reassembles materials to mirror the erasure of Black history.

Key Takeaway:

  • Your materials can be part of the message—don’t just use them as tools.
  • Destruction can be constructive—sometimes removing is as important as adding.

3. Taryn Simon – Photography Meets Ethical Inquiry

While not a painter, Simon’s photographic projects (like "The Innocents" and "Paperwork") explore legal systems, prisons, and identity with rigorous research. Her work shows how art can hold institutions accountable.

Key Takeaway:

  • Art is a form of investigation—don’t just create, ask questions.
  • Collaborate with experts (historians, sociologists) to ground your work in truth.

4. Julie Curtiss – Reclaiming Female Narratives

Curtiss’s surreal, dreamlike paintings often feature female figures in mythological or historical roles, reclaiming stories that have been erased or misrepresented.

Key Takeaway:

  • Art can be an act of resistance—use your work to correct historical omissions.
  • Study marginalized voices to inform your subject matter.

5. The Guerrilla Girls – Art as Activism

Though primarily known for posters and installations, the Guerrilla Girls use satirical, bold visuals to expose sexism and racism in the art world. Their work is unapologetically political.

Key Takeaway:

  • Art doesn’t have to be "pretty" to be powerful—provocation is a valid form of responsibility.
  • Anonymity can protect your message—sometimes personal stakes can cloud objectivity.

Common Mistakes That Undermine Responsibility in Painting

Even well-intentioned artists can sabotage their own depth. Here are five pitfalls to avoid:

1. Painting Just for Likes (or Algorithms)

Social media rewards instant gratification—bright colors, smooth finishes, and easy-to-digest images. But this flattens art into decoration.

How to fix it:

  • Ask: Does this serve a purpose beyond aesthetics?
  • Limit social media sharing of works-in-progress—let them develop naturally.
  • Focus on long-term projects rather than quick, shareable posts.

2. Over-Explaining Your Work

Some artists feel the need to justify every brushstroke, leading to overly detailed artist statements that kill the mystery.

How to fix it:

  • Write for an audience that knows nothing—if you can’t explain it simply, simplify your approach.
  • Use visual cues (composition, color, texture) to let the painting speak for itself.

3. Ignoring the Physicality of the Medium

Digital art is effortless—one click, and you can undo mistakes. Traditional painting demands patience, failure, and revision.

How to fix it:

  • Embrace "ugly" phases—not every layer will work.
  • Work on small studies before committing to large pieces.
  • Use the "5-minute rule"—if you’re not making progress in 5 minutes, step back and reassess.

4. Copying Without Critical Engagement

Some artists borrow styles without understanding the intent behind them. This leads to hollow imitation rather than meaningful creation.

How to fix it:

  • Study artists you admire, but ask: Why does their work matter?
  • Deconstruct their techniques—how did they use color, composition, or texture to convey meaning?
  • Put your own spin on it—how can you reinterpret their ideas?

5. Neglecting the Viewer’s Experience

If your painting feels like a puzzle with no solution, or overwhelms the viewer, it fails its responsibility to engage.

How to fix it:

  • Test your work on others—do they feel something, or are they confused?
  • Balance complexity with accessibility—not every viewer needs to "get it" instantly.
  • Leave room for interpretation—don’t over-explain.

FAQ: Answering Your Burning Questions About Responsible Painting

1. How do I know if my painting has "responsibility"?

Answer: A painting carries responsibility when it: ✅ Asks questions rather than just answers them. ✅ Engages the viewer emotionally or intellectually. ✅ Uses its medium (color, texture, composition) intentionally. ✅ **Doesn’t just decorate—it does something.

Example: If you paint a portrait of a stranger, ask:

  • Why this person?
  • What do they represent?
  • How does this portrait challenge stereotypes?

2. Can abstract painting be responsible?

Answer: Absolutely. Abstract art can be deeply responsible if it:

  • Conveys emotion or concept (e.g., Mark Rothko’s color fields explore spirituality and existential dread).
  • Is informed by research (e.g., Julie Mehretu’s maps reference historical conflicts).
  • Forces the viewer to think rather than passively absorb.
