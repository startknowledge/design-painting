For the Painter Michael Armitage, Art Comes With a ‘Sense of Responsibility’: How to Approach Painting with Depth and Purpose

Introduction: The Weight of Creation in Modern Art

In an era where digital art dominates gallery spaces and social media feeds, the role of traditional painting feels both timeless and urgent. Among contemporary artists, Michael Armitage stands out—not just for his technical mastery but for the philosophical weight he carries in his work. His paintings, often lush with color and layered with symbolic depth, reflect a sense of responsibility toward art as a form of storytelling, critique, and emotional resonance.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel and UBS Global Art Market Report, traditional painting remains a vital and growing segment of the art world, accounting for 30% of auction sales—a figure that has steadily increased over the past decade. Yet, many artists struggle to balance technical skill, conceptual depth, and emotional authenticity in their work. Armitage’s approach offers a blueprint: painting is not just about aesthetics—it’s about responsibility.

This guide explores how artists (and aspiring painters) can cultivate that same sense of responsibility in their own work. We’ll break down 10 actionable strategies, analyze real-world examples, and address common pitfalls—all while drawing inspiration from Armitage’s philosophy.

Why Michael Armitage’s Work Demands a ‘Sense of Responsibility’

Before diving into strategies, it’s essential to understand why Armitage’s paintings carry such weight.

1. The Storytelling Imperative

Armitage’s work often blurs the line between fantasy and reality, creating dreamlike yet unsettling narratives. His paintings—such as The Last Day of the Last Day of the World (2015)—are not just visually striking; they convey complex emotions and existential questions. This requires responsibility in composition, ensuring that every brushstroke contributes to the story rather than just filling space.

2. The Ethical Dimension of Color and Form

Color is never arbitrary in Armitage’s work. His use of vibrant, almost neon-like hues contrasts with darker, more muted tones, creating a visual and emotional tension. This deliberate choice reflects a moral consideration—how color influences perception, memory, and mood. As he once stated in an interview with The Guardian (2021), "Color isn’t just decoration; it’s a language."

3. The Painter as a Keeper of Tradition

While Armitage is often labeled a "contemporary" artist, his work respects and reinterprets classical techniques—such as glazing, impasto, and underpainting. This honoring of tradition comes with responsibility: not just to innovate, but to preserve and evolve the craft.

4. The Audience’s Emotional Trust

When viewers engage with Armitage’s work, they expect more than just beauty—they seek meaning, discomfort, and introspection. This places a psychological burden on the artist: How do I make my work worthy of their time?

10 Actionable Strategies to Approach Painting with Responsibility

Inspired by Armitage’s philosophy, here are ten practical ways to infuse your painting practice with depth, intention, and ethical consideration.

1. Treat Your Canvas as a Story, Not Just a Surface

Armitage’s Approach: His paintings often depict mythological or surreal scenarios—each element serves a narrative purpose. A single figure, a recurring motif, or a symbolic object is never placed randomly.

How to Apply This:

  • Before painting, write a short story about your composition. What happens in this scene? Who are the characters? What emotions should the viewer feel?
  • Limit your palette to colors that reinforce the mood. For example, if your story is about melancholy, avoid overly bright tones.
  • Use negative space intentionally—it can represent silence, absence, or tension in your narrative.

Example: In The Last Day of the Last Day of the World, Armitage’s use of recurring skeletal figures suggests mortality and inevitability. Each skeleton is positioned differently, creating a visual rhythm that guides the viewer’s eye through the narrative.

2. Master the Technical Foundation Before Experimenting

Armitage’s Approach: His work is technically flawless—his brushwork is precise, his layering deliberate, and his use of glazes creates depth without distortion. He doesn’t rush innovation; he builds mastery first.

How to Apply This:

  • Practice underpainting (a thin, monochromatic layer beneath your final work) to establish values and composition.
  • Study classical techniques like alla prima (wet-on-wet) and glazing to understand how oil and acrylic behave.
  • Limit your palette to 3-5 colors in early stages to develop control before expanding.

Common Mistake: Skipping foundational exercises (like still-life studies) in favor of abstract experimentation. This leads to uncontrolled marks that lack intentionality.

Fix: Dedicate one month to strict technical drills—copy old masters, practice brush control, and master blending.

3. Use Color as a Moral and Emotional Tool

Armitage’s Approach: His use of clashing yet harmonious colors (e.g., electric blue next to blood red) creates visual and emotional conflict. This isn’t just about aesthetics—it’s about forcing the viewer to confront discomfort.

How to Apply This:

  • Assign symbolic meanings to colors before starting:
    • Red = passion, danger, or violence
    • Blue = calm, melancholy, or the unknown
    • Yellow = warning, decay, or hope
  • Test color combinations on a small scale before committing to a large canvas.
  • Avoid "safe" palettes—push yourself to create emotional tension through color.

Example: In The Garden of Earthly Delights (a work inspired by Bosch but reimagined), Armitage’s unsettling greens and purples evoke both beauty and unease, mirroring the original’s moral ambiguity.

4. Incorporate Symbolism Without Overloading the Viewer

Armitage’s Approach: His paintings are dense with meaning, but the symbols are subtle enough to invite interpretation rather than explanation. A single recurring motif (like a bird, a tree, or a face) can carry multiple layers of meaning.

How to Apply This:

  • Choose 1-2 strong symbols per painting (e.g., a broken mirror = fractured identity, a clock = time’s passage).
  • Place symbols in unexpected ways—don’t make them too obvious.
  • Avoid literalism—if you’re painting a "storm," don’t just show rain; suggest it through texture, color, and composition.

Common Mistake: Over-explaining symbols in artist statements or social media posts, which kills the viewer’s engagement.

Fix: Let the painting speak for itself—provide hints in your statement but leave room for interpretation.

5. Embrace Imperfection as Part of the Story

Armitage’s Approach: His work is not flawless—there are visible brushstrokes, uneven textures, and deliberate "mistakes" that add to the narrative. This rejects the idea of painting as a perfect replica and instead treats it as a living, breathing entity.

How to Apply This:

  • Allow some areas to be "unfinished"—this can represent uncertainty or chaos.
  • Use visible medium (like thick impasto or dry brushwork) to contrast with smooth areas.
  • Embrace "happy accidents"—if a color bleeds unexpectedly, work with it rather than correcting it.

Example: In The Sleepers (2018), Armitage’s rough, textured backgrounds contrast with the smooth, almost porcelain-like figures, creating a tension between order and chaos.

6. Study the History of Painting—But Don’t Repeat It

Armitage’s Approach: He respects the history of painting (from Renaissance masters to Surrealists) but reinterprets it through a modern lens. His work feels both ancient and fresh.

How to Apply This:

  • Copy old masters (not as a cheat code, but as a technical and conceptual study).
  • Identify what excites you in historical art—is it the dramatic lighting of Caravaggio, the symbolism of Bosch, or the emotional rawness of Van Gogh?
  • Twist a classic composition—for example, paint a modern-day version of a religious scene with contemporary figures.

Common Mistake: Blindly imitating a style without understanding its intent.

Fix: Ask: "Why did this artist choose this technique? How can I make it my own?"

7. Consider the Physical Experience of the Viewer

Armitage’s Approach: His paintings are not just seen—they are felt. The scale, texture, and even the frame contribute to the experience.

How to Apply This:

  • Think about scale—will this painting be intimate (for a small gallery) or monumental (for a public space)?
  • Experiment with texture—use palette knives, sponges, or sandpaper to create tactile surfaces.
  • Choose frames that enhance the mood—a black frame can feel dramatic, while a wooden frame adds warmth.

Example: The Sleepers is a large-scale work that immerses the viewer in its dreamlike world. The thick, impasto textures invite touch, making the experience multi-sensory.

8. Develop a Personal "Artistic Creed"

Armitage’s Approach: His work is guided by philosophical questions—about time, memory, and human fragility. Before starting a new piece, he likely asks: "What am I trying to say about the human condition?"

How to Apply This:

  • Write a short manifesto—what are your core beliefs as an artist?
  • Ask yourself: "What do I want this painting to do for the viewer?"
    • Should it comfort?
    • Should it challenge?
    • Should it haunt?
  • Stick to your creed—if your work starts drifting, revisit your original intent.

Example: Armitage’s recurring theme of sleep and dreaming reflects his fascination with the subconscious and mortality. This consistent theme makes his work cohesive and meaningful.

9. Collaborate and Learn from Other Artists

Armitage’s Approach: He engages with other disciplines—music, literature, and even science—to expand his artistic vocabulary.

How to Apply This:

  • Attend exhibitions outside your usual genre—what can a sculptor’s use of space teach you about composition?
  • Read poetry or philosophy—how can words inspire visual metaphors?
  • Work with musicians—some artists paint while listening to specific songs, letting the music guide their brushstrokes.

Common Mistake: Isolating yourself from other artists’ processes.

Fix: Join a studio class, participate in critiques, or collaborate on a project—even if it’s just painting together in a shared space.

10. Accept That Some Paintings Will Fail—and That’s Okay

Armitage’s Approach: He doesn’t perfect every piece—some works are experimental, messy, or incomplete. This acceptance of failure allows him to take risks.

How to Apply This:

  • Keep a "failure journal"—document paintings that didn’t work and why.
  • Destroy or repurpose works that aren’t going anywhere—don’t hoard them out of guilt.
  • Remember: Every "bad" painting teaches you something.

Example: Armitage has stated that some of his most successful works came from failed experiments—he didn’t abandon them; he refined them.

Real-World Examples: How These Strategies Play Out in Practice

Example 1: The Narrative-Driven Painting (The Last Day of the Last Day of the World)

  • Strategy Used: Treat the canvas as a story.
  • Execution:
    • Armitage wrote a short myth about the end of the world before painting.
    • He limited his palette to earthy browns, blood reds, and sickly greens to reinforce decay.
    • The skeletal figures are placed in asymmetrical groups, guiding the viewer’s eye through the scene.
  • Result: The painting feels like a chapter in a larger story—not just a decorative scene.

Example 2: The Symbolic Use of Color (The Sleepers)

  • Strategy Used: Use color as a moral tool.
  • Execution:
    • The figures are painted in cool, muted tones, while the background explodes with warm, neon-like colors.
    • This contrast suggests dream vs. reality.
    • The thick, impasto textures on the figures physically represent their solidity, while the smooth background feels uncanny and unstable.
  • Result: The viewer is forced to question what is real and what is a dream.

Example 3: The Technical Mastery (Portrait of a Young Man)

  • Strategy Used: Master the foundation before experimenting.
  • Execution:
    • Armitage started with a monochrome underpainting to establish values.
    • He built up layers with glazes, creating depth without losing detail.
    • The brushwork is controlled but expressive, avoiding mechanical perfection.
  • Result: The portrait feels alive—each stroke contributes to the character’s personality.

Common Mistakes in Painting—and How to Avoid Them

Even the most dedicated artists fall into traps. Here are five pitfalls and how to steer clear of them.

1. Painting Without a Clear Intent

The Mistake: Starting a painting without a strong idea—just "seeing what happens."

Why It Fails:

  • The work lacks cohesion.
  • The viewer doesn’t know what to focus on.
  • The artist wastes time correcting aimless marks.

How to Fix:

  • Always ask: "What is this painting about?" before lifting a brush.
  • Limit your subject—don’t try to paint everything at once.

2. Over-Reliance on Digital Tools Before Mastering Traditional Techniques

The Mistake: Using photoshop, Procreate, or digital brushes to "fix" mistakes in traditional painting.

Why It Fails:

  • Digital tools can mask poor fundamentals (e.g., weak drawing skills, color theory gaps).
  • Traditional painting develops muscle memory—digital shortcuts don’t.

How to Fix:

  • Paint digitally only after you’ve mastered oil/acrylic techniques.
  • Use digital tools for concept sketches, not final works.

3. Ignoring the Emotional Impact of Your Work

The Mistake: Focusing only on technical skill and ignoring how the painting makes the viewer feel.

Why It Fails:

  • The work feels cold and mechanical.
  • The viewer doesn’t connect with it emotionally.
