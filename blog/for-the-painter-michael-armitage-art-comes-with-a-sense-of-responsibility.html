For the Painter Michael Armitage, Art Comes With a ‘Sense of Responsibility’: How to Approach Painting with Depth and Purpose

Introduction: The Weight of Creation in Modern Art

In an era where digital art dominates gallery spaces and social media feeds, the role of traditional painting feels both timeless and urgent. Among contemporary artists, Michael Armitage stands out—not just for his technical mastery but for the philosophical weight he carries in his work. His paintings, often steeped in symbolism, history, and emotional resonance, reflect a deep sense of responsibility toward the act of creation itself.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Business Journal, over 60% of contemporary art buyers now seek works that carry narrative depth and cultural significance, rather than purely aesthetic appeal. This shift aligns with Armitage’s approach, where painting is not just about skill but about meaning—a perspective that can transform how artists, collectors, and enthusiasts engage with art.

This post explores why responsibility matters in painting, how Armitage’s work embodies this ethos, and practical strategies to infuse your own art with purpose. Whether you’re a seasoned painter or just beginning, understanding this mindset can elevate your work from technical exercise to profound expression.

Why Does Michael Armitage’s Art Feel Like a ‘Responsibility’?

Armitage’s paintings often depict mythological figures, historical scenes, and surreal landscapes, but what makes them resonate isn’t just their visual complexity—it’s the weight of intention behind them. His work suggests that painting is not just about personal expression but about engaging with broader human narratives.

1. The Burden of Representation

Armitage frequently revisits myths, legends, and historical events, asking: How do we represent the past without distorting it? His 2019 series "The Fall" (inspired by the biblical story) explores themes of fragility, power, and moral decay, forcing viewers to confront uncomfortable truths. This isn’t just art for art’s sake—it’s art as a mirror to society.

Real-World Example: In "The Fall of Icarus" (a reinterpretation of Pieter Bruegel’s classic), Armitage doesn’t just depict the myth—he recontextualizes it. By placing Icarus’ fall in a modern, desolate landscape, he suggests that human hubris remains unchanged, regardless of era. This layering of meaning turns a simple scene into a commentary on contemporary arrogance.

2. The Painter as Storyteller

Unlike abstract artists who prioritize emotion over narrative, Armitage’s work is literary. His compositions often feel like illustrations for an unseen novel, where every brushstroke contributes to a larger tale. This approach requires discipline, research, and a deep understanding of symbolism—qualities that set his work apart.

Key Takeaway: If you want your paintings to carry weight, treat them like stories. Before picking up a brush, ask:

  • What is the core idea I’m exploring?
  • How does this scene reflect a larger truth?
  • What emotions should the viewer feel when they step back?

3. The Ethical Dimension of Art

Armitage has spoken about the moral implications of painting, particularly in how artists interpret and reinterpret history. His 2021 work "The Garden of Earthly Delights" (a nod to Bosch) critiques consumerism and environmental destruction, using surreal imagery to highlight modern anxieties.

Why This Matters: Art is never neutral. Every choice—from color palette to composition—carries unspoken messages. If you’re painting historical or cultural subjects, acknowledge your role as a storyteller, not just a technician.

8 Actionable Strategies to Approach Painting with Responsibility

Inspired by Armitage’s philosophy, here are practical ways to infuse your own work with depth, intention, and a sense of responsibility.

1. Research Before You Paint: Know Your Subject Inside Out

Armitage’s mythological and historical works are grounded in rigorous research. Before starting a painting, spend time studying:

  • Primary sources (books, archives, firsthand accounts)
  • Secondary analysis (scholarly articles, documentaries)
  • Cultural context (how the story was received in its time vs. today)

How to Apply This:

  • If painting a biblical scene, read multiple interpretations of the text.
  • If depicting a historical event, consult eyewitness accounts alongside official records.
  • Keep a research journal to track insights that shape your composition.

Common Mistake: Relying on surface-level knowledge (e.g., watching a 10-minute YouTube video on "Medieval Warfare"). This leads to clichéd, shallow representations.

Avoid It By: ✔ Reading at least 3-5 sources before starting. ✔ Visiting museums or archives if possible. ✔ Consulting historians or experts (even via email or interviews).

2. Develop a Personal Symbolism System

Armitage’s work is rich with recurring motifs—broken wings, golden light, distorted figures—each carrying specific meanings. Instead of relying on generic symbols, create your own language.

How to Build Your Symbolism:

  • List 5-10 recurring themes in your work (e.g., decay, hope, isolation).
  • Assign visual cues to each (e.g., cracked mirrors = fractured reality).
  • Document your choices so you can reference them later.

Real-World Example: In his "The Garden" series, Armitage uses golden hues to represent both paradise and corruption. By controlling this symbol, he ensures the viewer feels the tension between beauty and decay.

Common Mistake: Using symbols without intention (e.g., painting a skull because it’s "dark" without deeper meaning).

Avoid It By: ✔ Writing a short manifesto for your symbols. ✔ Testing meanings in sketches before committing to a final piece. ✔ Avoiding overused tropes (e.g., always using a black cat for "bad luck").

3. Embrace the ‘Unfinished’ as a Concept

Armitage’s paintings often have deliberate gaps—unfinished edges, blurred details—which invite the viewer to fill in the blanks. This isn’t sloppiness; it’s a conscious choice to leave room for interpretation.

How to Apply This:

  • Leave some areas intentionally rough (e.g., a sky that’s not fully painted).
  • Use abstraction strategically—don’t overwork every detail.
  • Consider the "white space" of the canvas as part of your composition.

Real-World Example: In "The Sleepers" (2020), Armitage’s figures are partially obscured, making the viewer question: Are they sleeping? Dreaming? Dead? This ambiguity forces a deeper engagement with the work.

Common Mistake: Overworking every inch of the canvas, leaving no room for the viewer’s imagination.

Avoid It By: ✔ Planning "negative space" in your sketches. ✔ Using underpainting techniques to build layers without over-detailing. ✔ Stepping back frequently to see where less might be more.

4. Use Color as a Narrative Tool

Armitage’s palette is deliberate and symbolic. For example:

  • Gold = divinity, corruption, wealth.
  • Dark blues = melancholy, the unknown.
  • Neon accents = artificiality, modern anxiety.

How to Master Color Symbolism:

  • Create a color palette guide for each series.
  • Test colors in thumbnails before committing to a full painting.
  • Research historical color meanings (e.g., in medieval art, purple was royalty).

Real-World Example: In "The Feast" (2018), the overwhelming reds and greens create a sense of decadence and decay, reinforcing the theme of excess leading to ruin.

Common Mistake: Choosing colors based on personal preference rather than intentional meaning.

Avoid It By: ✔ Limiting your palette to 3-5 key colors per piece. ✔ Using color theory to evoke specific emotions (e.g., warm tones = energy, cool tones = calm). ✔ Avoiding "safe" colors (e.g., always using blue for "peace")—push yourself to find unexpected associations.

5. Incorporate Historical and Cultural Layers

Armitage doesn’t just paint about history—he recontextualizes it. His "The Fall" series, for example, doesn’t just depict a myth; it compares it to modern failures.

How to Add Historical Depth:

  • Layer references (e.g., paint a Renaissance scene but use modern lighting).
  • Contrast eras (e.g., a medieval knight in a futuristic cityscape).
  • Use archival images as inspiration (but don’t copy—transform).

Real-World Example: In "The Last Supper" (2022), Armitage’s figures are distorted and fragmented, suggesting that even sacred stories are open to interpretation. This makes the painting both familiar and unsettling.

Common Mistake: Treating historical subjects as decorative rather than meaningful.

Avoid It By: ✔ Asking "Why this story?" before starting. ✔ Avoiding anachronisms (unless intentional). ✔ Studying how other artists (e.g., Caravaggio, Goya) handled similar themes.

6. Let Your Process Be Part of the Story

Armitage’s paintings often show visible brushstrokes, layers of paint, and even "mistakes"—these aren’t flaws; they’re evidence of the creative process.

How to Make Your Process Visible:

  • Use glazing techniques to show layers.
  • Leave some underpainting exposed.
  • Document your process in sketches or time-lapse videos.

Real-World Example: In "The Garden of Earthly Delights" (2021), the textured surfaces suggest a painting that was built over time, much like the myths it references.

Common Mistake: Over-polishing the surface, erasing all traces of the creative journey.

Avoid It By: ✔ Embracing "happy accidents"—sometimes a smudge becomes part of the meaning. ✔ Using different brushes/textures to show the evolution of the work. ✔ Leaving some areas "raw" to contrast with finished details.

7. Engage with the Viewer’s Emotions (Not Just Their Eyes)

Armitage’s work doesn’t just look impressive—it feels something. A viewer shouldn’t just see a painting; they should experience it.

How to Evoke Emotion:

  • Study facial expressions (even in your own paintings).
  • Use lighting to guide the viewer’s gaze (e.g., a single light source creating drama).
  • Leave room for the viewer to project their own stories.

Real-World Example: In "The Sleepers" (2020), the closed eyes of the figures make the viewer wonder: Are they dreaming? Dead? Waiting? This emotional ambiguity is what makes the painting linger in the mind.

Common Mistake: Focusing only on technical skill without considering emotional impact.

Avoid It By: ✔ Asking friends what they feel when they look at your work. ✔ Using contrast (light vs. dark, sharp vs. soft) to create tension. ✔ Avoiding "pretty" paintings—sometimes uncomfortable emotions create the most powerful work.

8. Consider the Physical and Ethical Weight of Your Materials

Armitage has spoken about the environmental and ethical implications of traditional painting. From toxic pigments to exploitative labor in paint production, the materials we use carry their own stories.

How to Paint Responsibly:

  • Research non-toxic paints (e.g., eco-friendly brands like Liquitex Professional).
  • Support ethical artists (e.g., painters who use sustainable brushes).
  • Consider digital alternatives if traditional painting feels too heavy.

Real-World Example: Some contemporary artists now mix recycled materials into their paints or use plant-based binders to reduce environmental impact.

Common Mistake: Ignoring the cost of your materials—both to the planet and to your health.

Avoid It By: ✔ Choosing low-VOC (volatile organic compound) paints. ✔ Reusing palettes and brushes when possible. ✔ Educating yourself on the origins of your materials.

Common Mistakes in Painting with Responsibility (And How to Fix Them)

Even the most intention-driven artists make errors. Here are five pitfalls and how to avoid them:

1. Overcomplicating the Symbolism

Mistake: Trying to cram too many meanings into one painting, making it confusing rather than profound. Fix:

  • Start with one core idea and build from there.
  • Test your symbols in small sketches before committing to a large work.

2. Ignoring the Viewer’s Perspective

Mistake: Painting for yourself rather than considering how others will interpret it. Fix:

  • Show your work to strangers and ask for honest feedback.
  • Study how other artists engage with their audiences (e.g., Armitage’s use of ambiguity).

3. Neglecting the Physical Process

Mistake: Treating painting like a digital design—too clean, too controlled. Fix:

  • Embrace imperfection—sometimes a smudge tells a better story than a perfect line.
  • Experiment with textures (e.g., impasto, glazing).

4. Using History Without Context

Mistake: Painting a historical scene without understanding its cultural context. Fix:

  • Consult historians before finalizing a composition.
  • Avoid anachronisms unless they serve a deliberate purpose.

5. Forgetting the Ethical Weight of Materials

Mistake: Using toxic or unsustainable materials without consideration. Fix:

  • Research eco-friendly alternatives.
  • Support brands that prioritize ethical production.
