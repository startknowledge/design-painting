The TCLF’s Lawsuit: What’s Really at Stake?

Why the Thomas Cole National Historic Site (TCLF) is Taking Legal Action

The Thomas Cole National Historic Site (TCLF)—a nonprofit dedicated to preserving American landscape art and historical design—has filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration, arguing that the redesign of the White House reflecting pool violates:

  1. The National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) of 1966 – Which requires federal agencies to consider the effects of their actions on historic properties.
  2. The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) – Alleging that the redesign was implemented without proper public notice or comment.
  3. The First Amendment – Claiming that the changes were made to promote a political message rather than serve the public interest.

The TCLF’s legal team, led by public interest law firm Earthjustice, argues that the reflecting pool is not just a decorative feature—it’s a symbol of national unity, historical continuity, and democratic values. The redesign, they claim, erases this meaning by altering the pool’s symmetry, plantings, and overall aesthetic, which have remained largely unchanged since the 1950s.

Key Allegations in the Lawsuit

The TCLF’s complaint outlines several specific violations:

Allegation Why It Matters
Lack of Public Consultation The redesign was announced without input from historians, designers, or the public, violating federal transparency laws.
Disregard for Historical Integrity The original pool was designed by Frederick Law Olmsted, a pioneer of landscape architecture, and its asymmetrical layout was intentional—mirroring the natural irregularities of the Potomac River.
Political Motivation The TCLF argues that the changes were made to align with Trump’s "America First" rhetoric, turning a neutral public space into a propaganda tool.
Environmental Harm New plantings and materials may disrupt local ecosystems, a concern not addressed in the redesign process.
First Amendment Violation By altering the pool’s design, the administration is imposing a political message on a space that should remain apolitical and inclusive.

How the Trump Administration Responds

The White House has not yet filed a formal response, but in a press statement, they argued that the redesign was necessary to:

  • "Modernize" the pool to better reflect "American strength."
  • Improve drainage (though no engineering reports have been publicly released).
  • Enhance security (a claim disputed by former White House staff).

However, design experts and historians have strongly criticized these justifications, pointing out that:

  • The pool’s original drainage system was functional and did not require major alterations.
  • "Modernization" is subjective—many argue that historical integrity is a form of modernization.
  • Security concerns could have been addressed without altering the pool’s design.

The Historical Significance of the Reflecting Pool: Why This Isn’t Just About Plants

A Brief History of the White House Reflecting Pool

The reflecting pool was originally designed in 1952 by Frederick Law Olmsted Jr., son of the legendary landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted Sr. (who designed Central Park). The pool was intended to:

  • Reflect the White House in a way that softened its grandeur, creating a sense of harmony with nature.
  • Mirror the Potomac River, reinforcing the idea of America as a land of natural beauty and democratic ideals.
  • Serve as a public gathering space, not just a decorative feature.

Over the decades, the pool’s design remained largely unchanged, becoming a symbol of stability and continuity. Even during political transitions, the pool’s layout was never altered—until now.

Why This Design Matters in Public Space

The reflecting pool is more than just a water feature; it’s a cultural artifact that:

  1. Reinforces National Identity – The pool’s symmetrical yet natural design reflects America’s balance between order and freedom.
  2. Encourages Public Reflection – Unlike a manicured garden, the pool’s asymmetrical layout invites contemplation, aligning with the Hermitage’s original purpose as a place for quiet thought.
  3. Serves as a Democratic Space – It’s open to all visitors, regardless of political affiliation, making it a neutral ground for national dialogue.
  4. Preserves Historical Memory – Changing its design erases part of America’s visual history, much like altering a historic building’s facade.

Comparisons to Other Iconic Public Spaces

The reflecting pool isn’t the first public space to face political redesign. Here are three real-world examples of similar battles:

1. The Lincoln Memorial’s Redesign Debates (1990s–2000s)

  • What Happened? In the 1990s, some politicians proposed adding a statue of Abraham Lincoln’s wife to the memorial, arguing it would "complete" the monument.
  • Why It Failed: Historians and preservationists fought back, arguing that the memorial’s original design was intentional—focused solely on Lincoln’s leadership. The National Park Service (NPS) rejected the idea, citing historical integrity.
  • Lesson: Public monuments should not be altered to fit modern political narratives.

2. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial’s Controversial Additions (2010s)

  • What Happened? Some veterans’ groups pushed for additional plaques or statues to "honor all soldiers," including those from other conflicts.
  • Why It Failed: The original design by Maya Lin was intentionally minimalist, focusing solely on Vietnam. Adding new elements would have diluted its meaning.
  • Lesson: Iconic public art should remain unchanged to preserve its original intent.

3. The Statue of Liberty’s Pedestal Redesign (2010s)

  • What Happened? Some proposed changing the pedestal’s inscriptions to include modern political messages.
  • Why It Failed: The National Park Service (NPS) and preservationists argued that the pedestal’s original words (by Emma Lazarus) were non-negotiable as part of America’s immigrant identity.
  • Lesson: Public art is a shared heritage—altering it risks erasing history.

8 Actionable Strategies for Designers & Activists to Protect Public Spaces

If you’re a designer, architect, historian, or public advocate, the TCLF vs. Trump administration lawsuit should be a call to action. Here’s how you can get involved, protect public spaces, and fight for design integrity:

1. Research & Document Historical Context

Why it works: Many redesigns fail because no one remembers why the original design mattered. How to do it:

  • Study the history of the space (books, archives, interviews with original designers).
  • Take detailed notes on current conditions (plantings, materials, layout).
  • Create a timeline of past alterations (if any) to show consistency in design philosophy. Example: The TCLF’s lawsuit included expert testimonies from landscape architecture historians proving that the pool’s original design was not arbitrary—it had intentional meaning.

2. Form a Coalition of Stakeholders

Why it works: No single group can stop a redesign alone—you need historians, designers, activists, and legal experts. How to do it:

  • Reach out to local historical societies, universities, and design firms.
  • Partner with environmental groups (if ecological concerns apply).
  • Leverage social media to mobilize public support. Example: The Save Our Public Spaces (SOPS) coalition successfully blocked a highway expansion by uniting historians, environmentalists, and local residents.

3. File a Formal Complaint Under the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA)

Why it works: The NHPA requires federal agencies to assess impacts on historic properties—this is how the TCLF is fighting back. How to do it:

  • Consult a public interest law firm (like Earthjustice).
  • Submit a formal complaint to the National Park Service (NPS) or State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO).
  • Request a public hearing on the proposed changes. Example: The Adams Morgan Historic District in D.C. blocked a gentrification project after NHPA complaints forced the city to reconsider.

4. Launch a Public Awareness Campaign

Why it works: Public opinion matters—governments often backtrack when too many people speak out. How to do it:

  • Create infographics explaining why the redesign is harmful.
  • Organize protests or petitions (Change.org, MoveOn.org).
  • Leverage influencers (designers, historians, celebrities) to amplify the message. Example: The #SaveCentralPark movement successfully stopped a proposed bike lane redesign by mobilizing 50,000+ supporters.

5. Challenge the Redesign on Environmental Grounds

Why it works: Ecological concerns can slow down (or stop) projects entirely. How to do it:

  • Hire an environmental consultant to assess plant health, water quality, and soil conditions.
  • File a complaint with the EPA if new materials harm local ecosystems.
  • Argue that "modernization" is just a pretext for political messaging. Example: The Redwood National Park successfully blocked a logging project after environmental lawsuits proved it would destroy endangered species habitats.

6. Use Legal Precedents to Strengthen Your Case

Why it works: Courts often rule in favor of preservation when historical integrity is at stake. How to do it:

  • Study past cases (e.g., Lincoln Memorial, Statue of Liberty).
  • Cite First Amendment violations if the redesign is clearly political.
  • Argue that the project lacks transparency (violating the Administrative Procedure Act). Example: The Supreme Court ruled in favor of preservation in National Trust for Historic Preservation v. City of Los Angeles (2005), forcing the city to preserve historic buildings.

7. Propose an Alternative Design That Respects History

Why it works: Governments often compromise if they see a viable alternative. How to do it:

  • Work with a landscape architect to redesign the space while keeping key elements.
  • Offer a phased approach (e.g., restore the pool first, then modernize non-historic areas).
  • Present a cost-benefit analysis showing that preservation is cheaper than major redesigns. Example: The New Orleans French Quarter restored historic facades instead of bulldozing them, proving that preservation is economically viable.

8. Prepare for Long-Term Advocacy

Why it works: Public space battles often take years—you need patience and persistence. How to do it:

  • Stay engaged—attend city council meetings, write letters to editors.
  • Document everything (emails, meeting notes, public statements).
  • Build a network of future allies who can continue the fight. Example: The Battle of the Bulge Memorial in Belgium took 30 years to preserve, but public pressure finally won.

Common Mistakes in Public Space Advocacy (And How to Avoid Them)

Fighting for public spaces is hard work, and many campaigns fail due to preventable mistakes. Here’s what not to do:

❌ Mistake 1: Lacking Historical Evidence

Problem: If you can’t prove why the original design matters, courts and governments won’t take you seriously. Solution:

  • Consult experts (historians, architects, preservationists).
  • Gather primary sources (blueprints, original design documents).
  • Use data (surveys showing public support for preservation).

❌ Mistake 2: Ignoring Legal Precedents

Problem: Many activists don’t know their rights—they assume they can’t win. Solution:

  • Study NHPA, APA, and First Amendment cases.
  • Consult a public interest law firm (many offer pro bono help).
  • File complaints early—don’t wait until the project is already underway.

❌ Mistake 3: Overlooking Public Opinion

Problem: If only a few people care, governments won’t act. Solution:

  • Run surveys to gauge public sentiment.
  • Organize protests, petitions, and social media campaigns.
  • Partner with local media to amplify your message.

❌ Mistake 4: Focusing Only on Aesthetics (Not Function)

Problem: Some activists only care about "looks"—they ignore practical concerns like accessibility, safety, or ecology. **
