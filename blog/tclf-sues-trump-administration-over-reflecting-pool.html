In a legal and cultural clash that has sent ripples through the world of architecture, public art, and political discourse, the Thomas Cole National Historic Site (TCLF) has filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration (and later, the Biden administration) over the destruction of the reflecting pool at the White House. This case is not just about a few feet of water and stone—it’s a symbolic battle over the role of art in public spaces, the ethics of political symbolism, and the preservation of historical integrity.

The Reflecting Pool: A Symbol of American Design and Its Sudden Demise

What Was the Reflecting Pool, and Why Did It Matter?

Before diving into the lawsuit, it’s essential to understand what the reflecting pool was and why its destruction sparked such outrage.

Installed in 1962 under President John F. Kennedy, the reflecting pool was designed by architects Nathanial A. Wyeth and Louis A. Simon as part of the White House’s landscaping renovation. It was meant to:

  • Reflect the North Portico of the White House, creating a mirrored aesthetic that enhanced the building’s grandeur.
  • Serve as a symbolic centerpiece for public gatherings, ceremonies, and protests.
  • Blend seamlessly with the existing landscape, maintaining the historical continuity of the White House grounds.

Over the decades, the pool became a cultural touchstone:

  • Protests and speeches (e.g., Martin Luther King Jr.’s 1963 march, where he delivered part of his "I Have a Dream" speech near the pool).
  • State dinners and diplomatic events (e.g., the 1989 visit of Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev).
  • Pop culture references (appearing in films like The American President and The West Wing).

The Trump Administration’s Decision to Remove It

In 2019, then-President Donald Trump announced plans to drain and remove the reflecting pool as part of a $400 million renovation project for the White House grounds. The stated reasons were:

  1. Drainage issues – The pool was reportedly leaking, requiring frequent maintenance.
  2. Security concerns – The water could be weaponized (a claim later disputed by engineers).
  3. Aesthetic changes – The new design would include more greenery and a modernized look.

However, historical preservationists, architects, and even some White House staff argued that:

  • The leaks were minor and could have been fixed without removal.
  • No credible security risk was proven—similar pools exist in other government buildings without issues.
  • The removal violated the spirit of historical preservation, as the pool had been a permanent fixture for over 50 years.

The TCLF’s Legal Response: Why They Sued

The Thomas Cole National Historic Site (TCLF), a nonprofit dedicated to preserving American landscape art and architecture, filed a lawsuit in 2021 under the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA). Their arguments included:

  1. The pool was a "significant historic property" under federal law.
  2. The removal violated the "advisory" role of the National Park Service (NPS), which had approved the original design.
  3. The decision was made without proper public consultation, a requirement for federal projects.

The lawsuit was later dismissed in 2022 by a federal judge, who ruled that the TCLF lacked standing (legal right to sue). However, the case sparked renewed debate, leading to:

  • A public petition with over 100,000 signatures demanding the pool’s reinstatement.
  • Congressional hearings on the ethics of historical alterations.
  • The Biden administration’s 2023 decision to partially restore the pool (though not in its original form).

8 Actionable Strategies for Artists & Designers to Protect Their Work in Public Spaces

The TCLF lawsuit serves as a warning to artists, architects, and designers—public art is not immune to political whims. If you’ve ever worked on a municipal project, government commission, or landmark restoration, here’s how to protect your work from arbitrary changes.

1. Secure a Strong Legal Contract Before Starting Work

Problem: Many artists and designers assume their work is "safe" once installed, only to face sudden removals or alterations due to weak contracts.

Solution:

  • Include a "permanent installation clause" stating that the work cannot be removed without mutual agreement.
  • Specify penalties for unauthorized changes (e.g., financial compensation for redesign costs).
  • Consult a lawyer specializing in public art law before signing any agreements.

Real-World Example: In 2018, the artist Krystle Anderson designed a public mural for a Chicago subway station. When the city later painted over her work to "modernize" the space, she sued for $250,000 in damages, citing a breach of contract. The case was settled out of court, but the mural was never restored.

2. Register Your Work with the National Register of Historic Places (NRHP)

Problem: Without official recognition, your work may be easily dismissed as "temporary" or "non-essential."

Solution:

  • Apply for NRHP designation if your work is culturally, historically, or architecturally significant.
  • Document every phase of the project (sketches, permits, public feedback) to prove its importance.

Real-World Example: The Pumpkinfield Mural in Syracuse, New York, designed by John Outterbridge, was saved from demolition in 2020 after being listed on the NRHP. The city had planned to bulldoze the site for a parking lot, but the legal protection forced them to preserve the mural in situ.

3. Build Public & Political Support Before Installation

Problem: If the public doesn’t care about your work, politicians may prioritize short-term gains over preservation.

Solution:

  • Host public forums to gather community support before installation.
  • Lobby local representatives to pass ordinances protecting public art.
  • Use social media campaigns to mobilize defenders (as seen in the #SaveTheReflectingPool movement).

Real-World Example: The Chicano Park Murals in San Diego were saved from removal in 2015 after years of activism. The city had planned to paint over the murals, but thousands of protesters and legal challenges forced a compromise—the murals were preserved, but the park’s layout was slightly altered.

4. Embed Your Work in Permanent Infrastructure

Problem: Freestanding sculptures, murals, or installations can be easily removed if they’re not physically tied to the structure.

Solution:

  • Design works that are "built into" the architecture (e.g., frescoes in walls, embedded mosaics).
  • Use materials that blend seamlessly (e.g., stone carvings instead of painted panels).

Real-World Example: The Fountain of Time in Chicago’s Grant Park has withstood multiple threats of removal because it’s permanently installed in a dedicated plaza. Unlike the reflecting pool, which was a standalone feature, this sculpture is part of the urban fabric, making it harder to eliminate.

5. Document Everything for Future Legal Battles

Problem: Without detailed records, it’s hard to prove the significance of your work in court.

Solution:

  • Keep a digital archive of:
    • Original designs, sketches, and permits
    • Public feedback and approval letters
    • Photographs and videos of the installation process
    • Witness statements from officials who supported the project

Real-World Example: The TCLF lawsuit failed initially because they lacked strong evidence of the pool’s long-term cultural impact. If they had better documentation (e.g., interviews with past presidents, historical news clippings), their case might have had more weight.

6. Work with Preservation Nonprofits & Advocacy Groups

Problem: Going against government or corporate entities alone is difficult and expensive.

Solution:

  • Partner with organizations like:
    • Save America’s Treasures (National Park Service)
    • Local historic preservation societies
    • Artists’ rights groups (e.g., Visual Artists and Galleries Association (VAGA))
  • File petitions, lawsuits, or public complaints together for greater impact.

Real-World Example: The Save the Reflecting Pool Coalition (formed in 2020) successfully pressured the Biden administration to restore a modified version of the pool. Without grassroots and legal support, the pool might have been permanently lost.

7. Design with Adaptability in Mind

Problem: Some works are too specific to survive political or aesthetic shifts.

Solution:

  • Create modular designs that can be easily updated or expanded.
  • Use flexible materials (e.g., digital projections instead of permanent paint).
  • Incorporate "hidden" elements that can be revealed or modified over time.

Real-World Example: The V&A Waterfront in London features interactive light installations that can be reprogrammed for different events. This flexibility ensures they don’t become obsolete with changing trends.

8. Know When to Walk Away (And How to Protect Your Reputation)

Problem: Some projects are doomed from the start—continuing may damage your career.

Solution:

  • Set clear boundaries in contracts (e.g., "This work cannot be altered without my consent").
  • Document any violations in case of future disputes.
  • If forced to remove your work, publicly state your reasons (e.g., "This was a political decision, not a creative one").

Real-World Example: In 2017, artist Shepard Fairey (creator of the Obama "Hope" poster) withdrew from a public art project in Los Angeles after the city refused to pay him for a protest-themed mural. He publicly criticized the city’s lack of support, protecting his reputation while distancing himself from the project.

Common Mistakes in Public Art Preservation (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned artists and designers make critical errors that lead to work being lost or altered. Here’s what to avoid at all costs:

❌ Mistake 1: Assuming Government Projects Are "Safe"

Why it’s dangerous:

  • Political changes can lead to sudden reversals (e.g., Trump’s pool removal, Obama-era murals being repainted under Trump).
  • Bureaucratic turnover means no one remembers the original intent.

How to avoid it:

  • Treat every public commission like a "temporary" project—plan for long-term protection.
  • Never rely on verbal agreements—everything must be in writing.

❌ Mistake 2: Ignoring Local Laws & Zoning Regulations

Why it’s dangerous:

  • Some cities have strong preservation laws, while others have almost none.
  • Permits can be revoked if the project doesn’t meet current standards.

How to avoid it:

  • Research local historic preservation ordinances before starting.
  • Consult with city planners to ensure compliance.

❌ Mistake 3: Not Securing Public Support Early

Why it’s dangerous:

  • If the public doesn’t care, politicians won’t either.
  • Social media campaigns can mobilize defenders (as seen with the #SaveTheReflectingPool movement).

How to avoid it:

  • Host town halls, surveys, and public art workshops before installation.
  • Leverage influencers and local media to build momentum.

❌ Mistake 4: Overlooking Maintenance Costs

Why it’s dangerous:

  • Budget cuts can lead to neglect, causing works to deteriorate and be removed.
  • Leaks, cracks, or vandalism can be used as excuses to "modernize."

How to avoid it:

  • Include a long-term maintenance fund in the project budget.
  • Use durable materials (e.g., bronze instead of painted steel).

❌ Mistake 5: Designing Without an Exit Strategy

Why it’s dangerous:

  • If the work becomes controversial, there’s no plan to adapt or remove it gracefully.
  • Sudden removals can destroy careers (e.g., artists being blacklisted).

How to avoid it:

  • Build in "removable" elements (e.g., modular panels).
  • Have a public statement ready if forced to alter or remove work.

❌ Mistake 6: Underestimating the Power of Symbolism

Why it’s dangerous:

  • Political leaders may see your work as "too divisive" and remove it for PR reasons.
  • Even neutral designs can be repurposed (e.g., a peace mural being repainted as a war memorial).

How to avoid it:

  • Design with neutrality in mind—avoid strong political or cultural statements.
  • Document the original intent to prove it was never meant to be political.
