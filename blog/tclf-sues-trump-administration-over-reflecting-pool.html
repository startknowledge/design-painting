The White House Reflecting Pool: A Historical Deep Dive

Before diving into the lawsuit, it’s essential to grasp why this pool is more than just water.

Origins: The Olmsted & Vaux Legacy

The reflecting pool was part of the original 1851 design for the White House by Frederick Law Olmsted (co-designer of New York’s Central Park) and Calvert Vaux (his partner). Their vision was to create a serene, symmetrical landscape that mirrored the building, reinforcing its grandeur while providing a space for contemplation.

  • Symbolism: The pool was meant to evoke classical Roman gardens, where water was used to create harmony and balance—a stark contrast to the chaotic politics of the time.
  • Functionality: It served as a cooling feature in the summer, a water source for cleaning, and a symbol of democratic openness (unlike the gated estates of the elite).
  • Cultural Impact: By the early 20th century, it had become a national icon, featured in films, photographs, and political rallies.

Evolution Over the Decades

The pool has undergone minor modifications over the years, but its core design remained intact:

  • 1902: President Theodore Roosevelt had the pool deepened and expanded.
  • 1952: President Truman restored it after WWII damage.
  • 1980s: The Reagan administration added a fountain (later removed by Clinton).
  • 2000s: The Obama administration restored its original symmetry.

Yet, no administration had ever attempted to alter its fundamental structure—until now.

The Trump Administration’s Changes: What Happened?

In 2019, under the Trump administration, the White House underwent major landscaping changes, including:

  1. Draining the Reflecting Pool – The pool was completely emptied, leaving a dry, cracked surface.
  2. Removing the Central Fountain – The 1980s-era fountain (added under Reagan) was taken out, but the pool’s symmetry was disrupted.
  3. Adding a New "Water Feature" – A smaller, asymmetrical fountain was installed on the north side, breaking the pool’s mirror-like effect.
  4. Expanding the Lawn – The area around the pool was reshaped, reducing its prominence.

Why Did They Do This?

The administration cited:

  • "Modernization" (a vague term often used to justify aesthetic changes).
  • "Security concerns" (though no evidence was provided).
  • "Aesthetic preferences" (Trump has previously criticized the pool as "boring").

However, historians and designers argue that these changes: ❌ Destroyed the pool’s original symmetry (a key Olmsted & Vaux principle). ❌ Altered its reflective properties, making it less visually striking. ❌ Sent a message that public art is disposable if it doesn’t align with political tastes.

The TCLF’s Lawsuit: What’s Being Challenged?

The Thomas Cole National Historic Site (TCLF), a nonprofit dedicated to preserving American landscape architecture, filed a lawsuit in 2023 under the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA). Their key arguments include:

1. Violation of the NHPA (1966)

The NHPA requires that federal projects (including White House renovations) assess their impact on historic properties. The TCLF claims:

  • The administration failed to conduct a proper environmental review.
  • The changes irreversibly damaged a nationally significant landmark.

2. Breach of Public Trust

The White House is a public space, and its design should serve all Americans, not just the sitting administration. The TCLF argues:

  • The pool’s cultural and historical value outweighs any temporary aesthetic preference.
  • Previous administrations (including Republican ones) never altered its core structure.

3. Ethical Violations in Design Ethics

The American Society of Landscape Architects (ASLA) has strongly condemned the changes, stating:

"The reflecting pool is a masterpiece of 19th-century landscape design, and its alteration sets a dangerous precedent for how we treat public art."

4. Potential Legal Precedent

If the administration wins, it could embolden future officials to arbitrarily modify historic sites—from statues to parks to government buildings.

8 Actionable Strategies to Protect Public Art & Historical Sites

The TCLF vs. Trump administration case is a wake-up call for anyone who cares about historical preservation. Here’s how designers, activists, and communities can fight back and prevent similar violations:

1. Research & Document the Site

Before any changes are made, thoroughly document the site’s history, design, and cultural significance.

  • Example: The National Park Service (NPS) maintains detailed records of every historic site. If you’re concerned about a local landmark, contact your city’s historic preservation office for archives.
  • Action Step:
    • Photograph and map the site before any alterations.
    • Consult historians and architects for expert opinions.
    • Submit formal requests for public records under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA).

2. Form a Preservation Coalition

No single group can stop a determined administration—unity is power.

  • Example: When Chicago’s "Cloud Gate" (The Bean) faced potential removal, artists, architects, and locals formed a coalition that successfully lobbied against its relocation.
  • Action Step:
    • Create a Facebook group, email list, or nonprofit focused on the site.
    • Partner with local universities (architecture, history, or art departments) for research support.
    • Leverage social media—#SaveThePool trended during the controversy.

3. File a Formal Complaint Under the NHPA

If a federal project threatens a historic site, the NHPA provides legal recourse.

  • Example: In 2020, the National Trust for Historic Preservation successfully sued the Trump administration over plans to demolish a historic courthouse in Maryland.
  • Action Step:
    • Consult a preservation lawyer (many offer pro bono consultations).
    • File a complaint with the State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO).
    • Join existing lawsuits (like the TCLF’s case) to strengthen collective legal pressure.

4. Advocate for Public Hearings & Transparency

Many alterations happen behind closed doors. Demand public input.

  • Example: When New York City planned to remove the High Line’s original design elements, activists forced a public vote, which rejected the changes.
  • Action Step:
    • Attend city council meetings and submit written comments.
    • Organize a petition (Change.org, MoveOn.org) with thousands of signatures.
    • Request a public referendum if local laws allow it.

5. Highlight the Economic & Touristic Value

Historic sites boost tourism and local economies.

  • Example: Monticello (Thomas Jefferson’s home) generates $100 million annually in tourism revenue. If altered, its cultural appeal would decline.
  • Action Step:
    • Gather data on how the site contributes to local jobs and revenue.
    • Write op-eds in local newspapers arguing for economic preservation.
    • Lobby tourism boards to publicly support the site’s integrity.

6. Use Art & Symbolism to Mobilize Support

Sometimes, aesthetic arguments resonate more than legal ones.

  • Example: When London’s Parliament Square faced statue removals, artists created "ghost statues" (projections) to symbolize lost history.
  • Action Step:
    • Commission a mural or installation that visually represents the site’s importance.
    • Create a "virtual tour" (using 3D modeling) to show what the site should look like.
    • Host a "day of reflection" where people gather to honor the site’s legacy.

7. Push for Stronger Local Preservation Laws

Many cities have weak historic preservation ordinances. Amend them.

  • Example: San Francisco’s Design Review Board has successfully blocked destructive changes to historic buildings.
  • Action Step:
    • Propose new ordinances requiring public approval for major alterations.
    • Support ballot initiatives that strengthen preservation protections.
    • Work with architects to update design guidelines that prioritize historical integrity.

8. Prepare for Long-Term Legal Battles

If all else fails, be ready to fight in court.

  • Example: The Save Our Heritage Organisation (SOHO) has successfully sued to block the removal of historic statues in Florida.
  • Action Step:
    • Fundraise for legal fees (GoFundMe, crowdfunding platforms).
    • Document every violation (photos, videos, witness statements).
    • Consult preservation lawyers who specialize in NHPA cases.

Real-World Examples: How Other Sites Fought Back

Understanding how other preservation battles unfolded can provide valuable lessons for the reflecting pool case.

1. The Battle Over the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool (1990s)

  • Issue: The original reflecting pool (designed by Henry Bacon) was drained and altered in the 1990s.
  • Outcome: Public outcry led to a full restoration, and the National Park Service (NPS) now strictly monitors changes.
  • Lesson: Public pressure works—but legal action is often necessary.

2. The Fight to Save New York’s High Line

  • Issue: Early plans included removing original industrial elements and replacing them with generic modern designs.
  • Outcome: Activists and architects pushed for a "preservationist" approach, leading to a hybrid design that kept historic rail tracks.
  • Lesson: Collaborate with designers—they can balance modernity with history.

3. The Statue of Liberty’s Restoration (1980s)

  • Issue: The statue was deteriorating, and some proposed radical redesigns.
  • Outcome: A comprehensive restoration was funded, preserving its original copper patina.
  • Lesson: Funding is key—but public will drives change.

4. The Battle Over the Washington Monument’s Restoration (2000s)

  • Issue: Some wanted to add a new observation deck at the top.
  • Outcome: Historians argued it would "destroy the monument’s symmetry", and the plan was scrapped.
  • Lesson: Design integrity matters—even if it means rejecting "improvements."

5. The Fight to Keep the Vietnam Veterans Memorial’s Original Design

  • Issue: Some wanted to add a statue of a soldier to the memorial.
  • Outcome: Mayor Marion Barry and the public rejected the idea, keeping it as Maya Lin’s minimalist design.
  • Lesson: Sometimes, less is more—and public sentiment can override political pressure.

Common Mistakes in Historical Preservation (And How to Avoid Them)

Many preservation efforts fail due to avoidable mistakes. Here’s what not to do:

❌ Mistake 1: Relying Only on Emotional Appeals

  • Problem: If you only argue that the pool is "beautiful", courts may dismiss it as subjective.
  • Solution: Use historical, legal, and economic arguments (e.g., NHPA violations, tourism revenue, architectural principles).

❌ Mistake 2: Not Involving Experts Early

  • Problem: If you don’t consult historians or architects, your case may lack credibility.
  • Solution: Partner with the American Society of Landscape Architects (ASLA), the National Trust, or local universities.

❌ Mistake 3: Underestimating Legal Hurdles

  • Problem: Many preservationists don’t realize the NHPA requires formal complaints.
  • Solution: Consult a preservation lawyer before taking action.

❌ Mistake 4: Letting the Issue Fade Away

  • Problem: If you don’t keep the pressure on, administrations may reverse course.
  • Solution: Maintain media coverage, social media campaigns, and legal threats.

❌ Mistake 5: Compromising on Core Principles

  • Problem: Some groups accept "compromised" designs just to "keep the site intact."
  • Solution: Stand firm on non-negotiables (e.g., symmetry, reflection, original materials).

❌ Mistake 6: Ignoring Public Opinion

  • Problem: If the public doesn’t care, even strong legal cases may fail.
  • Solution: Organize rallies, petitions, and public forums to mobilize support.
