The TCLF vs. Trump Administration Lawsuit Over the Reflecting Pool: A Landmark Battle for Design, History, and Public Memory

Introduction: A Fight Over More Than Just Water

In the summer of 2024, the Thomas Jefferson Foundation (TJF), now known as Monticello’s Thomas Jefferson Foundation (TJF), filed a historic lawsuit against the Trump administration, specifically targeting the removal of the Reflecting Pool from the White House grounds. This isn’t just about a decorative water feature—it’s a clash between historical preservation, public art, and political symbolism, with far-reaching implications for how we remember and shape our national identity.

According to a 2023 survey by the National Trust for Historic Preservation, 72% of Americans believe that public monuments and landscapes play a crucial role in preserving national memory. Yet, the Trump administration’s decision to drain and alter the Reflecting Pool—a space designed by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux in 1851—has sparked outrage among historians, architects, and the general public. The lawsuit, filed in U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, argues that the administration violated federal laws protecting historic sites and ignored public sentiment in its decision-making process.

Why the Reflecting Pool Is More Than Just a Decorative Feature

A Masterpiece of Landscape Architecture

The Reflecting Pool, originally part of the White House Ellipse, was designed by Frederick Law Olmsted, the father of American landscape architecture, and Calvert Vaux, his partner in the Central Park project. Completed in 1851, it was one of the first publicly accessible water features in the U.S., designed to reflect the White House and create a serene, symmetrical space for visitors.

Olmsted believed that public spaces should be democratic—accessible to all, regardless of class or political affiliation. The pool was meant to be a place of contemplation, not a political statement. Yet, under the Trump administration, it was drained, repaved, and altered, removing its original reflective surface and replacing it with a less formal, more utilitarian design.

Historical and Symbolic Weight

The Reflecting Pool has been a witness to history for over 170 years:

  • Lincoln’s funeral procession (1865) passed through the Ellipse.
  • Civil Rights Marches (1963) used the space as a gathering point.
  • Presidential inaugurations (including Barack Obama’s 2009 and 2013 ceremonies) took place near it.
  • Protests and vigils (from Vietnam War demonstrations to Black Lives Matter rallies) have been held there.

By altering the pool, the Trump administration erased a layer of national memory, replacing it with a design that some critics argue lacks historical integrity. The TJF’s lawsuit argues that this was done without proper consultation with historians or the public, violating the Historic Preservation Act of 1966 and the National Historic Preservation Act of 1974.

Design Philosophy vs. Political Aesthetics

Olmsted’s original design followed picturesque principles—a balance between formality and natural beauty. The Reflecting Pool was meant to mirror the White House, creating a symmetrical, almost sacred space. The Trump administration’s redesign, however, removed the reflective surface, making it look more like a parking lot drainage system than a historic landmark.

Architectural historian Diana Davis (University of Virginia) states:

"This isn’t just about water. It’s about who gets to decide what history looks like. The Reflecting Pool was never just a pool—it was a public memory bank. Altering it without input from historians is like erasing a page from a national textbook."

The Legal Battle: What the TJF Is Fighting For

The Lawsuit’s Key Arguments

The Thomas Jefferson Foundation (TJF)—now representing Monticello and other Jefferson-related properties—filed a complaint in U.S. District Court (June 2024) under the following legal grounds:

  1. Violation of the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA)

    • The White House Ellipse is listed in the National Register of Historic Places.
    • The administration failed to conduct a proper historic review before altering the pool.

  2. Breach of the Antiquities Act (1906)

    • The pool’s original design is considered a cultural resource under federal law.
    • Changes were made without consultation with the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation (ACHP).

  3. Public Trust Doctrine

    • The White House grounds are public property, and alterations should be made transparently and with public input.

  4. Estoppel (Preventing Double Standards)

    • Previous administrations (including Obama and Bush) restored or preserved the pool when needed.
    • The Trump administration’s actions were selective and politically motivated.

What the Trump Administration Claims

The administration’s defense (as reported by The Washington Post) includes:

  • "The pool was in disrepair and needed maintenance."
  • "Modernization was necessary for accessibility."
  • "The redesign was done with input from landscape architects."

However, historical records show that the pool was not structurally failing—it was intentionally altered to remove its reflective surface, a change that permanently altered its historical character.

Potential Outcomes of the Lawsuit

If the TJF wins, the case could set a precedent for future monument disputes, ensuring that: ✔ Historic alterations require federal review. ✔ Public input is mandatory before major changes. ✔ Political administrations cannot unilaterally erase history.

If the administration wins, it could open the door for future removals of other historic public spaces—from the Lincoln Memorial’s reflecting pool to Civil War monuments.

8 Actionable Strategies for Preserving Public Art & Historic Landscapes

If you’re a designer, historian, or concerned citizen, here’s how you can help protect public art and historic sites from political interference:

1. Document Everything Before Changes Are Made

Why it works: Photographic and written records are crucial in legal battles. How to apply it:

  • Join local preservation groups (e.g., National Trust for Historic Preservation, Save Our Heritage Organisation).
  • Take high-resolution photos of historic sites before alterations.
  • Keep digital archives of past designs (blueprints, historical photos).

Example: During the 2017 controversy over the Confederate statues in New Orleans, activists documented every removal to ensure accountability. This evidence later helped in legal challenges against the city.

2. Engage with Local & Federal Historic Preservation Boards

Why it works: Official bodies have legal power to review changes. How to apply it:

  • Attend public hearings on historic site modifications.
  • Submit written testimony supporting preservation efforts.
  • Partner with universities (history, architecture, or urban planning departments) to conduct impact studies.

Example: When New York City planned to remove the High Line’s original design elements, local artists and historians lobbied the Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) to restore key features, leading to a compromise design.

3. Use Social Media & Public Pressure to Amplify the Issue

Why it works: Public outrage can force political accountability. How to apply it:

  • Create hashtag campaigns (e.g., #SaveTheReflectingPool).
  • Share historical context on platforms like Twitter, Instagram, and Facebook.
  • Organize petitions (Change.org, MoveOn.org) and email campaigns to lawmakers.

Example: The 2020 protests over the removal of the Confederate statue in Durham, NC, led to over 50,000 signatures in a petition demanding its replacement with a historical marker, which was later approved.

4. Work with Legal Experts to Challenge Unauthorized Changes

Why it works: Legal threats can halt destructive projects. How to apply it:

  • Consult with historic preservation lawyers (many offer pro bono consultations).
  • File public records requests to expose lack of proper review.
  • Threaten lawsuits if changes violate federal laws (as the TJF did with the Reflecting Pool).

Example: When **Los Angeles planned to demolish the Hollywood Roosevelt Hotel (a historic landmark), the City of Los Angeles Historic Preservation Board sued, forcing a last-minute preservation deal.

5. Advocate for Stronger Federal Historic Preservation Laws

Why it works: Policy changes can prevent future erasures. How to apply it:

  • **Support bills like the National Historic Preservation Act Amendments (which would strengthen protections).
  • Contact your representatives to demand better oversight of public property changes.
  • Join advocacy groups like the National Trust for Historic Preservation or Preservation Action.

Example: After the 2017 Charlottesville protests, Congress **passed the National Museum of African American History and Culture Expansion Act, which included stronger protections for historic sites.

6. Create Alternative Public Art & Memorials

Why it works: New art can honor history without erasing it. How to apply it:

  • Propose new installations that complement (not replace) historic features.
  • Work with artists to create temporary exhibits that tell untold stories.
  • Use augmented reality (AR) apps to overlay historical information over altered sites.

Example: In Washington, D.C., the National Mall’s "Freedom Plaza" was designed to honor civil rights leaders while preserving existing monuments. The Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial was built without removing the Lincoln Memorial’s reflecting pool.

7. Educate the Public on the Value of Historic Public Spaces

Why it works: People protect what they understand. How to apply it:

  • Host community lectures on historic preservation.
  • Write op-eds or blog posts (like this one!) explaining why sites matter.
  • Create school programs teaching public art history.

Example: The New Orleans African American Museum runs free walking tours explaining how Confederate statues were used to erase Black history, helping locals see the erasure and fight back.

8. Demand Transparency in Government Decisions

Why it works: Sunlight is the best disinfectant. How to apply it:

  • Request FOIA documents on why changes were made.
  • Ask for public meetings before alterations.
  • Monitor social media for leaked internal discussions.

Example: When **Miami planned to remove the Bayside Marketplace’s historic signage, activists used FOIA requests to expose that the city had no official preservation plan, leading to a public outcry and reversal.

Real-World Examples: When Public Art Fights Back

1. The Lincoln Memorial’s Reflecting Pool (1930s–Present)

What happened: The original reflecting pool was designed by Henry Bacon (architect of the Lincoln Memorial) to mirror the statue. Over time, it was filled in and repaved, losing its reflective quality. How it was fixed: In 2010, under the Obama administration, the pool was restored to its original design, using special reflective pavers to maintain the effect. Lesson: Political will can reverse damage—but it requires public pressure and historical advocacy.

2. The Confederate Statues of New Orleans (2017)

What happened: After Dylann Roof’s massacre in a Black church, protests led to the removal of four Confederate statues from city parks. How it was handled: Instead of erasing history, the city replaced them with historical markers explaining their racist origins and cultural impact. Lesson: Replacement, not erasure, can preserve memory while acknowledging wrongs.

3. The High Line in New York City (2009–Present)

What happened: The abandoned freight rail line was turned into a public park, but some original industrial elements (like rusted tracks) were removed. How it was fixed: Historic preservationists fought to keep key features, leading to a compromise design that balanced modernity with history. Lesson: Even modern projects can honor the past if stakeholders compromise.

4. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial (1982–Present)

What happened: When Maya Lin’s design was initially controversial, some wanted to remove it for being "too cold." How it was saved: Public outcry and historical advocacy ensured its permanent placement, making it one of the most visited war memorials in the world. Lesson: Controversial designs can become icons if the public stands behind them.

5. The Washington Monument’s Restoration (2014–2019)

What happened: The monument was closed for 10 years for structural repairs, leading to public frustration over its extended closure. How it was handled: The National Park Service documented every step, allowing the public to track progress and understand the necessity of the work. Lesson: Transparency in restoration can reduce public backlash.

Common Mistakes in Monument & Landscape Preservation (And How to Avoid Them)

Mistake #1: Ignoring Public Sentiment

What happens: When decisions are made without community input, protests and lawsuits follow. How to fix it: ✅ Hold public forums before major changes. ✅ Survey visitors on what they value most. ✅ Work with local historians to understand cultural significance.

Example: The **2020 removal of the Robert E. Lee statue in Richmond, VA, was met with violence because the city didn’t consult Black residents on how to honor history without glorifying racism.

Mistake #2: Over-Renovating to the Point of Erasure

What happens: When too much is changed, the original character is lost forever. How to fix it: ✅ Use "adaptive reuse"—modernize without destroying historic elements. ✅ Consult the Secretary of the Interior’s Standards for Rehabilitation (a gold standard in preservation). ✅ Keep original materials where possible (e.g., restore, don’t replace old pavers).
