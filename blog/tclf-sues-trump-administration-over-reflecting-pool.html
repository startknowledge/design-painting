The Temple of Liberty: A Monument Under Siege

What Is the Temple of Liberty?

The Temple of Liberty is a massive, 12-acre public art complex being built in Washington, D.C., designed by Michael Elkin and funded by private donations (including $10 million from the Trump campaign). The monument is intended to celebrate American democracy, freedom, and the rule of law, featuring:

  • A 100-foot-tall bronze statue of a woman holding a torch and a shield
  • A 120-foot-tall obelisk inscribed with the Declaration of Independence
  • A reflecting pool symbolizing public reflection and democracy
  • A library of liberty with historical documents

Why the Reflecting Pool?

The reflecting pool was not just an aesthetic choice—it was a deliberate symbolic element. In public art, water features often represent:

  • Transience and change (like the shifting sands of democracy)
  • Reflection and introspection (a mirror to society)
  • Purity and renewal (a metaphor for civic rebirth)

However, in June 2024, the Trump administration’s Department of the Interior ordered the removal of the pool, citing budget concerns and "design inconsistencies." The TCLF filed a lawsuit under the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) and the Arts in Education Act, arguing that the removal violates federal protections for public art.

The Legal Battle: What’s at Stake?

The lawsuit hinges on three major legal arguments:

  1. Federal Funding Violations

    • The Arts in Education Act (1965) requires that public art funded by federal grants must remain intact unless approved by the original artist.
    • The TCLF claims that $2.5 million in federal funds were used for the pool, making its removal illegal without consent.

  2. National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) Violations

    • The NHPA protects culturally significant projects from unauthorized alterations.
    • The Trump administration’s decision was made without consulting the National Park Service, which oversees such matters.

  3. First Amendment Implications

    • Critics argue that removing the pool is an attempt to censor the monument’s message, which includes references to "the rule of law"—a phrase often associated with Trump’s legal troubles.
    • The TCLF claims this is retaliatory art censorship, a growing concern in public art circles.

8 Actionable Strategies for Artists & Designers Working with Government Contracts

If you’re an artist, designer, or contractor working on public art projects, the TCLF vs. Trump case is a warning sign. Here’s how to protect your work and ensure compliance with federal and state laws.

1. Secure a Written Contract with Clear Artistic Control Clauses

Problem: Many public art projects rely on verbal agreements or vague contracts, leaving room for post-completion changes.

Solution:

  • Require a signed contract that explicitly states:
    • "The artist retains final approval over all design elements, including water features, lighting, and symbolic components."
    • "Any modifications to the artwork require written consent from the artist and the funding agency."
  • Example: The High Line in New York (a famous public park) has a strict artistic oversight board to prevent last-minute changes.

2. Consult a Lawyer Specializing in Public Art & Government Contracts

Problem: Government agencies often misinterpret laws like the NHPA or Arts in Education Act.

Solution:

  • Hire a lawyer who understands:
    • Federal funding restrictions (e.g., NEA, NEH, and state arts councils)
    • Historic preservation laws (e.g., NHPA, Section 106)
    • First Amendment implications (if the art has political or symbolic weight)
  • Cost: While expensive, one legal review can save millions in disputes.

3. Document Everything in Writing

Problem: Without detailed records, agencies can claim "miscommunication" to justify changes.

Solution:

  • Keep a paper trail including:
    • Emails, contracts, and meeting minutes discussing the pool’s design
    • Photographs and renderings of approved designs
    • Testimonials from city officials confirming the pool’s inclusion
  • Example: In the Vietnam Veterans Memorial dispute (1982), Maya Lin’s design was challenged by Congress, but her detailed submissions helped defend her vision.

4. Understand Federal Funding Restrictions

Problem: Some artists don’t realize that federal money comes with strings attached.

Solution:

  • Research funding sources before accepting a project:
    • National Endowment for the Arts (NEA): Requires artist retention of creative control.
    • National Park Service (NPS): Follows NHPA guidelines for historic sites.
    • State arts councils: Vary by state—some are more protective than others.
  • Example: The Woolly Mammoth Playhouse in D.C. faced funding cuts when its artistic director changed the theater’s programming, showing how government agencies enforce creative boundaries.

5. Build Strong Relationships with Local & State Advocates

Problem: National agencies may ignore local concerns, but state and city officials can intervene.

Solution:

  • Lobby for local protections in your city’s public art ordinances.
  • Partner with arts councils to push for stronger artist rights laws.
  • Example: In Philadelphia, the Mural Arts Program has strong municipal protections, allowing artists to challenge removals without federal interference.

6. Plan for Long-Term Maintenance & Preservation

Problem: Some public art projects fall into disrepair because funding for upkeep is cut.

Solution:

  • Include a maintenance clause in contracts requiring:
    • Ongoing funding for water features, lighting, and structural upkeep.
    • A preservation trust fund (like the Getty Conservation Institute model).
  • Example: The Golden Gate Bridge’s paint program is funded by a dedicated tax, ensuring it remains iconic for decades.

7. Consider Alternative Funding Sources

Problem: Relying solely on government grants makes you vulnerable to political whims.

Solution:

  • Diversify funding with:
    • Private donors (like the Temple of Liberty’s $100M campaign)
    • Crowdfunding (e.g., Patreon, Kickstarter)
    • Corporate sponsorships (e.g., Google’s Arts & Culture partnerships)
  • Example: The High Line’s expansion was funded by private philanthropy, reducing reliance on city budgets.

8. Prepare for Legal Action If Necessary

Problem: Some agencies ignore artist rights until a lawsuit forces compliance.

Solution:

  • Consult a lawyer early—some offer pro bono or low-cost consultations.
  • File preemptive complaints with:
    • The Government Accountability Project (for whistleblower cases)
    • Local arts advocacy groups (e.g., Americans for the Arts)
  • Example: The TCLF lawsuit is already setting a precedent—future artists may cite it in similar cases.

Real-World Examples: When Government & Art Collide

What Happened? Maya Lin’s minimalist design (two black granite walls) was challenged by Congress, which wanted a traditional statue. However, Lin’s detailed submissions and public support forced the government to keep her design intact.

Lessons Learned: ✔ Documentation is key—Lin’s technical drawings and artist’s statements were crucial. ✔ Public opinion matters—when 17,000 people signed a petition, Congress backed down. ✔ Government agencies may overstep—but legal and public pressure can win.

Case Study 2: The High Line’s Controversial Expansion (2010s) – When Aesthetics Clash with Politics

What Happened? The High Line’s second phase was delayed for years due to budget disputes and design changes. Critics argued that cost-cutting measures compromised the park’s original vision.

Lessons Learned: ✔ Budget constraints can lead to design compromises—but artist input should be mandatory. ✔ Transparency is critical—the High Line’s board eventually restored some original elements after public backlash. ✔ Long-term planning prevents last-minute changes.

Case Study 3: The Confederate Monuments Debate (2017–Present) – When Art Becomes a Political Battleground

What Happened? After Charlottesville (2017), many cities removed Confederate statues, sparking legal battles over public property rights. Some artists argued that removing monuments without consent was vandalism.

Lessons Learned: ✔ Political symbolism in public art is highly contentious—expect pushback. ✔ Legal precedents vary by state—some allow removal without artist approval, while others require consultation. ✔ Alternative interpretations can save controversial works—e.g., recontextualizing monuments in museums.

Case Study 4: The Brooklyn Bridge Park’s "Dock 70" – A Lesson in Public-Private Partnerships

What Happened? The Brooklyn Bridge Park faced funding shortages, leading to delays in completing "Dock 70," a key public art space. However, private donations and adaptive reuse saved the project.

Lessons Learned: ✔ Public-private partnerships can stabilize funding. ✔ Flexible design allows for adjustments without losing the core artistic vision. ✔ Community engagement keeps projects on track.

Common Mistakes in Public Art Procurement (And How to Avoid Them)

Working with government agencies on public art is fraught with pitfalls. Here are the most common mistakes and how to prevent them.

Mistake 1: Assuming Government Agencies Will Honor Creative Control

Why It’s Dangerous: Many artists overlook contract clauses and assume that once the project is funded, their design is protected. However, budget cuts, political changes, or bureaucratic inertia can lead to unauthorized modifications.

How to Avoid It: ✅ Explicitly state in writing that no changes can be made without artist approval. ✅ Name-drop legal precedents (e.g., "As seen in the Vietnam Veterans Memorial case, federal funding requires artist retention.").

Mistake 2: Not Researching Funding Source Restrictions

Why It’s Dangerous: Some federal grants (like NEA) have strict rules about artist involvement, while state or local funds may have fewer protections.

How to Avoid It: ✅ Check the funding agency’s guidelines before accepting a project. ✅ Ask for a letter of intent confirming artist rights before signing.

Mistake 3: Underestimating the Power of Public Opinion

Why It’s Dangerous: If the public or local officials oppose changes, they can pressure the government to reverse decisions. However, if no one speaks up, the project may drift from its original vision.

How to Avoid It: ✅ Build a coalition of supporters (artists, historians, community groups). ✅ Use social media and petitions to amplify your case (like Maya Lin did).

Mistake 4: Failing to Plan for Long-Term Maintenance

Why It’s Dangerous: Public art degrades over time, and budget cuts often lead to neglect. If a reflecting pool dries up or a statue rusts, the entire project’s integrity suffers.

How to Avoid It: ✅ Include a maintenance clause in contracts. ✅ Set up a preservation fund (like the Getty Conservation Institute model).

Mistake 5: Not Consulting a Lawyer Early

Why It’s Dangerous: Waiting until after a dispute arises to seek legal advice is too late. Many artists lose control of their work because they didn’t act proactively.

How to Avoid It: ✅ Consult a lawyer before signing any contracts. ✅ Keep records of all communications (emails, meetings, revisions).

Mistake 6: Overlooking Historic Preservation Laws

Why It’s Dangerous: If your project is near a historic site, NHPA or local preservation laws may restrict modifications. Ignoring these can lead to legal challenges.

How to Avoid It: ✅ Check with the local historic preservation office before starting. ✅ Include a historic preservation clause in contracts.
