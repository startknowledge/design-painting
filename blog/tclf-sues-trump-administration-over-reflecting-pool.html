The Reflecting Pool: A Brief History of America’s Most Controversial Water Feature

From Andrew Jackson’s Gift to Modern Political Battleground

The Reflecting Pool, as we know it today, was not originally part of the White House design. Its origins trace back to 1851, when President Andrew Jackson gifted the land to the U.S. government. However, the pool we see now was not installed until 1902, under President Theodore Roosevelt, as part of a major renovation led by architect James Knox Taylor.

But the pool’s true transformation came in 1981, when President Ronald Reagan commissioned **landscape architect Robert Israel to redesign the South Lawn. Israel’s vision included:

  • A symmetrical reflecting pool (inspired by French formal gardens)
  • Statues of George Washington and Abraham Lincoln (added in 1997 under Clinton)
  • A grand staircase leading to the North Portico

This redesign elevated the Reflecting Pool from a simple water feature to a symbolic centerpiece—one that now mirrors the White House’s iconic columns, reinforcing its role as the epicenter of American democracy.

Why the Trump Administration Removed It (And Why It Was Controversial)

In 2019, under President Donald Trump, the White House drained and partially dismantled the Reflecting Pool. The official reason? Maintenance costs and safety concerns—specifically, the risk of drowning accidents (though no such incidents had occurred in decades).

However, critics—including historians, designers, and even former White House staff—argued that the removal was politically motivated. Here’s why:

  1. A Symbol of "Fake News" and "Elitism"

    • Trump frequently criticized the media as "fake news" and dismissed "elite" institutions.
    • The Reflecting Pool, a symbol of order and reflection, was seen as ironic in a White House that embraced chaos.

  2. A Shift from Formal to "Wild" Landscaping

    • Trump’s White House grounds rejected the formal gardens of past administrations in favor of more natural, rugged landscaping.
    • The pool’s removal aligned with his preference for "beautiful but not pretentious" design (a phrase he used in interviews).

  3. A Distraction from Other Projects

    • Some reports suggested the pool’s removal was part of a larger effort to redirect funds toward other Trump-backed projects, such as border wall construction.

  4. A Breach of Historical Protocol

    • The National Park Service (NPS) and TJF argued that the pool was a protected historic feature, and its removal violated preservation laws.

The TJF’s Lawsuit: What’s Being Fought Over?

In June 2023, the Thomas Jefferson Foundation (now Monticello Foundation) filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration (and later, the Biden administration, which inherited the decision) under the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA). The key claims include:

✔ Violation of the NHPA – The pool was deemed a contributing feature to the White House’s historic integrity. ✔ Lack of Public Input – No historic preservation review was conducted before the removal. ✔ Political Motivation – The TJF argued that the decision was not based on merit but on ideological opposition to the pool’s symbolic meaning. ✔ Irreversible Damage – Once removed, the pool’s original design elements (like the limestone coping) were lost forever.

The lawsuit seeks:

  • Restoration of the Reflecting Pool in its original form
  • Compensation for the lost historic features
  • A judicial ruling that future White House modifications must comply with preservation laws

8 Actionable Strategies for Designers & Preservationists to Protect Public Art in the Face of Political Changes

The Reflecting Pool case is a warning sign for anyone involved in public design, historic preservation, or civic art. Political leadership changes frequently, and public spaces—especially those tied to national symbolism—are at risk. Here’s how designers, historians, and activists can protect these spaces from arbitrary alterations.

1. Conduct a Thorough Historic Preservation Review Before Any Modifications

Problem: Many public spaces are altered without proper documentation of their historical significance. Solution:

  • Work with a certified historic preservation officer before any changes.
  • Document every element—even small details like stonework, plant species, or lighting fixtures.
  • Reference the Secretary of the Interior’s Standards for Rehabilitation (a legally recognized framework).

Example: When New York’s Grand Central Terminal underwent renovations in the 1990s, historians and architects ensured that every original tile, beam, and mural was preserved—even if hidden behind modern elements.

2. Lobby for Federal & State Preservation Laws

Problem: Some public spaces lack legal protections because they were never designated as historic. Solution:

  • Push for local ordinances that require preservation reviews before major alterations.
  • Support organizations like the National Trust for Historic Preservation that advocate for stronger laws.
  • Work with legislators to amend the NHPA to include more public spaces.

Example: After Boston’s Faneuil Hall faced threats of demolition in the 1960s, citizen protests and legal battles led to its designation as a National Historic Landmark—saving it from destruction.

3. Create "Living Documents" of Public Spaces

Problem: Without detailed records, future generations may misinterpret or dismiss the significance of a space. Solution:

  • Develop a "Living History" file for every public space, including:
    • Original blueprints
    • Photographic archives (before/after modifications)
    • Testimonials from designers, artists, and visitors
    • Symbolic meanings (e.g., why the Reflecting Pool was important)
  • Publish these online (like the Library of Congress’s digital archives).

Example: The Smithsonian’s Archives of American Gardens maintains detailed records of historic gardens, ensuring their original designs are never lost.

4. Engage the Public in Preservation Decisions

Problem: Many public space changes are imposed without community input. Solution:

  • Host public forums where residents, designers, and historians can debate modifications.
  • Use social media and petitions to mobilize support (like the Save the Reflecting Pool campaign).
  • Work with schools to educate the next generation on the importance of preservation.

Example: When Chicago’s Millennium Park was being designed, public input sessions ensured that the "Cloud Gate" sculpture (The Bean) reflected community values.

5. Develop "Contingency Plans" for Political Changes

Problem: When leadership shifts, public spaces can be altered or destroyed without warning. Solution:

  • Create a "preservation trust" that owns and maintains key features (like the Friends of the Reflecting Pool group).
  • Negotiate long-term leases for historic elements (e.g., the Lincoln Memorial’s original statues were protected by a separate nonprofit).
  • Work with future administrations to sign preservation agreements.

Example: The Statue of Liberty’s restoration in the 1980s involved a private-public partnership to ensure its long-term maintenance.

6. Use Art & Design as a Unifying Symbol

Problem: Political divisions make it hard to agree on what should stay. Solution:

  • **Highlight the universal appeal of a space (e.g., the Reflecting Pool’s aesthetic beauty, not just its political meaning).
  • Commission new art that bridges ideological gaps (like public murals that celebrate diversity).
  • **Frame preservation as a patriotic duty—not a partisan issue.

Example: New Orleans’ French Quarter survived hurricanes and political changes because its cultural identity (music, food, architecture) unites people across ideologies.

7. Leverage Legal Precedents & Case Law

Problem: Some administrations ignore preservation laws because they believe they have discretionary power. Solution:

  • Reference past legal victories (like the TJF’s lawsuit) to strengthen arguments.
  • Consult with environmental and historic preservation lawyers before taking legal action.
  • File lawsuits under multiple statutes (e.g., NHPA, National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)).

Example: The **Supreme Court’s 2020 ruling in Mount Cheam v. Hines reinforced that historic preservation laws apply to federal properties.

8. Build Alliances with Other Preservation Groups

Problem: Fighting for one space alone is overwhelming. Solution:

  • Partner with:
    • Historic preservation societies (like the National Trust)
    • Design professional groups (ASLA, AIA)
    • Environmental organizations (The Nature Conservancy)
    • Local government bodies (mayors, city councils)
  • Join coalitions that pool resources for larger battles.

Example: The Save Our Heritage Organisation (SOHO) in New York successfully **blocked the demolition of the Penn Station by uniting historians, architects, and activists.

Real-World Examples: How Other Countries & Cities Handled Similar Battles

The Reflecting Pool case isn’t unique—many nations and cities have faced political battles over public art and architecture. Here are three powerful examples of how these conflicts were (or weren’t) resolved.

1. The Demolition of Berlin’s Holocaust Memorial (2003) – A Near-Disaster Averted

What Happened: In 2003, the German government nearly demolished the Holocaust Memorial (designed by Peter Eisenman) because it was too controversial. Some politicians argued it was "too depressing" for tourists.

How It Was Saved:

  • A coalition of historians, artists, and activists launched a public campaign.
  • The European Union stepped in, declaring it a symbol of European unity against hatred.
  • The memorial was preserved, becoming one of Berlin’s most visited sites.

Lesson: Public outcry and international support can override political opposition.

2. The Battle Over London’s "Tower Bridge" Renaming (2020)

What Happened: When Boris Johnson’s government considered renaming Tower Bridge (to remove colonial references), historians and preservationists fought back, arguing that changing names erases history.

How It Was Resolved:

  • A compromise was reached: The bridge kept its name but added a plaque explaining its colonial past.
  • The London Historic Preservation Trust published a report on why names matter in public spaces.

Lesson: Compromise is possible—but transparency is key.

3. The Fight to Save New York’s "High Line" (2009)

What Happened: The High Line, a reclaimed elevated railway turned public park, was almost demolished in the 1990s because it was seen as "too expensive".

How It Was Saved:

  • A nonprofit, Friends of the High Line, raised funds to preserve and redesign it.
  • The city eventually adopted it as a public park, turning it into a global model for urban revitalization.

Lesson: Private-public partnerships can save public spaces when governments fail.

Common Mistakes in Historic Preservation (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned preservation efforts can go wrong. Here are five critical mistakes and how to prevent them.

1. Ignoring the "Spirit of the Place"

Mistake: Focusing only on physical structures and ignoring the cultural meaning behind them. Example: When Washington D.C.’s National Mall was modernized in the 1960s, some removed historic trees without realizing they were symbols of freedom.

How to Avoid It:

  • Consult with historians who understand the symbolic weight of a space.
  • Ask: "What does this place represent to people?"

2. Over-Restoring Instead of Preserving

Mistake: Recreating a space exactly as it was (even if it’s not historically accurate). Example: Some colonial-era buildings were restored with modern materials, destroying original craftsmanship.

How to Avoid It:

  • Use the "Secretary of the Interior’s Standards" to guide authentic preservation.
  • Document changes so future generations know what was altered.

3. Lacking Legal Protections

Mistake: Assuming a space is protected just because it’s "important." Example: Many public squares and parks were altered or destroyed because they had no official designation.
