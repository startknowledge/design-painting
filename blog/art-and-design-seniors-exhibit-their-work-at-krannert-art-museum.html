Why Senior Exhibitions Like Krannert’s Matter

1. A Rite of Passage for Emerging Artists

For many students, participating in a senior art and design exhibition is the culmination of years of hard work, experimentation, and refinement. Unlike class assignments or smaller gallery shows, these exhibitions are public, high-stakes presentations that demand professional-level execution.

At Krannert, students are given curatorial freedom—meaning they often design their own installations, write accompanying essays, and even negotiate with museum staff to ensure their vision is realized. This level of autonomy and responsibility is rare in most academic settings, making it a critical stepping stone for those entering the professional world.

2. A Bridge Between Academia and Industry

Many employers and art directors actively seek out graduates who have exhibited their work in reputable venues. A senior show at Krannert—especially one that receives critical acclaim or media coverage—can boost a graduate’s credibility almost overnight.

For example, graphic design seniors who create interactive digital installations or branding projects for real clients often gain industry attention. Similarly, industrial design students who prototype functional yet artistic objects may attract recruiters from top firms like IDEO or Fuseproject.

3. A Platform for Experimentation and Risk-Taking

One of the most valuable aspects of a senior exhibition is the encouragement to take risks. Unlike traditional assignments with strict guidelines, these projects allow students to:

  • Blend disciplines (e.g., combining digital animation with traditional sculpture)
  • Explore unconventional materials (e.g., recycled plastics, bioplastics, or AI-generated textures)
  • Challenge societal norms (e.g., socially conscious murals, protest art, or speculative design)

Past Krannert exhibitions have featured works that push ethical boundaries, such as:

  • A data visualization project mapping climate change impacts in Illinois
  • A wearable tech installation exploring body autonomy and digital privacy
  • A site-specific sculpture critiquing consumerism in fast-fashion culture

These pieces don’t just look impressive—they spark conversation, which is exactly what forward-thinking employers and galleries are looking for.

8 Actionable Strategies to Elevate Your Creative Work (Inspired by Krannert’s Best Pieces)

If you’re preparing for your own senior exhibition—or simply looking to refine your portfolio—here are eight proven strategies inspired by the most award-winning and impactful works at Krannert.

Strategy 1: Develop a Strong Conceptual Foundation

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: Many of the most memorable pieces at Krannert aren’t just visually striking—they tell a story. Whether through symbolism, metaphor, or direct commentary, the best projects connect emotionally with viewers.

Example: One standout project was "Echoes of the Digital Divide", a series of augmented reality (AR) portraits that superimposed historical labor movements onto modern workers’ faces. The artist, a graphic design senior, used AI-generated facial mapping to create a haunting visual dialogue between past and present struggles.

How to Apply This:

  • Start with a question, not just an aesthetic goal. Ask: "What do I want viewers to feel? What do I want them to question?"
  • Research deeply. The best concepts build on existing discourse (e.g., climate activism, digital ethics, cultural identity).
  • Develop a "concept statement"—a short paragraph explaining your work’s intent, influences, and significance.

Pro Tip: If your project is abstract, pair it with strong textual elements (e.g., an accompanying zine, QR codes linking to essays, or interactive audio descriptions).

Strategy 2: Master the Art of Minimalism (When Less Is More)

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: Some of the most visually powerful pieces at Krannert rely on restraint. Whether through negative space, monochromatic palettes, or sparse typography, minimalism forces the viewer to engage more deeply with the work.

Example: A product design senior created "The Last Print", a handmade, single-sheet newspaper that critiqued the decline of print media. The entire piece was black ink on white paper, with no images—just typographic essays on the history of journalism. The simplicity made it unforgettable.

How to Apply This:

  • Edit ruthlessly. Remove every unnecessary element—whether it’s a superfluous color, a distracting texture, or an extraneous object.
  • Use contrast strategically. High-contrast designs (e.g., black text on white, or a single bold color against neutral tones) draw the eye.
  • Consider "less is more" in functionality. If designing a physical object, ask: "Does this serve a purpose beyond decoration?"

Pro Tip: Minimalism works best when paired with strong typography or a compelling narrative. If your piece is purely visual, ensure it has a clear "hook"—something that stops viewers in their tracks.

Strategy 3: Integrate Technology Thoughtfully (Not Just for the Sake of It)

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: Technology is everywhere in design today, but the most successful Krannert projects don’t just use tech for novelty—they enhance the story.

Example: A digital art senior created "Memory Fragments", an interactive LED wall where viewers could touch projected light patterns to trigger personal anecdotes about memory loss. The piece blended hardware, software, and emotional storytelling in a way that felt both futuristic and deeply human.

How to Apply This:

  • Choose tech that serves the concept. If your project is about isolation, a VR experience might work. If it’s about community, a collaborative digital mural could be better.
  • Keep interactions intuitive. Avoid overly complex controls—viewers should be able to engage with your work within seconds.
  • Document the process. If your piece is tech-heavy, include behind-the-scenes content (e.g., a video essay on your coding journey or a technical breakdown of how it works).

Pro Tip: If you’re not a coder, collaborate with tech-savvy peers or faculty mentors. Many Krannert students pair with computer science majors to bring their ideas to life.

Strategy 4: Use Materiality to Tell a Story

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: The physical properties of materials can enhance a piece’s meaning. Whether it’s recycled wood, biodegradable plastics, or unconventional textures, the best projects make viewers feel the work.

Example: An industrial design senior created "Waste to Wonder", a series of chairs made entirely from discarded fishing nets. The rough, tangled texture of the material visually represented the problem of ocean pollution, while the functional design offered a symbolic solution.

How to Apply This:

  • Research materials with purpose. If your piece is about sustainability, use eco-friendly materials. If it’s about transience, consider perishable elements (e.g., sandcastles, ice sculptures).
  • Experiment with textures. A smooth surface can feel cold and clinical, while a rough, handmade finish can feel organic and human.
  • Consider the lifecycle of your materials. Will they biodegrade, be repurposed, or end up in a landfill? Your choice can reinforce or undermine your message.

Pro Tip: If you’re working with unconventional materials, document the process—show viewers how the piece was made, not just what it looks like.

Strategy 5: Create Interactive or Participatory Experiences

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: Passive viewers are less likely to remember your work. The most engaging pieces at Krannert invite interaction, making the experience personal and memorable.

Example: A public art senior installed "The Listening Wall", a series of sound-activated panels where visitors could whisper messages that would be recorded and played back in a loop. The piece turned the gallery into a communal space, encouraging strangers to connect.

How to Apply This:

  • Design for touch, sound, or movement. Even small interactions (e.g., a button that reveals hidden text, a lever that changes lighting) can immersive the viewer.
  • Encourage social engagement. If possible, design for groups—e.g., a collaborative drawing station or a shared digital canvas.
  • Consider accessibility. Ensure your interactive elements are usable for people with disabilities (e.g., captioned audio, tactile feedback).

Pro Tip: If your piece is too complex for a gallery setting, simplify it for a portfolio. A video documentation of the interaction can convey the experience just as effectively.

Strategy 6: Develop a Cohesive Visual Language

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: The best senior projects don’t just look good—they feel like they belong together. A consistent visual language (color palette, typography, iconography) reinforces the theme and makes the work more memorable.

Example: A graphic design senior created "The Unseen City", a series of posters, a zine, and a website all centered on invisible urban spaces (e.g., subway tunnels, rooftops, underground tunnels). The **entire project used a single color scheme—deep blues and grays—with a **custom typeface that mimicked subway graffiti to unify the pieces.

How to Apply This:

  • Develop a "visual bible"—a document outlining your colors, fonts, icons, and patterns.
  • Limit your palette. Stick to 3-5 colors max to avoid visual clutter.
  • Ensure consistency across mediums. If you’re working with print, digital, and physical objects, make sure they visually dialogue with each other.

Pro Tip: If you’re not a designer, use free tools like Canva or Adobe Illustrator’s "Color Guide" to standardize your visuals.

Strategy 7: Incorporate Real-World Context (Even If It’s Speculative)

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: The most impactful projects don’t just exist in a vacuum—they relate to real-world issues, even if they’re fictional or speculative.

Example: An urban design senior created "Neo-Chicago 2150", a vision of a post-climate-change city where floating neighborhoods, vertical farms, and AI-managed infrastructure replaced traditional urban planning. The project didn’t just look futuristic—it proposed solutions to current environmental crises.

How to Apply This:

  • Base your work on real data. If your piece is about housing inequality, research current policies and statistics.
  • Consider "what if" scenarios. Even if your project is speculative, it should feel grounded in reality.
  • Engage with local communities. If possible, collaborate with real organizations (e.g., nonprofits, businesses, or government agencies) to add authenticity.

Pro Tip: If you’re working on a speculative project, clearly label it as such—but still ground it in real-world relevance.

Strategy 8: Prioritize Presentation and Installation

What Krannert’s Top Works Do: Even the most brilliant concept can fall flat if the presentation is weak. The best senior projects consider how they’ll be displayed—from lighting and placement to viewer flow.

Example: A sculpture senior created "The Weight of Silence", a series of suspended metal spheres that represented unspoken traumas. The artist carefully calculated the balance of each piece so that viewers had to lean in to see them, creating a physical and emotional tension.

How to Apply This:

  • Think like a curator. Where will your piece be placed in the gallery? How will lighting affect its appearance?
  • Consider the viewer’s path. Will they see your piece first, last, or in passing? Adjust accordingly.
  • Test your installation. If possible, mock up your piece in a small space before the final exhibition.

Pro Tip: If you’re working with a tight budget, focus on the most critical elements—e.g., a strong backdrop, strategic lighting, or a clear signage system.
