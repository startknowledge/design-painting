How an Artist’s Mural Transformed a Concession Stand at Willoughby Gilson Park: A Case Study in Public Art & Community Engagement

Introduction: The Power of Public Art in Urban Spaces

Public art has long been recognized as a transformative force in urban environments, breathing life into public spaces and fostering community connection. According to a 2023 report by the National Endowment for the Arts (NEA), cities with vibrant public art initiatives see a 20% increase in foot traffic and a 15% boost in local business revenue within a year of implementation. Additionally, a 2022 study by the University of Pennsylvania found that exposure to public art reduces stress levels by 37% and enhances overall well-being.

One of the most striking examples of public art’s impact is the artist’s mural at Willoughby Gilson Park’s concession stand, a project that didn’t just beautify a functional space but also revitalized local tourism, encouraged civic pride, and even influenced food sales. This case study explores how a well-executed mural project can turn an ordinary concession stand into a cultural landmark, while offering actionable strategies for artists, municipalities, and businesses looking to replicate this success.

Why Public Art Matters: The Case for Investment in Community Spaces

Before diving into the specifics of Willoughby Gilson Park’s mural, it’s essential to understand why public art matters—especially in public spaces like parks, stadiums, and transit hubs.

1. Economic Benefits of Public Art

  • Increased Tourism & Revenue: A 2021 study by the American Alliance of Museums found that cities with prominent public art attract 40% more visitors than those without.
  • Higher Property Values: Neighborhoods with public art see property values increase by up to 12% within five years (per a 2020 Urban Land Institute report).
  • Boosted Local Businesses: Retailers and restaurants near public art installations report a 25% increase in sales (NEA, 2023).

2. Social & Psychological Benefits

  • Reduced Crime Rates: Studies show that well-designed public art can lower crime by up to 30% in high-traffic areas (Journal of Urban Affairs, 2022).
  • Enhanced Mental Health: Exposure to art in public spaces has been linked to lower stress levels and improved mood (Harvard Health Publishing, 2021).
  • Strengthened Community Identity: Public art projects foster sense of place, making residents feel proud and connected to their city.

3. Functional & Aesthetic Enhancements

  • Improved Wayfinding: Murals and sculptures can serve as visual guides, helping visitors navigate public spaces more easily.
  • Seasonal Appeal: Thematic murals (e.g., seasonal changes, local history) keep public spaces fresh and engaging year-round.
  • Branding Opportunities: Businesses can leverage public art for marketing, turning a simple concession stand into a recognizable landmark.

The Willoughby Gilson Park Mural: A Masterclass in Public Art Execution

Willoughby Gilson Park, a beloved 200-acre recreational area in Cleveland, Ohio, faced a challenge: its concession stand was functional but forgettable. Located near the park’s entrance, it was a high-traffic but low-impact space—until a local artist, Eleanor "Ellie" Hartwell, was commissioned to transform it into a visual storyteller.

The Vision Behind the Mural

Hartwell’s mural, titled "The Heart of Willoughby," was designed to: ✅ Celebrate local history—incorporating nods to the park’s Indigenous roots and early settlers. ✅ Reflect the park’s natural beauty—depicting the Cuyahoga River, forests, and wildlife. ✅ Encourage interaction—using interactive elements (e.g., a "find the hidden animal" game for kids). ✅ Boost brand visibility—the concession stand’s logo was subtly woven into the mural’s design.

Key Features of the Mural’s Success

  1. Thematic Depth – Instead of a generic landscape, the mural told a story, making it memorable and shareable.
  2. Durability & Maintenance – Painted on weather-resistant acrylic, it required minimal upkeep over three years.
  3. Community Involvement – Hartwell collaborated with local schools, inviting students to contribute small elements.
  4. Functional Integration – The mural didn’t just decorate; it guided visitors (e.g., directional arrows painted into the design).
  5. Seasonal Updates – During holidays, temporary elements (like a mini "Thanksgiving feast" scene) were added.

The Results: A 40% Increase in Concession Sales

Within six months of the mural’s completion, the park saw:

  • A 40% rise in concession stand sales, with visitors spending 15% more per transaction.
  • Social media buzz, with #WilloughbyMural trending locally and featured in Cleveland Magazine’s "Best of Cleveland" list.
  • Increased park attendance, with weekend crowds growing by 22%.
  • Corporate sponsorship interest, leading to a partnership with a local brewery for a "Mural Brew" event.

8 Actionable Strategies to Transform Your Own Public Space with Art

Inspired by Willoughby Gilson Park’s success, here are eight proven strategies to bring public art to life in your own projects—whether you're an artist, municipality, or business owner.

1. Define a Clear Purpose & Story

A generic mural is forgettable. A mural with a story is legendary.

How to Apply This:

  • Research the location’s history (e.g., Indigenous land, industrial past, famous events).
  • Engage the community—host workshops where locals suggest themes.
  • Example: The High Line in New York wasn’t just a park; it was a restored railway turned art installation, telling the story of NYC’s industrial evolution.

Common Mistake: Skipping research and creating something too generic (e.g., a random rainbow without context).

2. Choose the Right Artist & Collaborate Early

The best murals are co-creations between artist and client.

How to Apply This:

  • Host an open call for artists (use platforms like ArtDeadline or Mural Arts Philadelphia’s database).
  • Provide clear briefs—include budget, timeline, and community expectations.
  • Example: The Pittsburg Public Art Initiative worked with local artists to document Pittsburgh’s steel industry, ensuring authenticity.

Common Mistake: Micromanaging the artist, leading to a design that feels forced or uninspired.

3. Make It Interactive & Engaging

Passive art is seen. Interactive art is experienced.

How to Apply This:

  • Add hidden elements (e.g., "Find the squirrel in every mural").
  • Include QR codes linking to augmented reality (AR) experiences.
  • Example: The "Mural of the Month" series in Los Angeles invites visitors to vote on future themes via social media.

Common Mistake: Overcomplicating interactivity, making it frustrating rather than fun.

4. Ensure Durability & Low Maintenance

A beautiful mural that fades in six months is a wasted investment.

How to Apply This:

  • Use weather-resistant paints (e.g., acrylic, ceramic, or powder-coated metal).
  • Test samples in the location’s climate (e.g., saltwater resistance for coastal areas).
  • Example: The San Diego Trolley murals use UV-resistant paint, keeping colors vibrant for over a decade.

Common Mistake: Cutting costs on materials, leading to peeling, cracking, or color fading.

5. Integrate Functional Elements

Why just decorate when you can enhance usability?

How to Apply This:

  • Paint directional arrows into the mural.
  • Use color-coding for different park zones.
  • Example: The Boston Public Library’s murals guide visitors to specific sections while telling historical stories.

Common Mistake: Treating the mural as a standalone decoration, ignoring its practical role.

6. Involve the Community in the Process

Public art should belong to the people—not just the artists or city officials.

How to Apply This:

  • Host public brainstorming sessions (e.g., "What should our mural represent?").
  • Feature local stories (e.g., interviews with elders, veterans, or artists).
  • Example: The "Portland Public Art Project" included input from over 5,000 residents before finalizing designs.

Common Mistake: Excluding the community, leading to backlash or indifference.

7. Plan for Seasonal & Temporary Updates

A static mural loses appeal over time. Keep it fresh.

How to Apply This:

  • Add temporary elements (e.g., holiday-themed additions).
  • Rotate murals in high-traffic areas.
  • Example: The "Seattle Mural Festival" features new works every year, keeping the city’s art scene dynamic.

Common Mistake: Leaving the mural unchanged, causing viewer fatigue.

8. Measure Success & Gather Feedback

Did the mural achieve its goals? Track the data.

How to Apply This:

  • Monitor foot traffic before and after installation.
  • Survey visitors on their experience (use Google Forms or SurveyMonkey).
  • Track social media engagement (hashtags, shares, mentions).
  • Example: The "Chicago Public Art Initiative" uses heatmaps and sales data to prove ROI.

Common Mistake: Assuming success without evidence, leading to poor future funding decisions.

Common Mistakes in Public Art Projects (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned public art projects can fail if key pitfalls aren’t addressed. Here’s what to watch out for:

1. Lack of Clear Vision

Mistake: Commissioning an artist without specific goals (e.g., "just make it look nice"). Solution:

  • Define objectives (e.g., "boost tourism," "tell a local story," "reduce graffiti").
  • Example: The Portland Public Art Initiative started with three clear goals:
    1. Celebrate diversity
    2. Enhance wayfinding
    3. Encourage civic pride

2. Poor Location Selection

Mistake: Placing a mural in a low-traffic area where no one sees it. Solution:

  • Choose high-visibility spots (e.g., park entrances, transit hubs, downtown streets).
  • Example: The Seattle Central Library’s mural was placed right at the entrance, ensuring maximum exposure.

3. Ignoring Budget Realities

Mistake: Underestimating costs for materials, permits, and maintenance. Solution:

  • Allocate 10-15% of the budget for unexpected expenses.
  • Example: The Boston Public Library’s mural project initially budgeted $500K but ended up at $750K due to unexpected structural prep work.

4. No Community Engagement

Mistake: Creating art without local input, leading to backlash or indifference. Solution:

  • Host public workshops, surveys, and feedback sessions.
  • Example: The "Detroit Mural Project" involved over 2,000 residents in design decisions.

5. Neglecting Maintenance

Mistake: Assuming the mural will last forever without upkeep. Solution:

  • Schedule regular cleanings (every 6-12 months).
  • Use weather-resistant materials (e.g., ceramic paint, powder-coated metal).
  • Example: The San Diego Trolley murals require annual touch-ups but have lasted over 15 years.

6. Overlooking Legal & Permit Requirements

Mistake: Skipping zoning laws, easement agreements, or property rights checks. Solution:

  • Work with city planners to ensure legal compliance.
  • Example: A 2018 mural in Miami was demolished after 6 months because the artist didn’t secure proper permits.
