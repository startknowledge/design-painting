How an Artist’s Mural Transformed a Concession Stand at Willoughby Gilson Park: A Case Study in Public Art & Community Engagement

Introduction: The Power of Public Art in Revitalizing Public Spaces

Public art has long been recognized as a catalyst for community engagement, economic growth, and aesthetic enhancement. According to a 2023 report by the National Endowment for the Arts (NEA), communities with vibrant public art initiatives see a 23% increase in foot traffic and a 15% boost in local business revenue within two years of implementation. Meanwhile, a 2022 study by the Urban Land Institute (ULI) found that well-designed public art installations can reduce vandalism by up to 30% while improving mental well-being among visitors.

One such transformative project took place at Willoughby Gilson Park, a beloved recreational area in Cleveland, Ohio. When the park’s aging concession stand needed a refresh, local artist Sarah Chen was commissioned to create a large-scale mural that would not only modernize the space but also tell the story of the park’s history and the surrounding community. The result? A fully revamped concession stand that became a local landmark, drawing visitors, boosting sales, and fostering a stronger sense of pride among residents.

Why Public Art in Concession Stands Works: The Business & Community Benefits

Before diving into the Willoughby Gilson Park mural, let’s examine why public art—especially in commercial spaces like concession stands—can be a game-changer.

1. Increased Foot Traffic & Sales

A 2021 study by the American Society of Landscape Architects (ASLA) found that businesses near public art installations see up to 40% more visitors than those without. At Willoughby Gilson Park, the mural didn’t just make the concession stand more visually appealing—it became a destination.

2. Enhanced Brand Identity & Local Pride

When a business integrates local art, it signals to customers that it cares about the community. The mural at Willoughby Gilson Park featured historical references to Cleveland’s industrial past, making it instantly recognizable to locals while also attracting tourists.

3. Reduced Vandalism & Increased Safety

Public art acts as a visual deterrent to graffiti and vandalism. The 2022 Cleveland Police Department report noted that areas with well-maintained public art experience fewer incidents of property damage than those without.

4. Improved Mental Well-Being & Tourism

A 2023 Harvard study found that exposure to beautiful public art reduces stress and increases sense of place. For parks and recreational areas, this means longer visits, happier customers, and repeat business.

5. Tax & Grant Incentives

Many cities offer tax breaks or grants for businesses that invest in public art. Cleveland, for example, has a Public Art Program that provides funding for projects like the Willoughby Gilson Park mural.

The Willoughby Gilson Park Mural: A Step-by-Step Breakdown

Now, let’s take a deep dive into how the mural at Willoughby Gilson Park was conceived, executed, and maintained—offering actionable lessons for anyone looking to bring public art to their own space.

Step 1: Research & Community Engagement

Before picking up a brush, Sarah Chen spent three months researching:

  • The park’s history (established in 1925, originally a Works Progress Administration project)
  • Nearby landmarks (the Cuyahoga River, Cleveland’s industrial heritage)
  • Local art movements (Cleveland’s strong mural tradition, including works by John Outterbridge)

Key Takeaway: Always involve the community in the planning phase. Conduct surveys, hold focus groups, or even host a "mural brainstorming night" to gather ideas.

Step 2: Defining the Vision & Budget

The park’s management team worked with Chen to define the mural’s purpose:

  • Educational (teaching visitors about Cleveland’s history)
  • Aesthetic (making the concession stand stand out)
  • Commercial (encouraging sales)

Budget Breakdown:

  • Artist fee: $35,000 (covered by a city grant)
  • Materials (paint, primer, protective coating): $8,000
  • Permits & safety measures: $5,000
  • Marketing (promoting the unveiling): $3,000

Total: $51,000

Key Takeaway: Public art doesn’t always require a massive budget. Look for grants, sponsorships, or crowdfunding to offset costs.

Step 3: Designing the Mural

Chen’s design incorporated:

  • Historical elements (depictions of early 20th-century park workers)
  • Modern touches (a stylized Cuyahoga River with contemporary boats)
  • Interactive features (a QR code linking to a digital timeline of the park’s history)

Why This Worked:

  • Appealed to all ages (kids loved the playful elements, adults appreciated the historical context)
  • Encouraged social media sharing (visitors took photos of the QR code, boosting local engagement)

Key Takeaway: A great mural tells a story—whether it’s historical, cultural, or purely abstract, it should resonate with the audience.

Step 4: Execution & Safety Measures

The mural was painted over two weeks with:

  • Scaffolding for safety
  • Weatherproof materials (acrylic paint with a UV-resistant sealant)
  • Community involvement (local high school art students assisted in touch-ups)

Key Takeaway: Safety first! Ensure proper permits, scaffolding, and weather protection to avoid delays or accidents.

Step 5: Unveiling & Community Celebration

The mural’s debut was a major event, featuring:

  • Live music (a local jazz band)
  • Free food samples (from the concession stand)
  • Artist Q&A sessions

Result: Over 500 attendees, with local news coverage and social media buzz.

Key Takeaway: A grand unveiling turns a simple mural into a community experience—boosting word-of-mouth marketing.

Step 6: Maintenance & Long-Term Success

To ensure the mural lasted, the park implemented:

  • Regular cleaning (soft brushes to remove dust)
  • Annual touch-ups (repainting faded areas)
  • Community monitoring (encouraging visitors to report any damage)

Result: Five years later, the mural remains vibrant, with no major vandalism incidents.

Key Takeaway: Public art requires ongoing care. Establish a maintenance plan before installation.

8 Actionable Strategies to Transform Your Space with Public Art

Inspired by the Willoughby Gilson Park success story, here are eight proven strategies to bring public art to your own business or public space.

1. Start with a Clear Vision & Goals

Before commissioning any artist, ask:

  • What message do we want to convey? (Historical? Modern? Playful?)
  • Who is our target audience? (Families? Tourists? Locals?)
  • How will this benefit our business? (More sales? Better branding?)

Example: A Cleveland coffee shop wanted to attract students, so they commissioned a mural featuring local college mascots—resulting in a 30% increase in foot traffic during school semesters.

2. Research Local Artists & Their Styles

Don’t just pick the most expensive artist—find someone whose style aligns with your vision.

  • Check portfolios (look for past public art projects)
  • Read reviews (ask other businesses for recommendations)
  • Attend local art fairs (network with artists in person)

Example: A Chicago food truck park hired a street artist known for vibrant, abstract designs, which doubled their social media engagement in six months.

3. Secure Funding Through Grants & Sponsorships

Public art doesn’t have to break the bank. Explore:

  • City/state grants (many offer 50-70% funding)
  • Corporate sponsorships (local businesses may sponsor in exchange for branding)
  • Crowdfunding (platforms like ArtBackers specialize in public art projects)

Example: A Denver park raised $40,000 for a mural through a community crowdfunding campaign, with matching funds from the city.

4. Involve the Community in the Process

People are more likely to support and protect art they helped create. Ways to engage:

  • Host a "mural design contest" (let locals vote on concepts)
  • Invite feedback via surveys or social media polls
  • Feature local stories in the artwork (e.g., "This mural honors Cleveland’s steelworkers")

Example: A Boston library involved local historians in designing a mural about the city’s immigrant communities—increasing library visits by 25%.

5. Choose Durable, Weather-Resistant Materials

Public art faces sun, rain, and vandalism. Use:

  • Acrylic paint (easy to touch up, UV-resistant)
  • Epoxy coatings (for high-traffic areas)
  • Mosaic tiles (for extreme durability)

Example: A Miami beachfront mural used marine-grade epoxy to withstand saltwater corrosion—remaining vibrant for eight years.

6. Plan for Safety & Permits

Before painting, ensure:

  • Proper scaffolding (if working at height)
  • Weather protection (tarps, windbreaks)
  • Permits (check local regulations on public art)

Example: A New York subway mural was delayed for three months due to unpermitted scaffolding—costing the artist $15,000 in fines.

7. Market the Unveiling Like a Big Event

A grand opening turns a mural into a community experience. Ideas:

  • Live performances (music, poetry readings)
  • Free giveaways (local businesses sponsor prizes)
  • Social media challenges (e.g., "Tag us in your mural selfie!")

Example: A Portland food hall hosted a "Mural Night" with free samples, live painting demos, and a DJ—tripling their usual sales that evening.

8. Establish a Maintenance Plan

Public art requires ongoing care. Create a schedule for:

  • Regular cleaning (soft brushes, mild soap)
  • Annual inspections (check for peeling paint, cracks)
  • Community reporting (encourage visitors to alert staff of damage)

Example: A San Francisco park mural had minimal vandalism because the city painted over graffiti within 48 hours and rewarded tipsters with free park passes.

Real-World Examples of Public Art Transforming Commercial Spaces

The Willoughby Gilson Park mural isn’t the only success story—here are three more case studies proving that public art can boost business and community pride.

1. The "Big U" Mural Project (New York City)

Location: A series of murals along High Line Park in Manhattan. Impact:

  • Increased property values by 20% in adjacent buildings
  • Doubled foot traffic for nearby cafés and shops
  • Reduced homeless encampments by 40% (due to increased surveillance from art-loving visitors)

Why It Worked:

  • Collaborative design (involved local artists, historians, and urban planners)
  • Seasonal themes (changed designs to reflect spring, summer, fall)
  • Interactive elements (QR codes linking to virtual tours of NYC’s history)

Lesson: Public art should evolve with the community—don’t make it static.

2. The "Color Me Rad" Murals (Los Angeles)

Location: Graffiti-covered walls in Skid Row, transformed into vibrant, community-driven murals. Impact:

  • Reduced gang-related graffiti by 60% (since locals protected the official art)
  • Increased tourism for nearby art galleries and restaurants
  • Provided jobs for local artists and youth programs

Why It Worked:

  • Bottom-up approach (artists were hired from the neighborhood)
  • Educational component (each mural had a story behind it)
  • Funded by corporate sponsors (Google and Bank of America donated)

Lesson: Even high-crime areas can benefit from public art—if done with community trust.

3. The "Mural of the Americas" (Chicago)

Location: A massive, interactive mural at Millennium Park. Impact:

  • Generated $12 million in tourism revenue in its first year
  • Inspired a wave of local mural projects across Chicago
  • Became a symbol of cultural unity (featured Indigenous, African, and Latin American art)

Why It Worked:

  • Cultural representation (reflected Chicago’s diverse population)
  • Digital integration (AR app allowed visitors to explore hidden details)
  • Long-term funding (city allocated $500,000 annually for maintenance)

Lesson: Public art should reflect the people—not just decorate a space.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Integrating Public Art

While public art can be transformative, many projects fail due to poor planning. Here are five critical mistakes and how to avoid them.

1. Ignoring Community Feedback

Mistake: Commissioning an artist without local input leads to unpopular designs that don’t resonate. Solution:

  • Host public workshops to gather ideas
  • Survey potential visitors before finalizing the design
  • Consider hiring a local artist (they understand the community’s tastes)

Example: A Houston food court mural was criticized for being "too corporate"—until they replaced it with a design featuring local food traditions, which doubled sales.

2. Skimping on Quality Materials

Mistake: Using cheap paint or weak substrates leads to quick fading and damage. Solution:

  • Invest in UV-resistant acrylic paint
  • Use epoxy or mosaic tiles for high-traffic areas
  • Apply a protective sealant to extend lifespan

Example: A Miami beach mural painted with regular latex paint faded in six months—while a neighboring mural with epoxy lasted five years.
