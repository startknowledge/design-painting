This project didn’t just beautify a functional space—it revitalized local tourism, encouraged social interaction, and even influenced food sales by making the concession stand a must-see attraction.   • How this mural was conceived and executed

8 Actionable Strategies to Transform a Concession Stand (or Any Public Space) with Art

If you’re an artist looking to take on a public art project, or a business owner wanting to boost engagement through art, these strategies—inspired by the Willoughby Gilson Park mural—will help you maximize impact and community buy-in.

1. Conduct Deep Research Before Designing

The Willoughby Gilson mural wasn’t just pretty—it was meaningful. The artist spent three months researching the park’s history, interviewing former players, fans, and local historians, and even studying architectural blueprints of the original 1923 stadium.

How to apply this:

  • For artists: Visit the location multiple times, observe foot traffic patterns, and talk to locals, business owners, and city planners.
  • For businesses: Partner with local historians, museums, or cultural organizations to gather inspiration.
  • Use tools like:
    • Google Earth to study the space’s surroundings
    • Local archives for historical photos and documents
    • Surveys (via Google Forms or Typeform) to ask the community what they’d like to see

Pro Tip: If the space has historical significance, consider collaborating with a historian or archivist to ensure accuracy.

2. Choose a Theme That Resonates with the Community

The Willoughby Gilson mural didn’t just depict baseball—it told a story. It included:

  • A timeline of the park’s evolution (from 1923 to present)
  • Silhouettes of famous players (like Lou Boudreau, a Cleveland native)
  • Industrial motifs (like cargo ships and factory smokestacks) to reflect Cleveland’s past

How to apply this:

  • Identify 3-5 key themes that define the location (e.g., sports, history, nature, local culture).
  • Avoid clichés—instead of a generic "sunset" mural, think unique to the place.
  • Example themes for different spaces:
    • Beachfront concession stand: Marine life, surf culture, local fishing history
    • Urban park: Abstract representations of city life, hidden murals in alleyways
    • School cafeteria: Student art, historical figures from the school’s past

3. Make the Art Interactive (Without Overcomplicating It)

The Willoughby Gilson mural included QR codes that, when scanned, played short videos of old games and interviews with former players. This turned a static image into an experience.

How to apply this:

  • Low-tech options:
    • Puzzle pieces that, when assembled, reveal a hidden message
    • Augmented reality (AR) markers (using apps like HP Reveal or Adobe Aero)
    • Scavenger hunt elements (e.g., "Find the hidden baseball in the mural")
  • High-tech options (if budget allows):
    • Touchscreen displays with rotating content
    • Motion sensors that trigger animations when people approach
    • Social media integration (e.g., a hashtag like #WilloughbyMural that posts user photos)

Pro Tip: Keep it simple. If the tech fails, the mural should still stand alone as art.

4. Work with the Right Materials for Durability

Public art faces sun, rain, and constant foot traffic. The Willoughby Gilson mural used:

  • Acrylic paint on metal panels (easy to clean, weather-resistant)
  • UV-resistant sealant to prevent fading
  • Reinforced backing to withstand bumps from carts and chairs

How to apply this:

  • For outdoor murals:
    • Use exterior-grade acrylic or enamel paints
    • Apply a clear, waterproof sealant (like Mod Podge Outdoor or Varathane)
    • Consider metal or fiberglass panels if the surface is prone to damage
  • For indoor murals (like concession stands):
    • Milk paint or chalk paint (easy to touch up)
    • Vinyl decals (if the surface is non-porous, like metal or glass)

Common Mistake: Using cheap, non-weatherproof materials that peel or fade within months.

5. Involve the Community in the Process

The Willoughby Gilson mural wasn’t just imposed on the community—it was co-created. The artist:

  • Hosted a public workshop where locals could sketch ideas
  • Featured community members in the mural (e.g., a retired umpire, a little league coach)
  • Shared progress updates via social media and local news outlets

How to apply this:

  • Host a "mural brainstorming session" at a local café or library.
  • Create a hashtag (e.g., #MyWilloughbyMural) and encourage submissions.
  • Feature community members in the artwork (e.g., portraits of local heroes).
  • Use social media polls to decide on minor details (e.g., "Should the mural include a dog?").

Pro Tip: Transparency builds trust. If the community feels heard, they’re more likely to support and protect the artwork.

6. Optimize the Mural for Photography & Social Media

The Willoughby Gilson mural became a viral sensation because it was designed with Instagram in mind. The artist:

  • Included bright, high-contrast colors (easy to capture on phone cameras)
  • Added playful elements (like a "selfie spot" with a fun backdrop)
  • Placed the mural in a high-traffic, well-lit area

How to apply this:

  • Use the "Rule of Thirds" in your composition for better photos.
  • Add a "signature" element (e.g., a small logo, a repeating pattern) that people recognize.
  • Ensure good lighting—avoid murals in shady or poorly lit areas.
  • Encourage tagging by placing a small sign like:

    "Tag us @WilloughbyMural for a chance to be featured!"

7. Partner with Local Businesses for Cross-Promotion

The concession stand’s owner leveraged the mural to boost sales. They:

  • Offered "Mural Specials" (e.g., "Buy a hot dog, get a free postcard with the mural design")
  • Collaborated with nearby restaurants to create limited-edition merch (e.g., mugs with the mural design)
  • Hosted "Mural Viewing Nights" with live music and food trucks

How to apply this:

  • Offer businesses a "mural discount" (e.g., 10% off for customers who tag the mural).
  • Create a "mural bundle" (e.g., a T-shirt + poster set).
  • Host events like:
    • Art walks (invite local artists to perform nearby)
    • Themed nights (e.g., "Vintage Baseball Night" with old-school snacks)
    • Charity fundraisers (donate proceeds to a local cause)

8. Plan for Maintenance & Longevity

Public art only works if it lasts. The Willoughby Gilson mural was designed with maintenance in mind:

  • Easy-to-clean surfaces (no intricate textures that trap dirt)
  • A dedicated cleaning schedule (weekly wipe-downs with mild soap)
  • A backup plan (digital prints for touch-ups if needed)

How to apply this:

  • Use durable materials (as mentioned earlier).
  • Establish a cleaning protocol (e.g., soft brushes, non-abrasive cleaners).
  • Set up a "mural care fund" (community donations to cover touch-ups).
  • Document the process (take photos before/after cleaning to track wear).

Real-World Examples of Successful Public Art in Functional Spaces

1. The "Big U" Mural Project (New York City)

While not a concession stand, the High Line’s "Big U" mural project in NYC proves that large-scale art can transform functional spaces. The 1.4-mile-long elevated park features dozens of murals by artists like Kehinde Wiley, turning a utility corridor into a cultural landmark.

Lessons for concession stands:

  • Scale matters—even a small mural can feel impactful if placed strategically.
  • Art can guide movement (e.g., a mural leading visitors from the parking lot to the stand).

2. The "Food Truck Mural" in Portland, Oregon

A Portland food truck owner commissioned a mural depicting local food history, complete with interactive elements (like a "choose your own adventure" food-themed story). The result?

  • 30% increase in foot traffic to the food truck.
  • Featured in major food blogs, driving new customers.

Lessons for concession stands:

  • Food + art = a winning combo—people love edible murals (e.g., a mural of a giant pizza with toppings that match the menu).
  • Tell a story—even if it’s just about the food itself.

3. The "Train Station Murals" in Barcelona

Barcelona’s Sants Station features murals by artists like Joan Miró, turning a functional transit hub into an art gallery. The murals:

  • Reduced perceived wait times (people linger to admire the art).
  • Increased local pride (tourists take photos, locals feel a sense of ownership).

Lessons for concession stands:

  • Art can make waiting feel like an experience (e.g., a mural that tells a seasonal story—spring flowers in April, holiday themes in December).
  • Small spaces can have big impact—even a single wall can feel like a gallery.

Common Mistakes in Public Art Projects (And How to Avoid Them)

Public art projects fail when they’re rushed, unplanned, or disconnected from the community. Here’s what went wrong in some notable flops—and how to avoid the same pitfalls.

1. Ignoring Local Culture (The "Out-of-Touch" Mural)

Example: A mural in a Mexican-American neighborhood depicted generic "Latin culture" (maracas, sombreros) without local input. The result? Boos from residents who felt it misrepresented their heritage.

How to avoid it:

  • Don’t assume you know the culture—ask, don’t guess.
  • Work with local artists or cultural organizations for guidance.
  • Avoid stereotypes—instead of clichés, focus on specific stories.

2. Poor Material Choices (The "Faded Fast" Mural)

Example: A mural in a high-traffic park used cheap, non-weatherproof paint that peeled within six months. The city had to repaint it, costing $20,000 in extra funds.

How to avoid it:

  • Test materials outdoors first (spray a small section and observe for a year).
  • Consult a mural specialist before committing to a technique.
  • Budget for maintenance—art is an investment, not a one-time expense.

3. No Community Involvement (The "Top-Down" Mural)

Example: A city imposed a mural on a neighborhood without consulting residents. The result? Vandalism within weeks because locals felt no ownership.

How to avoid it:

  • Host public meetings to gather feedback.
  • Feature local faces in the artwork (e.g., portraits of community leaders).
  • Use social media to keep people updated on progress.

4. Poor Placement (The "Hidden Gem" Mural)

Example: A stunning mural was painted in a dark alley with no foot traffic. It went completely unnoticed.

How to avoid it:

  • Place art where people naturally gather (near entrances, high-visibility spots).
  • Ensure good lighting (natural or artificial).
  • Consider accessibility—can people easily see and photograph it?

5. Overcomplicating the Design (The "Too Much" Mural)

Example: A mural in a school cafeteria had too many elements—no clear focal point, making it hard to appreciate.

How to avoid it:

  • Follow the "less is more" principle—one strong image > 10 weak ones.
  • Use negative space to guide the viewer’s eye.
  • Test the design digitally first before committing to paint.
