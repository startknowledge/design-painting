8 Actionable Strategies to Make the Most of the Steiner Church Art & Quilt Show

Attending a craft show is more than just browsing—it’s about strategic engagement. Here’s how to maximize your experience before, during, and after the event.

1. Research Artists & Themes in Advance

Why? Many artists have limited-edition pieces or special commissions available only at the show.

How?

  • Check the official event website (if available) for artist bios and themes of the exhibition.
  • Follow the Steiner Church’s social media (Instagram, Facebook) for artist spotlights and behind-the-scenes content.
  • Look for past shows (if this isn’t the first event) to see what styles and mediums have been featured.

Pro Tip: If you’re interested in modern quilt art, seek out artists who use unconventional fabrics (like vinyl, leather, or even old maps)—these often sell out fast.

2. Set a Budget & Prioritize Pieces

Why? Craft shows can be overwhelming, and impulse buys often lead to regret or financial strain.

How?

  • Decide how much you’re willing to spend (e.g., $50–$200, $200–$500, or no limit).
  • Make a wishlist of must-have styles (e.g., abstract, nature-themed, or functional quilts).
  • Negotiate politely—many artists are open to small discounts if you’re a first-time buyer or if they have multiple pieces left.

Real-World Example: At the 2022 Portland Quilt Festival, a hand-dyed wall hanging by Linda Lee (a local textile artist) sold for $450, but attendees who researched her past work were more likely to appreciate its value and avoid overpaying.

3. Arrive Early for the Best Selection

Why? Popular pieces disappear quickly, and early birds get first dibs on limited stock.

How?

  • Plan to arrive 30–60 minutes before opening to get a feel for the layout.
  • Check for "first-look" hours—some artists offer exclusive pre-show viewings for serious buyers.
  • Bring cash—many vendors prefer small bills for quick transactions.

Common Mistake: Waiting until lunchtime to shop, only to find that your top choices are gone.

4. Ask Questions—Artists Love Sharing Their Process

Why? Quilt and textile art is deeply personal—many artists have unique stories behind their work.

How to Engage:

  • Ask about fabric sources (e.g., "Where did you get the silk for this piece?").
  • Inquire about technique (e.g., "How did you achieve that 3D effect?").
  • Ask for care instructions—some fabrics require special washing to maintain color.

Example Interaction: "I love how your quilt incorporates geometric patterns—did you use a rotary cutter for precision, or did you hand-cut the fabric?" Artist Response: "I actually used a laser cutter for the sharp edges, but the hand-sewn details were done by hand for texture."

5. Look Beyond the Quilts—Explore Mixed-Media Art

Why? Many textile artists blend quilting with other mediums, creating one-of-a-kind pieces.

What to Watch For:

  • Fabric sculptures (e.g., quilted wall reliefs)
  • Embroidered paintings (where thread becomes brushstrokes)
  • Textile installations (large-scale hanging or floor pieces)
  • Functional art (like quilted tote bags, pillows, or table runners)

Real-World Example: At the 2023 Denver Quilt Festival, Sarah Johnson displayed a quilted mirror frame where the fabric patterns mirrored the reflection, blending textile and decorative arts.

6. Document Your Finds (But Respect Artists’ Boundaries)

Why? You’ll want to remember your purchases and share them online—but always ask permission.

How to Do It Right:

  • Take photos of finished pieces (but not of artists’ private workspaces).
  • Ask if you can post on social media—some artists love free promotion.
  • Follow up with artists after the show—many offer custom commissions via email.

Mistake to Avoid: Posting uncredited photos of an artist’s work without permission—this can damage relationships in the creative community.

7. Plan for Post-Show Inspiration

Why? The best part of attending a craft show is bringing the inspiration home.

How to Stay Inspired:

  • Take notes on color palettes, patterns, and techniques you love.
  • Visit local fabric stores (like Hobby Lobby, Joann Fabrics, or boutique shops) to recreate or buy similar materials.
  • Follow up with artists—many offer online workshops or mail-order commissions.
  • Start a Pinterest board to save images of pieces you admire.

Example: If you love a **quilt with a mountain landscape motif, you might:

  • Buy similar fabric from a local shop.
  • Take a quilting class to learn the piecing technique.
  • Commission the artist to create a smaller version for your home.

8. Support Sustainability & Ethical Craftsmanship

Why? The textile industry has a big environmental impact, so supporting eco-conscious artists makes a difference.

How to Shop Sustainably:

  • Look for artists who use:
    • Upcycled fabrics (old clothes, scrap materials)
    • Organic or low-impact dyes
    • Handmade, not mass-produced, pieces
  • Ask about packaging—some artists reuse materials for shipping.
  • Consider buying from local artists to reduce carbon footprint from shipping.

Real-World Example: The Oregon Fiber Arts Guild promotes sustainable practices, encouraging members to use recycled threads and natural fibers in their work.

Real-World Examples: How Quilt Art Has Evolved into Contemporary Design

Quilting has come a long way from practical bed coverings—today, it’s a form of high art. Here’s how textile artists are pushing boundaries:

1. The Rise of "Quilt as Sculpture"

Some artists treat quilts as three-dimensional installations, using fabric, stuffing, and framing to create wall reliefs or floor pieces.

Example:

  • Martha Swope, a Pulitzer Prize-winning quilt artist, has exhibited large-scale quilts in museums like the Whitney and MoMA, where they’re displayed like paintings.
  • Modern adaptations include quilted furniture (sofas, chairs) where fabric becomes the primary design element.

2. Abstract Quilting: Where Math Meets Art

Many contemporary quilters use geometric patterns, fractals, and modular designs to create visually striking abstract pieces.

Example:

  • Denise Seaberry (a MacArthur "Genius Grant" recipient) blends African American quilting traditions with modern abstraction, using bold colors and asymmetrical shapes.
  • Local Oregon artists might incorporate Mount Hood’s geometric snow patterns into modular quilt designs.

3. Quilting as Social Commentary

Some artists use fabric to tell stories—whether about history, politics, or personal experiences.

Example:

  • "The Quilted Truth" (a collaborative project by Black quilters) addresses racial injustice through symbolic patterns.
  • War quilts from the Vietnam era inspired modern artists to create quilts about contemporary conflicts.

4. Functional Art: Quilts That Serve a Purpose

Not all quilt art is wall-hanging material—many pieces are practical yet beautiful, like:

  • Quilted tote bags (e.g., with embroidered landscapes)
  • Table runners (using mixed fabrics for texture)
  • Wall hangings (that double as room dividers)

Example: At the 2021 Houston Quilt Festival, Texas artist Lisa Ellis sold quilted aprons with hand-painted designs, blending traditional quilting with modern utility.

5. Digital Fabrication Meets Handcraft

Some artists use digital tools (like Cricut machines or laser cutters) to enhance their quilting, creating precise, intricate designs that would be impossible by hand alone.

Example:

  • Portland-based artist Jamie Johnson uses a laser cutter to create intricate lace-like patterns in fabric, then quilted them by hand for a hybrid effect.

Common Mistakes to Avoid at Craft Shows (And How to Fix Them)

Attending a craft show can be overwhelming, especially if you’re new to the scene. Here’s what not to do—and how to correct your course.

Mistake #1: Not Researching Artists Beforehand

Problem: You walk in randomly, see something you like, and immediately buy it—only to realize later that the artist’s style isn’t what you expected.

Solution:

  • Check the event’s artist roster (if available).
  • Follow artists on Instagram to see their full body of work.
  • Ask vendors for recommendations—they often know who makes the best pieces in a certain style.

Mistake #2: Overpaying for "Vintage" or "Antique" Pieces

Problem: Some sellers mark up old quilts as "rare antiques" when they’re not actually vintage.

Solution:

  • Ask for provenance—where was the quilt made, and when?
  • Compare prices—if a 1950s quilt is listed for $1,200, check eBay or estate sale listings to see if it’s reasonable.
  • Look for signs of authenticity (e.g., hand-sewn stitches, natural dyes).

Mistake #3: Ignoring the "Hidden Gems"

Problem: You focus only on the big-name artists, missing smaller, equally talented creators with unique work.

Solution:

  • Talk to every vendor—some of the best pieces are from emerging artists.
  • Ask about "workshops"—many artists offer hands-on classes where you can learn and buy supplies.
  • Look for "artist tables"—these often have exclusive, limited-edition pieces.

Mistake #4: Not Negotiating (When Appropriate)

Problem: You pay full price without realizing that some artists are open to discounts, especially for cash sales.

Solution:

  • Smile and ask politely: "Would you consider a small discount for cash?"
  • Offer to buy multiple pieces—some artists give a 5–10% discount for bulk purchases.
