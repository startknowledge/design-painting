Cathalijn Wouters’s Lyrical Practice: How Her Blurring of Painting and Drawing Redefines Contemporary Art

Introduction: The Rise of Lyrical Abstraction in Modern Art

In recent years, the boundaries between painting and drawing have become increasingly fluid, giving rise to a new wave of lyrical abstraction that challenges traditional artistic categorization. Artists like Cathalijn Wouters are pushing these limits by merging gestural mark-making, fluid color, and conceptual depth into works that defy easy classification.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel & UBS Art Market Report, abstract art—particularly lyrical and gestural abstraction—has seen a 22% increase in collector interest over the past five years. This surge reflects a broader cultural shift toward emotional expression over representational precision, making Wouters’s approach not just relevant but essential for contemporary artists exploring hybrid practices.

This post dives deep into Cathalijn Wouters’s lyrical practice, examining how she blurs the lines between painting and drawing, the technical and conceptual strategies behind her work, and actionable takeaways for artists looking to experiment with similar techniques.

Who Is Cathalijn Wouters?

Before exploring her artistic methods, it’s important to understand who Cathalijn Wouters is and why her work resonates so strongly in today’s art world.

Background and Influences

Wouters, a Dutch artist based in Amsterdam, emerged in the late 2010s as part of a new generation of European abstract painters who reject rigid formalism in favor of organic, intuitive mark-making. Her influences span:

  • Lyrical Abstraction (Joan Mitchell, Helen Frankenthaler)
  • Post-Painterly Abstraction (Morris Louis, Kenneth Noland)
  • Contemporary Gestural Painting (Gerhard Richter, Cy Twombly)
  • Dutch Golden Age Techniques (Rembrandt’s loose underdrawings)

Unlike traditional abstract painters who rely on structured grids or geometric precision, Wouters’s work is fluid, spontaneous, and deeply personal. Her pieces often feel like a conversation between the subconscious and the hand, where painting and drawing coexist in a single gesture.

Key Characteristics of Her Work

Wouters’s practice is defined by:

  1. The Absence of Distinct Lines – Her drawings are not linear; instead, they emerge from paint itself, making the distinction between "drawing" and "painting" artificial.
  2. Layered Transparency – She uses thin washes of color that allow underlayers to peek through, creating a depth of memory in the work.
  3. Organic Growth – Each piece evolves organically, with no pre-planned composition, much like landscapes or weather patterns.
  4. Emotional Resonance – Her work evokes melancholy, wonder, and movement, making it viscerally engaging rather than purely visual.

How Cathalijn Wouters Blurs the Line Between Painting and Drawing

Wouters’s genius lies in her ability to eliminate the rigid separation between these two mediums. Traditional painting is often associated with broad strokes, color fields, and surface manipulation, while drawing is precise, linear, and often preparatory. But in her hands, both techniques dissolve into one another, creating a hybrid language of mark-making.

1. The Erasure of the Brushstroke vs. Line Distinction

One of the most striking aspects of Wouters’s work is how she treats brushstrokes as if they were drawn. Instead of using a brush to apply paint in broad, textural strokes, she drags, drips, and smears in ways that resemble ink or charcoal lines.

Real-World Example: Imagine a piece where vertical streaks of ochre appear to float across a field of blue, but upon closer inspection, they are not drawn but rather the result of a brush being pulled through wet paint. The illusion of a line is created purely through paint behavior, not a pencil or pen.

2. The Use of "Negative Drawing" in Painting

Wouters often employs negative drawing—a technique where spaces between marks become the subject. This is a drawing principle applied to painting, where the absence of paint (or its thinness) defines the form.

How She Does It:

  • She loads a brush with thin, translucent paint and flicks or drags it across the canvas.
  • The dry areas (where paint didn’t adhere) emerge as "lines" in the composition.
  • This creates a duality: the paint is both the mark and the absence of mark.

3. The Role of Accidents as Intentional Gestures

Wouters embraces imperfection, treating spills, drips, and unintended marks as essential to the piece. This is a drawing mindset—where every mark, even the "wrong" one, contributes to the whole.

Actionable Tip: If you’re experimenting with this approach:

  • Work on a slightly tilted canvas to encourage uncontrolled drips.
  • Use a palette knife to scrape and disrupt layers, creating organic breaks in the surface.
  • Document "failed" attempts—sometimes the most unplanned marks become the strongest elements.

4. The Integration of Under-Drawing with Paint

While many abstract painters skip underdrawings, Wouters uses them as a foundation—but not in a traditional sense. Instead of pre-planning lines, she draws lightly with diluted paint before building up thicker layers.

Technique Breakdown:

  1. First Layer (Drawing Phase):
    • She uses a soft brush or sponge to sketch with thin, watery paint.
    • These ghostly lines remain visible beneath later applications, adding depth and history to the piece.
  2. Second Layer (Painting Phase):
    • She builds up with opaque or semi-opaque colors, but the initial marks still bleed through, creating a layered narrative.

5. The Use of "Wet-on-Wet" Techniques to Create Fluidity

Wouters’s work often feels alive, as if the paint is still moving. This is achieved through wet-on-wet techniques, where layers of paint blend seamlessly rather than sitting atop one another.

How to Replicate This:

  • Apply a base layer while it’s still slightly wet.
  • Use a damp brush to soften edges and merge colors.
  • Avoid overworking—let the organic flow dictate the form.

8 Actionable Strategies to Blur Painting and Drawing in Your Own Work

Inspired by Wouters’s approach, here are practical techniques to merge painting and drawing in your practice:

1. Start with a "Scribble" Underlayer

Instead of beginning with a blank canvas, cover it with quick, gestural marks—like ink scribbles or charcoal smudges. Then, paint over them in thin, translucent layers.

  • Why it works: The initial marks remain subtle but present, adding texture and memory to the final piece.

2. Use a "Drawing" Medium in Painting

Experiment with ink, pastel, or pencil within a painting context:

  • Dip a brush in ink and drag it through wet paint to create line-like streaks.
  • Press a pastel stick into dry paint to embed marks that won’t wash away.
  • Sketch lightly with a pencil before painting, letting the graphite show through in places.

3. Embrace "Negative Space Drawing" in Paint

Instead of filling the canvas, leave gaps where paint is thin or absent:

  • Use a sponge or rag to dab away sections of paint, revealing the underlayer.
  • Work in "subtractive" layers—remove paint to define shapes rather than adding.

4. Work with "Dripping" as a Drawing Tool

Wouters’s work often includes controlled drips that behave like drawn lines:

  • Hold the brush at an angle and let paint drip onto the canvas.
  • Use a spray bottle to disturb wet paint, creating organic, line-like patterns.
  • Tilt the canvas and allow gravity to guide the flow.

5. Combine "Thick" and "Thin" Marks in One Piece

Create contrast by mixing impasto (thick paint) and very thin washes:

  • Scrape off some paint to expose the ground, creating negative lines.
  • Use a palette knife to build up certain areas while leaving others bare.

6. Use "Blind Contour" Techniques in Painting

In blind contour drawing, you draw without looking at the paper. Apply this to painting:

  • Close your eyes and drag a brush across the canvas without visual guidance.
  • Let your arm move freely, creating unpredictable, expressive marks.

7. Incorporate "Erased" Elements

Just as drawers erase mistakes, painters can "erase" paint:

  • Use a putty knife to scrape away sections of paint.
  • Wipe with a damp cloth to remove color and reveal underlayers.
  • This creates a dialogue between presence and absence.

8. Work in "Series" to Develop a Language

Wouters often creates groups of related works where each piece builds on the last:

  • Start with one technique (e.g., dripping).
  • Refine it over multiple canvases, noticing patterns in your marks.
  • Develop a "vocabulary" of gestures that define your hybrid style.

Common Mistakes When Blurring Painting and Drawing—and How to Avoid Them

While experimenting with lyrical, hybrid techniques, artists often fall into pitfalls that can undermine the intended effect. Here’s how to avoid them:

1. Over-Planning the Composition

Mistake: Trying to control every mark leads to rigidity, killing the spontaneity that makes Wouters’s work so compelling. Solution:

  • Work in stages—start with loose, exploratory marks before refining.
  • Trust the process—let unplanned intersections become part of the design.

2. Ignoring the "Underlayer"

Mistake: Painting over initial marks too heavily erases their emotional and textural contribution. Solution:

  • Use translucent glazes to preserve underlayers.
  • Test different paint consistencies (e.g., watercolor vs. acrylic) to see what bleeds through best.

3. Forcing a "Line" Where None Should Be

Mistake: Trying to make every mark look like a drawn line can feel artificial. Solution:

  • Embrace ambiguity—some marks should feel like paint, others like drawing.
  • Vary your tools (brushes, sponges, fingers) to create different effects.

4. Neglecting the "Negative Space"

Mistake: Filling the canvas completely removes the dynamic tension between mark and absence. Solution:

  • Leave gaps—empty space can be as powerful as paint.
  • Use a grid to block out areas you’ll intentionally leave blank.

5. Overworking the Piece

Mistake: Adding too many layers can muddy the work, losing its lyrical quality. Solution:

  • Work in sessions—let the first layer dry before adding more.
  • Step back often to assess what’s working and what’s overcomplicating.

6. Not Experimenting with Different Mediums

Mistake: Sticking to one technique (e.g., only brushwork) limits your hybrid potential. Solution:

  • Mix mediums (e.g., ink + oil paint, pastel + acrylic).
  • Try unconventional tools (e.g., forks, sponges, fingers).

7. Disregarding the "Emotional Core"

Mistake: Focusing only on technique without personal expression makes the work cold and mechanical. Solution:

  • Ask yourself: What does this mark feel like?
  • Let your emotions guide the direction of the piece.

Real-World Examples of Artists Blurring Painting and Drawing

While this post focuses on Cathalijn Wouters, many contemporary artists practice similar hybrid techniques. Here are three standout examples and how they merge painting and drawing:

1. Julie Mehretu – The Map as a Drawing/Painting Hybrid

Mehretu’s large-scale abstract works combine architectural plans, calligraphy, and gestural marks into complex, layered compositions.

  • Drawing Aspect: Her intricate linework resembles architectural blueprints or ancient manuscripts.
  • Painting Aspect: She builds up these lines with thick, impasto paint, creating depth and texture.
  • Key Takeaway: Her work shows how structured drawing can evolve into painting through layering and abstraction.

2. Gerhard Richter – The Erasure of Medium Boundaries

Richter’s "Blast" paintings (from the 1980s) mimic the look of explosions through layered, chaotic marks.

  • Drawing Aspect: The initial scribbles resemble sketches of destruction.
  • Painting Aspect: He builds up these marks with thick, textured paint, making them feel like both drawing and sculpture.
  • Key Takeaway: Richter proves that even the most "painted" works can retain a drawing-like quality through gestural spontaneity.

3. Agnes Martin – Minimalist Linework in Paint

Martin’s delicate grids and lines are almost like drawings, yet they are painted with extreme precision.

  • Drawing Aspect: Her thin, repetitive lines feel hand-drawn.
  • Painting Aspect: The subtle color shifts and textured surfaces make them undeniably painted.
  • Key Takeaway: Her work demonstrates how even the simplest "drawing" can be elevated by painting techniques.
