Cathalijn Wouters’ Lyrical Practice: How Her Blurring of Painting and Drawing Redefines Contemporary Art

Introduction: The Rise of Lyrical Abstraction in Modern Art

In recent years, the boundaries between painting and drawing have become increasingly fluid, giving rise to a new wave of lyrical abstraction that challenges traditional artistic categorization. Among the most influential figures in this movement is Cathalijn Wouters, a Dutch artist whose work defies conventional distinctions between mediums. According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel Survey, over 65% of contemporary art collectors now favor hybrid practices that merge painting, drawing, and digital techniques, reflecting a growing demand for experimental, non-linear artistic expression.

Wouters’ approach—often described as "lyrical and gestural"—blurs the lines between controlled precision and spontaneous mark-making, creating works that feel both intimate and expansive. Her practice invites viewers to engage with texture, color, and emotional resonance rather than rigid compositional rules. This shift aligns with a broader trend in contemporary art, where process over product is increasingly valued.

In this guide, we’ll explore:

  • How Wouters’ lyrical practice redefines painting and drawing
  • Key techniques she employs (and how you can adapt them)
  • Real-world examples of her work and its impact
  • Common mistakes artists make when blending these mediums—and how to avoid them
  • Actionable strategies to develop your own hybrid approach

By the end, you’ll have a clear roadmap for integrating Wouters’ principles into your own artistic practice, whether you’re a painter, draughtsman, or experimental artist.

Why Cathalijn Wouters’ Work Matters: The Philosophy Behind Lyrical Abstraction

Wouters’ art is deeply rooted in lyrical abstraction, a movement that emerged in the mid-20th century but has seen a resurgence in the 2020s. Unlike hard-edge abstraction (think Barnett Newman or Ellsworth Kelly), lyrical abstraction prioritizes emotional expression, fluidity, and organic growth.

Key Philosophical Influences

  1. Automatism & Spontaneity – Inspired by André Masson and Jackson Pollock, Wouters embraces uncontrolled, intuitive mark-making while still maintaining a sense of deliberate composition.
  2. Materiality & Texture – She treats paint and drawing tools as extensions of her body, allowing the medium to dictate movement rather than the other way around.
  3. Color as Emotion – Her palette is rich and layered, with hues that evoke memory, mood, and atmosphere rather than literal representation.
  4. The Dialogue Between Mediums – Unlike traditional painters who work within a single discipline, Wouters intertwines drawing and painting in ways that feel seamless rather than forced.

How This Differs from Traditional Painting & Drawing

Traditional Painting Traditional Drawing Wouters’ Lyrical Approach
Fixed surface (canvas) Often on paper Hybrid surfaces (canvas, paper, mixed media)
Brushwork as controlled Line as deliberate Spontaneous yet structured mark-making
Color as flat application Line as outline Color as texture, line as gesture
Composition as rigid Composition as sketch Evolving, non-linear structure

This fusion of disciplines is what makes Wouters’ work so compelling—it feels like a conversation between different artistic languages rather than a rigid adherence to one.

8 Actionable Strategies to Blur Painting and Drawing Like Cathalijn Wouters

If you’re looking to incorporate lyrical abstraction into your practice, Wouters’ methods offer a flexible framework. Below are eight key strategies you can adapt, whether you’re a beginner or an experienced artist.

1. Embrace "Accidental" Marks as Intentional Gestures

Wouters often allows her tools to move freely, creating organic, unpredictable lines that later become part of the composition.

How to Apply This:

  • Start with loose, unplanned strokes—don’t overthink them.
  • Use unconventional tools: Squeegees, sponges, or even fingers can create textural contrasts.
  • Work in layers: Let one mark influence the next, building depth through spontaneity.

Example: In her piece "Untitled (2022)", Wouters began with thin, wavering lines that seemed almost like doodles. As she layered thicker, gestural brushstrokes over them, the initial marks became anchors for the composition, creating a dialogue between control and chaos.

2. Use Color as a Textural Element, Not Just a Flat Wash

Unlike traditional painting, where color is often applied smoothly, Wouters treats it as a physical material—something that builds, erodes, and interacts with other marks.

How to Apply This:

  • Mix colors on the palette but also on the surface—let them bleed into each other.
  • Scrape, smear, or drag paint to create organic transitions.
  • Use underpainting (a thin, transparent layer) to reactivate color in later stages.

Example: In "Lyric Forms (2021)", Wouters began with a monochrome underlayer of burnt umber. As she added layers of cobalt blue and cadmium red, she scraped and dragged the paint with a palette knife, creating glazed, luminous patches that pulse beneath the surface.

** 3. Work in Series to Develop a "Language" of Marks

Wouters often creates connected works where each piece builds on the last, refining her gestural vocabulary.

How to Apply This:

  • Start a series—even if it’s just five related studies.
  • Experiment with the same tools or techniques across pieces.
  • Document your process—sketching failed attempts can lead to unexpected discoveries.

Example: Her "Horizon Series (2020)" began with horizontal line studies on paper. As she progressed, she transferred these marks to canvas, amplifying them with paint while keeping the drawing’s delicacy. The result was a bridge between sketch and finished work.

4. Combine Drawing and Painting in the Same Work

Wouters doesn’t see drawing and painting as separate acts—she interweaves them within a single piece.

How to Apply This:

  • Start with a drawing (charcoal, ink, or pencil) and paint over it.
  • Use drawing tools mid-painting—dabbing ink or pressing graphite into wet paint.
  • Create "hybrid zones" where one medium transitions into another.

Example: In "Interlude (2023)", Wouters began with fine, inked lines that mapped out a subtle landscape. She then painted over them with thin washes of color, allowing the ink to bleed and interact with the paint. The final effect was a work that felt like a conversation between two languages.

5. Play with Scale to Create Dramatic Contrasts

Wouters often contrasts tiny, delicate marks with large, sweeping gestures, creating visual tension.

How to Apply This:

  • Start small (a sketch or a detail) and scale up in later stages.
  • Use different tools for different scales—a fine liner for details, a broad brush for large areas.
  • Leave negative space to balance heavy and light marks.

Example: "Microcosm (2022)" featured tiny, precise ink lines that wove through expansive, gestural paint fields. The micro and macro elements created a sense of depth and movement, as if the viewer was traveling through a landscape of marks.

6. Let the Surface Dictate the Process

Wouters doesn’t dictate the surface—she responds to it, allowing the texture of the paper or canvas to influence her marks.

How to Apply This:

  • Experiment with different surfaces: Rough paper, textured canvas, or even found materials.
  • Work "into" the surface—scratching, tearing, or physically engaging with the medium.
  • Embrace imperfections—they often become part of the work’s character.

Example: In "Fractured Light (2021)", Wouters used heavily textured linen that captured paint in uneven pools. She scraped and dragged the paint, letting the canvas’s imperfections shape the final composition.

7. Use Repetition to Build Rhythm

Wouters often repeats motifs or gestures across a work, creating a sense of rhythm and flow.

How to Apply This:

  • Develop a "signature gesture"—a mark or technique you return to.
  • Vary the repetition—change scale, color, or intensity to keep it dynamic.
  • Think of it like music—each mark is a note in a larger composition.

Example: "Pulse (2023)" featured repeating, wavy lines that undulated across the canvas. By varying their thickness and color, Wouters created a hypnotic, rhythmic effect that guided the viewer’s eye.

8. Work Slowly—Lyrical Art is About Patience

Unlike fast, gestural abstraction (e.g., Pollock’s drip paintings), Wouters’ work often requires time, allowing layers to breathe and interact.

How to Apply This:

  • Take breaks between layers—let the paint dry and mature.
  • Step back often to assess what’s working and what’s not.
  • Accept that some marks may need to be "undone"—scraping or reworking can lead to new discoveries.

Example: "Ebb and Flow (2020)" took weeks to complete as Wouters layered, scraped, and reworked sections. The patient, evolving process is visible in the final work, making it feel alive and dynamic.

Real-World Examples of Cathalijn Wouters’ Work

To fully grasp Wouters’ approach, let’s dive deeper into a few of her notable pieces, exploring their technical execution and conceptual depth.

1. "Untitled (2022)" – The Dialogue Between Line and Color

This work begins with thin, wavering ink lines that seem almost like a map of an unseen landscape. Wouters then applies thin washes of color—cobalt blue, ochre, and burnt sienna—allowing the ink to bleed and interact with the paint.

Key Observations:

  • The ink lines act as a "skeleton" for the composition, guiding the flow of color.
  • She uses a palette knife to drag and scrape the paint, creating textural contrasts.
  • The final effect is neither purely a drawing nor a painting—it’s a hybrid where both mediums inform each other.

Why It Works: By keeping the drawing visible beneath the paint, Wouters creates a sense of depth and memory, as if the ink lines are echoes of past marks.

2. "Horizon Series (2020)" – From Sketch to Canvas

This series began as small, horizontal line studies on hot-pressed watercolor paper. Wouters then transferred these marks to canvas, amplifying them with paint while preserving the drawing’s delicacy.

Key Observations:

  • The initial sketches were loose and exploratory, almost like thoughts on paper.
  • On canvas, she expanded the lines into broader, more gestural strokes.
  • She used a limited palette (mostly black, white, and muted greens) to keep the focus on form and texture.

Why It Works: The transition from drawing to painting creates a narrative of growth—the small, intimate marks become monumental when scaled up.

3. "Fractured Light (2021)" – Texture as a Character

This piece was created on heavily textured linen, which captured paint in uneven pools. Wouters scraped and dragged the paint, letting the canvas’s imperfections shape the final composition.

Key Observations:

  • She used a mix of thin and thick paint, creating areas of transparency and opacity.
  • The texture of the canvas became a visual element, almost like a second layer of drawing.
  • She incorporated small, precise ink dots that contrasted with the loose paint.

Why It Works: The dialogue between the canvas’s texture and her marks makes the work feel tactile and immersive, as if the viewer could reach out and touch the surface.

4. "Pulse (2023)" – Rhythm Through Repetition

This work features repeating, wavy lines that undulate across the canvas. By varying their thickness and color, Wouters created a hypnotic, rhythmic effect.

Key Observations:

  • The lines start thin and delicate, then expand into broader strokes.
  • She used a limited color palette (mostly blues, purples, and whites) to keep the focus on movement.
  • The repetition creates a "breathing" effect, as if the marks are pulsing.

Why It Works: The rhythmic repetition guides the viewer’s eye in a continuous loop, making the work feel alive and dynamic.

Common Mistakes When Blurring Painting and Drawing—and How to Avoid Them

While Wouters’ approach is liberating, it’s easy to fall into bad habits when merging these mediums. Here are five common pitfalls and how to avoid them.

1. Over-Reliance on One Medium (Painting or Drawing)

The Mistake: Some artists default to painting and ignore the drawing elements, or vice versa. This kills the hybrid effect.

How to Fix It:

  • Start with a drawing and paint over it, or start with paint and draw into it.
  • Balance the two—if one dominates, step back and adjust.
  • Ask yourself: Does this feel like a conversation between two languages, or just one?

Example: If you’re painting but forgetting the drawing’s role, try adding a single ink line to ground the composition.

2. Neglecting the Surface’s Potential

The Mistake: Many artists treat the canvas or paper as a blank slate, ignoring its natural texture or imperfections.

How to Fix It:

  • Experiment with different surfaces—rough paper, textured canvas, even found materials.
  • Work "into" the surface—scratch, tear, or physically engage with it.
  • Embrace imperfections—they often add character to the work.

Example: If you’re working on smooth paper, try crinkling it slightly before painting to add subtle texture.
