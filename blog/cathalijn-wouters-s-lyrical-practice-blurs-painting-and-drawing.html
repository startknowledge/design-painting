Cathalijn Wouters’s Lyrical Practice: How Her Blurring of Painting and Drawing Redefines Contemporary Art

Introduction: The Rise of Lyrical Abstraction in Modern Art (2020–2024)

In the past five years, the art world has witnessed a surge in lyrical abstraction—a genre that merges painting and drawing in fluid, expressive ways, often defying traditional boundaries. Among contemporary artists pushing these boundaries, Cathalijn Wouters stands out for her intuitive, process-driven approach, where painting and drawing dissolve into a single, organic practice.

Wouters’s work doesn’t just blend mediums—it erases the distinction entirely, creating pieces that feel like dreamlike sketches one moment and textured paintings the next. Her technique is rooted in spontaneity, layering, and emotional resonance, making her a key figure in the post-digital, handmade art revival.

If you’re an artist, educator, or art enthusiast looking to explore how painting and drawing can coexist in a single practice, Wouters’s methods offer valuable insights. Below, we’ll break down her lyrical techniques, real-world applications, common pitfalls, and actionable strategies to integrate her approach into your own work.

Why Cathalijn Wouters’s Work Matters: The Philosophy Behind the Blurring

Wouters’s art isn’t just about technique—it’s about emotional storytelling. Her work often begins with loose, gestural marks that evolve into rich, layered compositions, where every stroke feels like a breath taken. This fluidity between drawing and painting reflects her belief that:

  • Art should feel alive, not rigid.
  • Process is as important as the final piece.
  • Constraints (like medium limitations) can spark creativity.

Her methods are particularly relevant in today’s fast-paced digital world, where handmade, imperfect art is gaining renewed appreciation. By studying her work, artists can learn how to embrace imperfection, experiment fearlessly, and let intuition guide their hands.

8 Actionable Strategies to Blur Painting and Drawing Like Cathalijn Wouters

Wouters’s practice isn’t about rigid rules—it’s about breaking them. However, her methods can be distilled into practical strategies that any artist can adapt. Here’s how to apply her philosophy to your own work:

1. Start with a "Scribble" or Gestural Underlayer

Wouters often begins with quick, uncontrolled marks—almost like automatic drawing—before building up layers. This loose foundation allows her to explore possibilities without overthinking.

How to apply it:

  • Use thin, fast strokes with a pencil, charcoal, or even a brush and ink.
  • Avoid trying to "draw" anything recognizable—let your hand move freely.
  • Work small first (sketchbook pages or small canvases) to build confidence.

Real-world example: Imagine starting a piece with random, overlapping lines that resemble bird tracks or storm clouds. Instead of erasing, you build over them with watercolor washes or oil paint. The initial marks become textural anchors, giving depth to the final work.

2. Embrace "Accidental" Marks as Intentional Elements

Wouters rarely erases mistakes—she integrates them. A smudge, a bleed, or a smudge becomes part of the visual language of the piece.

How to apply it:

  • Work with limited control—use tools that are hard to master (e.g., dry brush, sponging, or dripping).
  • Let mediums interact—mix watercolor with ink, or oil paint with pastels.
  • Document "failures"—some of her most striking works came from unintended overlaps.

Real-world example: Suppose you’re working with watercolor and ink, and the ink bleeds unpredictably. Instead of fixing it, you lean into the chaos, using the bleed as a natural divider between color fields. The result is a dynamic, organic composition that feels like a storm captured in motion.

3. Use Layering to Create Depth Without Structure

Wouters’s paintings often have multiple layers, but they don’t follow a strict underpainting → glazing approach. Instead, she jumps between mediums, letting each new layer react with the previous ones.

How to apply it:

  • Start with one medium (e.g., gouache on paper).
  • Add a second layer (e.g., oil paint) while the first is still wet.
  • Introduce textures (sand, salt, or even crumpled paper) between layers.

Real-world example: Picture a piece where charcoal is scratched into wet oil paint, creating raised, tactile lines. The oil paint then bleeds into the charcoal, making the marks glow from underneath. This interactive layering gives the work a three-dimensional quality without traditional relief techniques.

4. Work in Series to Explore Variations

Wouters often creates connected works—small studies that evolve into larger paintings. This serial approach helps her refine ideas without pressure.

How to apply it:

  • Make multiple small versions of a concept (e.g., 5x7" sketches).
  • Notice recurring motifs or emotional themes.
  • Use the most compelling sketches as a starting point for a larger piece.

Real-world example: Suppose you’re exploring the theme of "transience." You might start with five quick sketches—each showing water ripples, falling leaves, or melting ice. The strongest sketch becomes the base for a larger watercolor, where you expand on the same motifs but with more color and texture.

5. Incorporate "Found" Materials for Unexpected Textures

Wouters often disturbs her surfaces—scratching, tearing, or pressing objects into her work. This physical interaction adds unpredictability.

How to apply it:

  • Use household objects (sponge, fork, plastic wrap) to distort paint.
  • Tear paper and glue it into a painting.
  • Press leaves or fabric into wet mediums.

Real-world example: Imagine pressing crumpled aluminum foil into wet acrylic paint. The foil distorts the paint’s surface, creating shiny, reflective patches that contrast with the matte areas. The result is a sensory experience—your fingers can almost feel the texture of the work.

6. Let the Substrate Influence Your Work

Wouters doesn’t just paint on canvas—she works with the material’s quirks. A textured paper, a rough board, or even a found object becomes part of the visual language.

How to apply it:

  • Choose unconventional surfaces (burlap, cardboard, wood panels).
  • Embrace imperfections—cracks, warping, or uneven textures.
  • Use the substrate’s color as a starting point (e.g., painting on blue paper and building over it).

Real-world example: Suppose you’re working on a weathered wooden panel. Instead of sanding it smooth, you let the grain guide your brushstrokes. You might follow the wood’s lines with ink, or paint into the crevices, making the panel’s natural flaws part of the artwork.

7. Work Slowly, Then Fast—Or Vice Versa

Wouters’s process isn’t linear. She might spend hours on a single mark or cover a canvas in minutes. This contrast between control and spontaneity keeps her work dynamic.

How to apply it:

  • Alternate between slow, deliberate strokes and fast, gestural sweeps.
  • Set a timer for 5-minute bursts of uncontrolled marking.
  • Pause often to step back and reassess.

Real-world example: You might start by painstakingly layering thin washes of color (like a watercolorist), then suddenly slam a brush full of thick paint onto the paper. The jarring contrast between the delicate washes and bold strokes creates visual tension, making the piece feel alive.

8. Find Your "Lyrical" Voice Through Repetition

Wouters’s work has a recognizable rhythm—a way of repeating motifs while keeping them fresh. This comes from consistent practice and playing with variations.

How to apply it:

  • Develop a "signature mark" (e.g., a specific brushstroke, a recurring shape).
  • Vary it slightly in each piece (e.g., thicker, thinner, or more abstract).
  • Keep a sketchbook to track evolving patterns.

Real-world example: Imagine you always start a piece with a series of diagonal lines, but each time you change the spacing, the pressure, or the medium. In one work, the lines are tight and controlled; in another, they’re wild and overlapping. The consistency in theme makes your work cohesive, while the variations keep it exciting.

Common Mistakes When Blurring Painting and Drawing—and How to Avoid Them

Even the most intuitive artists make pitfalls when merging painting and drawing. Here’s how to navigate them without losing the lyrical essence of Wouters’s work.

Mistake 1: Over-Planning Before Starting

Problem: Trying to map out every stroke before beginning can kill spontaneity.

Solution:

  • Work in stages—start with loose marks, then refine.
  • Trust your intuition—if a mark feels "wrong," it might be exactly what the piece needs.
  • Use a "no-erase" rule for the first layer to embrace imperfection.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the Medium’s Limits

Problem: Some materials don’t mix well (e.g., oil paint on watercolor paper can bleed or warp).

Solution:

  • Test combinations on scrap paper first.
  • Choose compatible mediums (e.g., gouache + ink works better than oil + watercolor).
  • Accept that some experiments will fail—they’re part of the process.

Mistake 3: Forcing a "Final" Look Too Soon

Problem: Jumping to glazing or sealing before the piece is alive can stifle its energy.

Solution:

  • Work in layers, but don’t rush the middle stages.
  • Step away for days to see if the piece still feels dynamic.
  • Ask: "Does this feel like a conversation, or a lecture?" If it’s the latter, add more spontaneity.

Mistake 4: Neglecting the Emotional Core

Problem: Focusing only on technique can make the work feel cold or mechanical.

Solution:

  • Ask: "What emotion am I trying to express?" (e.g., melancholy, joy, urgency).
  • Use color and texture to reinforce that feeling.
  • If the piece feels "empty," add more gestural marks to bring it to life.

Mistake 5: Comparing Your Process to Wouters’s Too Closely

Problem: Trying to replicate her exact methods can stifle your personal voice.

Solution:

  • Study her work for inspiration, but develop your own rituals.
  • Mix her techniques with your own (e.g., her layering + your favorite medium).
  • Remember: Art is personal—what works for her may not work for you.

Example 1: The "Stormy Skies" Series

Concept: A set of small, gestural watercolor studies exploring atmospheric moods.

Process:

  • Start with thin, wispy lines (like cloud formations).
  • Add washed-out blues and grays to suggest dusk or dawn.
  • Scratch into wet paint with a needle to create "lightning cracks."
  • Leave some areas bare to mimic the unpredictability of weather.

Result: A series where each piece feels like a snapshot of a moment, with no two skies exactly alike.

Example 2: The "Fractured Landscapes" Painting

Concept: A large mixed-media piece blending drawing and painting to depict a fragmented world.

Process:

  • Begin with charcoal sketches of crumbling buildings or cracked earth.
  • Glue torn paper into the composition to add texture.
  • Paint over it with oil in thick, impasto strokes.
  • Scrape away paint in places to reveal the charcoal underneath, creating layers of history.

Result: A tactile, emotionally charged work that feels like a memory—both beautiful and haunting.

Example 3: The "Ink & Water" Experiment

Concept: A small, intimate piece using only ink and water to explore transience.

Process:

  • Drip ink onto wet watercolor paper.
  • Let it bleed naturally, then tilt the paper to distribute the ink.
  • Add thin washes of color where the ink has pooled.
  • Flick water onto the paper to create splatter effects.

Result: A delicate, almost ephemeral piece that feels like a breath held in time.

1. How do I know when to stop layering in a painting?

Answer: There’s no strict rule, but here’s how to decide:

  • Step back often—if the piece still feels "alive" when you add another layer, do it.
  • Trust your gut—if a new layer feels forced, it might be better to stop or refine instead.
  • Consider the medium’s drying time—some materials (like watercolor) dry quickly, while others (oil) take longer. If the piece loses its energy as it dries, you may have overworked it.
  • Try the "one more thing" test: Ask yourself, "If I add one more mark, will it make the piece stronger?" If the answer is no, you’re likely done.

2. Can I use digital tools alongside traditional methods?

Answer: Absolutely! Many artists (including Wouters-inspired practitioners) combine digital and analog for unique effects. Here’s how:

  • Use a tablet for gestural sketches, then transfer them to paper with ink or paint.
