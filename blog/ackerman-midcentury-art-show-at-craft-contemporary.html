Chapter 1: The Rise of Midcentury Modern—Why It’s Still Relevant in 2024

1.1 The Golden Age of Midcentury Design (1940s–1960s)

The midcentury modern era was defined by innovation, optimism, and a fusion of art and utility. Designers like Charles & Ray Eames, Eero Saarinen, Harry Bertoia, and Isamu Noguchi created pieces that were both functional and sculptural, often made from steel, wood, and organic materials.

  • Key characteristics of midcentury modern design:
    • Clean lines and organic shapes (inspired by nature)
    • Integration of art and furniture (e.g., Eames’ molded plywood chairs)
    • Use of warm, natural materials (teak, walnut, brass)
    • Minimalist yet expressive—form followed function, but with artistic flair

1.2 The Modern Revival (2010s–Present)

While midcentury design faded in popularity after the 1970s, it has seen a steady comeback due to: ✅ Nostalgia for the post-war era (a time of progress and creativity) ✅ Sustainability—many midcentury pieces are durable, handcrafted, and long-lasting ✅ Influence on contemporary designers (e.g., Studio Ko, Ronan & Erwan Bouroullec) ✅ Increased accessibility—vintage midcentury pieces are now widely available online and in galleries

Statistic Alert: According to The Design Trust’s 2023 Market Analysis, the vintage midcentury furniture market grew by 45% in the last five years, with Los Angeles emerging as a top hub for collectors.

1.3 Why Craft Contemporary is the Perfect Venue

Craft Contemporary, located in Los Angeles, is a nonprofit museum dedicated to contemporary craft. Their partnership with Ackerman—a leading dealer in vintage and contemporary design—creates a unique space where:

  • Art and design intersect
  • Emerging and established artists collaborate
  • The public engages with rare, historically significant pieces

Unlike traditional art museums, Craft Contemporary focuses on the tactile, the functional, and the handmade—making it the ideal stage for a midcentury art show.

Chapter 3: 8 Actionable Strategies to Engage with Midcentury Art

If you’re planning to attend the Ackerman show—or simply want to deepen your appreciation for midcentury design—here are eight practical strategies to make the most of the experience.

Strategy 1: Research Before You Go—Know the Icons

Before stepping into Craft Contemporary, familiarize yourself with key midcentury designers and their works. This will help you:

  • Identify rare pieces when you see them
  • Understand the craftsmanship behind each object
  • Appreciate the historical context of why certain designs became legendary

Action Step:

  • Watch documentaries like "The Eames: Design for the Masses" (2016)
  • Read "Midcentury Modern: Furniture of the Modern Movement" by Carol Lee Harrington
  • Follow @midcenturymodernmuseum on Instagram for daily insights

Strategy 2: Attend with a Purpose—Set Clear Goals

Not every visitor approaches an art show the same way. Define your objectives before attending:

  • For collectors: Look for authentic, rare pieces with provenance
  • For designers: Study materials, proportions, and ergonomics
  • For art lovers: Focus on the artistic expression behind functional objects
  • For students: Take notes on how midcentury design influenced contemporary work

Pro Tip: Bring a notebook or voice recorder to jot down observations.

Strategy 3: Engage with the Exhibit’s Interactive Elements

Many midcentury pieces are not just decorative—they’re interactive. At the Ackerman show, expect:

  • Touchable sculptures (like Bertoia’s wire works)
  • Light installations (Noguchi’s Akari lamps)
  • Furniture demonstrations (how an Eames chair is meant to be used)

Why It Matters: Midcentury design was made for people, not just for display. Sit on a chair, trace the lines of a sculpture, and feel the weight of a table—this physical engagement deepens your connection.

Strategy 4: Talk to the Experts—Ask the Right Questions

The Ackerman team and Craft Contemporary curators are goldmines of knowledge. Ask them:

  • "What makes this piece a true midcentury original?"
  • "How has this design influenced contemporary artists?"
  • "What’s the story behind this artist’s process?"
  • "Are there lesser-known midcentury designers worth exploring?"

Example Interaction: "I’ve seen the LCW Lounge Chair in many places, but what sets this particular version apart?" Response: "This one was reupholstered in 1968 by the Eameses themselves, using a rare olive-green wool that’s extremely difficult to find today."

Strategy 5: Take High-Quality Notes (Without a Phone)

While photography is usually restricted in galleries, sketching or note-taking helps you:

  • Remember details (colors, textures, dimensions)
  • Compare pieces (e.g., "This Saarinen Tulip Table is 1957 vs. this 1962 version—notice the leg design")
  • Document your thoughts on what resonates with you

Alternative: Use a small voice recorder to capture key insights.

Strategy 6: Explore the Surrounding LA Design Scene

Craft Contemporary is in Los Angeles, a mecca for midcentury design. After the show, visit:

  • The Getty Center (for midcentury architecture and art)
  • The Museum of Contemporary Art (MOCA) (for post-modern takes on midcentury)
  • Local dealers like Ackerman, Design Within Reach, or 1stDibs (for firsthand shopping)

Bonus: Many LA galleries host midcentury-themed pop-ups—check Instagram and Eventbrite for upcoming events.

Strategy 7: Join a Study Group or Online Community

Midcentury design has a dedicated following. Engage with:

  • Reddit’s r/midcenturymodern (for discussions and rare finds)
  • Facebook groups like "Midcentury Modern Collectors" (for networking)
  • Local meetups (e.g., LA Midcentury Modern Club)

Why It Helps: You’ll learn from others’ experiences, discover hidden gems, and stay updated on future exhibitions.

Strategy 8: Invest in a Midcentury Piece (If You’re Serious)

If you’re seriously considering collecting, follow these steps:

  1. Start with affordable pieces (e.g., Eames-style chairs, Noguchi tables)
  2. Verify authenticity (look for labels, provenance, and expert appraisals)
  3. Consider reupholstering (many classic pieces can be modernized while keeping their original structure)
  4. Buy from reputable dealers (Ackerman, 1stDibs, or established auction houses)

Warning: Avoid mass-produced knockoffs—authentic midcentury pieces appreciate in value.

Chapter 4: Real-World Examples—Iconic Pieces from Past Ackerman Shows

While we can’t show images, here’s a detailed breakdown of some of the most iconic and influential pieces that have graced past Ackerman exhibitions at Craft Contemporary.

4.1 The Eames LCW Lounge Chair & Ottoman (1950)

  • Designer: Charles & Ray Eames
  • Materials: Molded plywood, leather, and steel
  • Why It’s Legendary:
    • One of the most replicated yet revered pieces of midcentury design
    • The LCW (Lounge Chair Wood) was originally made for private clients before becoming mass-produced
    • The curved backrest and ergonomic design make it both a work of art and a comfortable seat
  • Fun Fact: The Eameses hand-sculpted the first prototypes using cardboard and plaster before moving to plywood.

4.2 Harry Bertoia’s Diamond Chair (1952)

  • Designer: Harry Bertoia
  • Materials: Welded steel and wire
  • Why It’s Legendary:
    • Made from scrap metal (Bertoia was a metalworker before becoming a sculptor)
    • The interwoven diamond pattern creates a light, airy effect while being extremely sturdy
    • Often called "the first modern chair" because it redefined what a chair could be
  • Controversy: Some collectors debate whether the original 1952 version (with blackened steel) is more valuable than later polished versions.

4.3 Isamu Noguchi’s Akari S1 Lamp (1951)

  • Designer: Isamu Noguchi
  • Materials: Woven paper, bamboo, and fabric
  • Why It’s Legendary:
    • Combines Japanese lantern traditions with Western modernism
    • The hand-woven paper shade creates a soft, diffused light—perfect for cozy, intimate spaces
    • Noguchi believed light should be an art form, not just functional
  • Rarity: Many early Akari lamps were destroyed in WWII—surviving pieces are highly sought after.

4.4 Eero Saarinen’s Tulip Table (1956)

  • Designer: Eero Saarinen
  • Materials: Cast aluminum and glass
  • Why It’s Legendary:
    • Rejected traditional legs in favor of a single, sculptural base
    • The organic, flowing shape was inspired by airplane wings and natural forms
    • Often called "the most beautiful table in the world" by design critics
  • Fun Fact: Saarinen tested the table’s stability by jumping on it—it held!

4.5 George Nakashima’s Walnut Table (1950s)

  • Designer: George Nakashima
  • Materials: Hand-cut walnut, with visible grain and knots
  • Why It’s Legendary:
    • Nakashima believed wood should tell its own story—he never sanded down flaws
    • His tables are handcrafted, one-of-a-kind pieces
    • Often compared to Japanese Zen philosophy—simple, pure, and deeply meaningful
  • Challenge: Authentic Nakashima tables can cost $50,000+, but forgeries are common—always verify with experts.
