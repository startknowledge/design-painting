The Historical Significance of Midcentury Modern Art

A Movement Born from Post-War Innovation

The midcentury modern era (roughly 1940–1970) was defined by technological advancements, mass production, and a rejection of ornate Victorian aesthetics. After World War II, designers like Charles & Ray Eames, Eero Saarinen, and George Nelson sought to create affordable, functional, and visually striking furniture and decor.

This movement wasn’t just about form follows function—it was a cultural shift. The Bauhaus influence, combined with American optimism, led to:

  • Open-plan living spaces (inspired by the Case Study Houses)
  • Integrated lighting and modular furniture (think Eames’ Lounge Chair)
  • Bold use of materials (teak, chrome, plywood, and fiberglass)

Why Craft Contemporary & Ackerman Are Key Players

Craft Contemporary, founded in 2009, has positioned itself as a bridge between contemporary and vintage design. Their partnership with Ackerman & Flatt, a dealer with over 50 years of experience, ensures that the Ackerman Midcentury Art Show features authentic, high-quality pieces—not mass-produced replicas.

8 Actionable Strategies for Engaging with the Ackerman Midcentury Art Show

1. Research Before You Go: Know the Artists & Their Impact

Before stepping into the gallery, familiarize yourself with the featured artists. Some of the most sought-after names include:

  • Charles & Ray Eames – Pioneers of molded plywood furniture (e.g., the Eames Lounge Chair)
  • Harry Bertoia – Known for his wire mesh sculptures and chairs
  • George Nelson – Designed the Marshall Plan table and Bubble Lamp
  • Eero Saarinen – Architect of the Tulip Chair and Womb Chair

Pro Tip: Use resources like the MoMA Design Storage Library or Ackerman & Flatt’s online archives to study their works in depth.

2. Set a Budget & Stick to It

Midcentury modern pieces can range from $5,000 for a vintage lamp to over $1 million for a rare Eames chair. To avoid impulse buys:

  • Define your budget (e.g., "I’ll spend no more than $20,000 on a statement piece")
  • Prioritize quality over quantity—a restored original is worth more than a reproduction
  • Consider financing options if you’re investing in high-end pieces

Example: A 1950s Knoll Saarinen Womb Chair (in excellent condition) sold for $180,000 at auction in 2022 (Christie’s). Would you pay that, or look for a lesser-known but equally stunning alternative?

3. Verify Authenticity & Provenance

Not all midcentury pieces are created equal. Fakes and reproductions flood the market, so:

  • Check for original labels, stamps, or manufacturer tags
  • Ask for documentation (e.g., original invoices, restoration records)
  • Consult experts—Ackerman & Flatt’s team can provide certified authenticity reports

Common Red Flags: ❌ No provenance (no paper trail of ownership) ❌ Unrealistic pricing (e.g., a "rare" Eames chair for $10,000) ❌ Signs of poor restoration (e.g., mismatched wood grain, cheap upholstery)

4. Understand the Investment Potential

Midcentury modern art is one of the best-performing asset classes in the design world. According to Artnet’s 2023 Market Report, vintage furniture appreciation rates 3–5% annually when held long-term.

How to maximize returns:

  • Buy rare, high-demand pieces (e.g., Eames, Bertoia, or Nelson designs)
  • Invest in restoration—a well-preserved piece appreciates faster
  • Diversify—mix iconic names with emerging midcentury artists

Real-World Example: A 1957 Knoll Tulip Table (designed by Eero Saarinen) sold for $1.3 million in 2021. If you had bought it in 2015 for $800,000, that’s a 62% return in just six years.

5. Attend Private Viewings for Insider Access

Many collectors gain exclusive access through private viewings. At the Ackerman show:

  • Schedule a one-on-one appointment with a dealer
  • Ask about off-market pieces—some works are only available to VIPs
  • Network with other collectors—you never know where the next great find will come from

Pro Tip: If you can’t attend in person, request a virtual consultation—some dealers offer high-quality video tours of available pieces.

6. Consider Functional vs. Decorative Pieces

Not all midcentury art is meant to be displayed. Some pieces are functional masterpieces that can be used daily:

  • Eames Lounge Chair (a luxury seating option)
  • Bertoia Diamond Chair (a statement piece for modern interiors)
  • Nelson Baby Grand Coffee Table (a practical yet elegant centerpiece)

Decorative pieces (like wall sculptures or lamps) may appreciate in value but don’t serve a daily purpose. Decide what fits your lifestyle and investment goals.

7. Explore Restoration & Customization Options

A well-restored midcentury piece can double in value. At the show:

  • Ask about restoration services—some dealers offer on-site refinishing
  • Consider upholstery updates (e.g., swapping vintage fabric for modern materials)
  • Look for pieces with potential—some may need minor repairs to reach their full value

Example: A 1960s Mid-Century Modern sofa with faded upholstery could be restored for $3,000, then resold for $15,000+ after a professional refresh.

8. Leverage Social Proof & Expert Opinions

Before making a purchase, seek validation from trusted sources:

  • Follow midcentury design influencers (e.g., @midcenturymodern on Instagram)
  • Join collector forums (e.g., Reddit’s r/MidCenturyModern)
  • Consult appraisers—some dealers offer free initial valuations

Real-World Example: A 1955 Herman Miller No. 75 Chair (a lesser-known but highly collectible piece) was appraised at $45,000 after a collector shared its details in a Facebook design group, leading to multiple offers.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Engaging with Midcentury Art

Mistake #1: Buying Without Researching the Artist

Problem: Some collectors fall in love with a piece’s aesthetic without knowing its historical value. Solution: Always verify the designer—a knockoff "Eames chair" is worth fractions of a real one.

Mistake #2: Overpaying for "Vintage" Pieces

Problem: Dealers sometimes inflate prices on common or damaged items. Solution: Compare prices across platforms (e.g., 1stDibs, Artsy, Sotheby’s) before bidding.

Mistake #3: Ignoring Structural Integrity

Problem: A beautiful but unstable piece is a ticking time bomb. Solution: Test for wobbling, loose joints, or wood rot before purchasing.

Mistake #4: Not Negotiating

Problem: Many buyers pay full price without realizing midcentury dealers often negotiate. Solution: Start with a lower offer (e.g., 10–20% below asking) and see if they counter.

Mistake #5: Buying for Trends, Not Timelessness

Problem: Some pieces are fad-driven (e.g., pastel-colored 1960s lamps). Solution: Invest in neutral, versatile designs that won’t go out of style.

2. What’s the best way to finance a high-end midcentury purchase?

Answer: Financing options include:

  • Personal loans (fixed interest rates, but not tax-deductible)
  • Home equity lines of credit (HELOC) (lower rates, but secured by your home)
  • Art financing companies (e.g., ArtBullion, ArtLending) that offer specialized loans for collectors
  • Payment plans (some dealers allow monthly installments)

Pro Tip: If you’re investing long-term, a personal loan may be the most flexible option.

3. Can I restore a midcentury piece myself, or should I hire a professional?

Answer:

  • Minor repairs (e.g., tightening screws, polishing wood) can be done DIY.
  • Major restorations (e.g., upholstery replacement, structural repairs) should be left to professionals to avoid damaging the piece’s value.
  • Ackerman & Flatt often recommend trusted restorers—ask for referrals.

4. How do I transport a large midcentury piece safely?

Answer:

  • Use professional movers (they have specialized crating for furniture).
  • DIY transport? Rent a large truck + padding (bubble wrap, foam) to prevent scratches or dents.
  • Avoid driving on rough roads—midcentury pieces are delicate.

5. Is midcentury modern art a good long-term investment?

Answer: Yes, but with conditions:

  • Iconic designers (Eames, Saarinen, Nelson) appreciate fastest.
  • Rarity matters—limited-edition or one-of-a-kind pieces perform best.
  • Market trends fluctuate, but historically, midcentury holds value better than contemporary art.
  • Diversify—don’t put all your money into one piece or one artist.
