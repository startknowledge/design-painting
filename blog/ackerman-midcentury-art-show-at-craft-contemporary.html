The Legacy of Midcentury Modern: A Brief Historical Context

Before diving into the Ackerman show, it’s essential to understand why midcentury modern design still captivates us today.

The Birth of a Movement: Post-War Optimism and Innovation

After World War II, the world entered an era of economic prosperity, technological advancement, and creative freedom. Architects and designers like Charles & Ray Eames, Eero Saarinen, and Harry Bertoia rejected the rigid lines of traditionalism in favor of organic shapes, open spaces, and mass-produced luxury.

Key characteristics of midcentury modern design include:

  • Clean, uncluttered lines (inspired by Bauhaus principles)
  • Integration of art and function (e.g., Eames’ molded plywood chairs)
  • Use of natural materials (teak, walnut, brass, and glass)
  • Bold, geometric patterns (think Alexander Girard’s textiles)
  • A rejection of ornamentation in favor of pure, essential forms

The Market Today: Why Midcentury Is Still Hot

According to Artnet’s 2023 Market Report, midcentury modern furniture and decor have seen a 38% increase in auction prices over the past five years. Why?

  • Investment appeal: Midcentury pieces hold their value exceptionally well.
  • Sustainability: Many vintage pieces are durable, high-quality, and ethically sourced compared to fast furniture.
  • Design versatility: From sleek Scandinavian influences to psychedelic pop art, midcentury styles adapt to modern interiors seamlessly.

The Ackerman Midcentury Art Show taps into this cultural resurgence, offering a curated selection of pieces that bridge the gap between history and contemporary relevance.

Why Craft Contemporary Is the Perfect Venue for This Show

Craft Contemporary, located in Los Angeles, is a nonprofit gallery and maker’s space dedicated to handcrafted art and design. Founded in 2001, it has become a hub for experimental artists, designers, and collectors, making it an ideal host for the Ackerman show.

Key Reasons This Venue Elevates the Exhibition

  1. Curatorial Excellence – Craft Contemporary’s team specializes in midcentury and modern design, ensuring a cohesive, thoughtfully selected display.
  2. Community Engagement – The gallery hosts workshops, lectures, and artist talks, enhancing the visitor experience beyond just viewing.
  3. Diverse Exhibitions – Past shows have featured Eames-inspired textiles, Bertoia’s sculptural wirework, and emerging midcentury revivalists, setting the stage for Ackerman’s unique offerings.
  4. Sustainable Focus – Craft Contemporary emphasizes ethical production and craftsmanship, aligning perfectly with midcentury’s anti-mass-production ethos.
  5. Strategic Location – Situated in Los Angeles’ Arts District, it attracts designers, architects, and high-net-worth collectors who drive the market.

What to Expect at the Ackerman Show

The exhibition will feature:

  • Vintage pieces from Ackerman’s extensive archive (including rare Eames, Saarinen, and Mid-Century Modern Furniture Co. items)
  • Emerging artists reinterpreting midcentury aesthetics with modern techniques

  • Exclusive auctions and consignment opportunities for serious collectors

8 Actionable Strategies to Make the Most of the Ackerman Midcentury Art Show

Attending an exhibition like this is more than just browsing—it’s an opportunity to learn, network, and potentially acquire rare pieces. Here’s how to maximize your experience:

1. Research Before You Go: Know the Key Players

Before stepping into Craft Contemporary, familiarize yourself with the artists and designers featured:

  • Charles & Ray Eames – Pioneers of molded plywood and organic modernism.
  • Eero Saarinen – Known for the Tulip Chair and fluid, sculptural forms.
  • Harry Bertoia – A master of wire sculpture and sound-based art.
  • Alexander Girard – The "father of midcentury textiles," blending African and Southwestern influences.

Pro Tip: Use resources like:

  • The Midcentury Modern Furniture Guide (by Adam Reed Tucker)
  • Sotheby’s Midcentury Auction Archives
  • Craft Contemporary’s past exhibition catalogs (available on their website)

2. Set a Budget (And Stick to It)

Midcentury pieces can range from $500 for a small sculpture to $500,000+ for a rare Eames chair. To avoid impulse buys:

  • Allocate a daily budget (e.g., $2,000 for the weekend).
  • Prioritize pieces based on condition, rarity, and resale potential.
  • Consider consignment if you’re unsure—some galleries offer payment plans.

Common Mistake: Buying based on emotional attachment rather than long-term value. Always ask: ✅ Is this piece in excellent condition? ✅ Does it align with current market trends? ✅ Can I resell it if my tastes change?

3. Attend the Pre-Show Lectures and Workshops

Craft Contemporary often hosts expert-led discussions before major exhibitions. These sessions provide:

  • Insights from collectors on how to spot fakes vs. authentic pieces.
  • Restoration tips from furniture conservators.
  • Market trends from auctioneers and dealers.

Example: In past years, they’ve featured Eames expert John T. Dorsey, who shared how to authenticate vintage Eames chairs by examining plywood grain patterns and metal welds.

4. Network with Dealers, Collectors, and Artists

The best deals often come from private conversations, not just the open floor:

  • Introduce yourself to dealers—they may offer exclusive off-site viewings.
  • Connect with fellow collectors—group buying can lead to discounts on bulk purchases.
  • Engage with artists—some may offer custom commissions based on your space.

Pro Tip: Bring business cards and follow up via LinkedIn or Instagram with a note like: "Loved your [Piece Name] at the Ackerman show—would love to discuss potential collaborations!"

5. Take Notes (And Photos—Strategically)

While you can’t photograph the entire show, you can:

  • Sketch key pieces in a notebook (helps with memory and future reference).
  • Note down dimensions, materials, and estimated values (useful for resale).
  • Use your phone for notes—jot down artist names, dealer contacts, and standout details.

Avoid: Taking high-res photos of entire rooms (many galleries have strict photography policies).

6. Understand the Market: Know What Sells

Not all midcentury pieces are created equal. Focus on high-demand categories:

  • Eames Lounge Chairs (originals sell for $50K–$200K+).
  • Bertoia Diamond Chair (prices range from $10K–$50K).
  • Saarinen Tulip Tables (vintage models go for $20K–$100K).
  • Alexander Girard Textiles (rare patterns can exceed $1,000 per yard).

Pro Tip: Check 1stDibs, Sotheby’s, and Phillips for recent sales data to gauge fair pricing.

7. Consider Alternative Ways to Acquire Pieces

Not everyone can afford high-end originals. Explore:

  • Reproductions (from Knoll, Herman Miller, or Thonet—often 30–50% cheaper).
  • Consignment deals (some dealers offer payment plans or trade-ins).
  • Emerging artists (Craft Contemporary may feature modern interpretations at lower price points).

Example: The Ackerman show might include a reproduction of an Eames side chair for $1,500, while an original could cost $10,000+.

8. Plan for the Aftermath: How to Display and Preserve Your Purchase

Buying midcentury art isn’t just about owning it—it’s about showcasing it properly:

  • Lighting matters: Use soft, adjustable LED lighting to prevent fading.
  • Climate control: Store pieces in 60–70°F with 40–50% humidity to avoid warping.
  • Regular cleaning: Dust with a soft microfiber cloth (avoid harsh chemicals).
  • Documentation: Take before-and-after photos of any restorations.

Pro Tip: If you’re not sure how to display a piece, ask the gallery for styling advice—some may offer free consultation services.

Real-World Examples: Standout Pieces from Past Ackerman Shows

While we can’t show images, here’s a detailed breakdown of the types of pieces you might encounter—based on past Ackerman exhibitions and Craft Contemporary’s past shows.

1. The Eames Lounge Chair & Ottoman (1950)

  • Why it’s iconic: Designed by Charles & Ray Eames, this piece redefined luxury seating with its molded plywood shell, leather upholstery, and brass hardware.
  • What to look for:
    • Original 1950s models (with hand-stamped "Eames" labels).
    • Condition checks: Look for cracks in the plywood, faded leather, or loose screws.
    • Price range: $50,000–$200,000+ (originals).
  • Modern alternative: Knoll reproductions (starting at $12,000).

2. Harry Bertoia’s "Diamond Chair" (1952)

  • Why it’s iconic: Made from welded steel wire, this chair is both a sculpture and a functional piece.
  • What to look for:
    • Original Bertoia label (forged labels are common fakes).
    • Wire integrity—check for rust or weak welds.
    • Price range: $10,000–$50,000 (depending on size and condition).
  • Fun fact: Bertoia also designed sound sculptures, some of which may be featured in the show.

3. Alexander Girard’s "Marimekko-Inspired Textile" (1960s)

  • Why it’s iconic: Girard blended African, Southwestern, and Scandinavian influences into bold, graphic patterns.
  • What to look for:
    • Original fabric tags (many reproductions lack these).
    • Colorfastness—hold fabric up to light to check for bleeding.
    • Price range: $500–$3,000 per yard (rare prints can exceed $5,000).
  • Modern use: Perfect for dining chairs, curtains, or wall hangings.

4. Eero Saarinen’s "Tulip Table" (1956)

  • Why it’s iconic: Its single-base design revolutionized dining furniture.
  • What to look for:
    • Original brass or chrome finish (oxidized metal is a red flag).
    • Glass integrity—check for cracks or delamination.
    • Price range: $20,000–$100,000 (depending on size).
  • Fun fact: Saarinen also designed the Gateway Arch in St. Louis—his architectural influence is everywhere.

5. Mid-Century Modern Furniture Co. "Wiggle Chair" (1960s)

  • Why it’s iconic: A playful, organic take on seating, often made from teak or walnut.
  • What to look for:
    • Hand-carved details—mass-produced versions lack artisan touches.
    • Wood stability—check for warping or loose joints.
    • Price range: $2,000–$15,000 (depending on rarity).

6. A Rare "Pop Art" Collage by Robert Rauschenberg (1960s)

  • Why it’s iconic: While not strictly midcentury, Rauschenberg’s combines (mixing found objects and paint) bridge the era’s experimental spirit.
  • What to look for:
    • Original paper and glue (reproductions often use cheap materials).
    • Artist’s signature (forged signatures are common).
    • Price range: $5,000–$50,000 (depending on size and provenance).
