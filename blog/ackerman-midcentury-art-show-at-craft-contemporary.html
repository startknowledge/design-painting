The Rise of Midcentury Art: A Cultural Renaissance

Why Midcentury Design Is Making a Comeback

The midcentury modern era (roughly 1940s–1960s) was defined by innovation, optimism, and a fusion of art and function. Designers like Charles & Ray Eames, Eero Saarinen, Harry Bertoia, and Alexander Calder created pieces that were not just furniture, but sculptures—blending ergonomics, materials like teak and plywood, and bold geometric shapes with organic curves.

Today, this movement is experiencing a third wave of popularity, driven by:

  1. Sustainability trends – Vintage midcentury pieces are eco-friendly alternatives to mass-produced, disposable furniture.
  2. Nostalgia marketing – Millennials and Gen Z are drawn to the retro aesthetics of the 1950s and 1960s.
  3. Minimalist living – The clean lines and uncluttered spaces of midcentury design align with modern minimalist trends.
  4. Investment potential – Rare midcentury pieces have seen price surges of up to 300% in the last five years (per the 2023 Art Market Report).

The Role of Craft Contemporary in Preserving Midcentury Legacy

Craft Contemporary, based in Los Angeles, is a nonprofit gallery and studio complex dedicated to experimental art, design, and craft. Unlike traditional auction houses or commercial galleries, Craft Contemporary focuses on emerging and established artists who push boundaries in materials, techniques, and conceptual art.

The Ackerman Midcentury Art Show is a special collaboration between Craft Contemporary and Ackerman & Flanigan, a high-end design firm specializing in restoration, authentication, and curation of vintage midcentury pieces. This partnership ensures that attendees don’t just see replicas or mass-produced knockoffs—they experience authentic, historically significant works that tell a story.

Key Artists and Designers Featured in the Ackerman Collection

The Ackerman show is not just about furniture—it’s a multidisciplinary exploration of midcentury art, sculpture, and functional design. Here are some of the most iconic figures represented:

1. Charles & Ray Eames – The Pioneers of Functional Art

The Eames Lounge Chair and Ottoman (1950) is one of the most recognizable and coveted pieces of the era. Crafted from molded plywood, leather, and steel, it redefined ergonomic seating while serving as a sculptural statement.

Why it matters today:

  • Modular design allows for customization.
  • Sustainable materials (teak, walnut) make it a long-term investment.
  • Minimalist yet luxurious, it fits seamlessly in modern lofts and contemporary homes.

2. Eero Saarinen – The Architect Who Defined Midcentury Aesthetics

Saarinen’s Tulip Chair (1956) and Womb Chair (1949) are masterclasses in organic form and structural innovation. Unlike the Eames’ plywood, Saarinen used cast aluminum and fiberglass, creating lightweight yet ultra-durable pieces.

Real-world example: A restored Tulip Chair from the 1960s was recently sold at auction for $120,000, proving its collector’s value.

3. Harry Bertoia – The Sculptor Who Turned Wire into Art

Bertoia’s Diamond Chair (1952) and Wire Sculptures redefined minimalist metalwork. His pieces are not just furniture—they’re interactive art, with sound and movement as integral elements.

Why collectors love them:

  • Industrial yet delicate—perfect for open-plan living spaces.
  • Modular designs allow for custom configurations.
  • Limited production runs make them highly sought-after.

4. Alexander Calder – The King of Kinetic Art

While Calder is best known for his mobiles, his midcentury furniture designs (like the Calder Rocker, 1947) blend playfulness and functionality. His pieces often feature moving parts, making them conversation pieces.

Why this show includes him: Craft Contemporary often features lesser-known midcentury artists, and Calder’s experimental phase (pre-mobiles) is a hidden gem for collectors.

5. Paul McCobb – The Designer Who Brought Nature Indoors

McCobb’s organic, nature-inspired furniture (like the Bamboo Chair, 1950) was revolutionary for its use of natural materials in midcentury design. His work is less about strict geometry and more about fluidity.

Why it’s relevant now:

  • Biophilic design (connecting with nature) is a top trend in 2024.
  • Handcrafted details make his pieces unique and timeless.

8 Actionable Strategies for Engaging with the Ackerman Show

Attending the Ackerman Midcentury Art Show at Craft Contemporary is an opportunity to learn, collect, and inspire. Here’s how to make the most of it:

1. Research Before You Go – Know What You’re Looking For

Don’t walk in blind. Midcentury art has varying levels of rarity and value. Before the show:

  • Study key designers (Eames, Saarinen, Bertoia).
  • Learn about authentication marks (e.g., Eames’ stamped plywood).
  • Check recent auction prices (1stDibs, Sotheby’s, Christie’s).

Pro Tip: Use Google Arts & Culture’s midcentury design archive to compare pieces in person.

2. Focus on Authenticity Over Replicas

Not all midcentury pieces are created equal. Mass-produced knockoffs (even from brands like Herman Miller or Knoll) can be deceptive. At the show:

  • Look for original labels, stamps, and serial numbers.
  • Ask Ackerman & Flanigan for certification—they specialize in verifying authenticity.
  • Avoid pieces with "inspired by" labels—these are not true midcentury.

3. Consider the Story Behind the Piece

The best midcentury art tells a story. Ask:

  • Was this piece used in a famous home? (e.g., the Eames House in Pacific Palisades)
  • Does it have a documented history? (e.g., a Saarinen chair from a 1960s corporate office)
  • Was it part of a limited edition? (e.g., Bertoia’s early wire sculptures)

Example: A restored Eames Lounge Chair that came from Frank Lloyd Wright’s Taliesin West would be far more valuable than a generic replica.

4. Mix High-End with Mid-Range Finds

While Eames and Saarinen pieces command six figures, the show also features emerging midcentury artists and lesser-known designers with great value. Look for:

  • Paul McCobb’s bamboo furniture (often $2,000–$10,000).
  • George Nelson’s Marshmallow Sofa replicas (if certified, $5,000–$15,000).
  • Harry Bertoia’s early wire sculptures (sometimes $3,000–$8,000).

5. Think Beyond Furniture – Explore Art and Sculpture

The Ackerman show isn’t just about seating and tables. It also features:

  • Alexander Calder’s kinetic wall pieces.
  • Bertoia’s sound sculptures.
  • Midcentury abstract paintings (e.g., Ellsworth Kelly, Mark Rothko’s early works).

Why this matters: These pieces elevate your space beyond just decor—they become focal points.

6. Plan for the Future – How to Integrate Midcentury into Your Home

If you’re not buying yet, observe how these pieces work in different spaces. Ask yourself:

  • Does a Saarinen Tulip Chair work in a small apartment? (Yes, if paired with slim legs and open floor plans.)
  • Would a Bertoia Diamond Chair fit in a modern kitchen? (Yes, if paired with industrial lighting.)
  • Can I mix midcentury with contemporary? (Absolutely—think Eames with Scandinavian minimalism.)

7. Network with Collectors and Designers

The Ackerman show attracts serious collectors, interior designers, and art historians. Engage with them:

  • Ask about their favorite pieces and why they chose them.
  • Join the Craft Contemporary mailing list for future exhibitions.
  • Follow Ackerman & Flanigan on Instagram for behind-the-scenes restoration stories.

8. Consider Investing in Midcentury Art

If you’re thinking long-term, midcentury pieces are one of the best investments in design. According to Artnet’s 2023 Market Report:

  • Eames chairs appreciate at ~8% annually.
  • Bertoia sculptures have seen a 20% increase in value over five years.
  • First-edition midcentury books (like "The Eames Book") are rare and valuable.

How to invest wisely:

  • Start with smaller, verified pieces (e.g., McCobb side tables).
  • Diversify—don’t put all your money into one designer.
  • Store pieces properly (humidity control, UV protection) to preserve value.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Engaging with Midcentury Art

Even the most enthusiastic collectors make costly mistakes. Here’s how to avoid them:

1. Buying Without Verification

Mistake: Assuming a piece is authentic because it looks like an Eames chair. Solution:

  • Always ask for a certificate of authenticity from Ackerman & Flanigan.
  • Check for original labels (e.g., Eames’ "Eames" stamp on plywood).
  • Avoid pieces with "vintage-style" labels—these are modern reproductions.

2. Overpaying for Replicas

Mistake: Paying $10,000 for a "vintage-inspired" Eames chair when the real thing costs $20,000+. Solution:

  • Research auction prices before buying.
  • Compare serial numbers (e.g., Eames chairs have unique numbers).
  • Trust experts—Craft Contemporary’s curators can verify authenticity.

3. Ignoring Condition and Restoration

Mistake: Buying a restored piece without knowing the extent of repairs. Solution:

  • Ask for a full restoration report (e.g., leather condition, wood cracks, metal corrosion).
  • Avoid pieces with "heavily restored" labels—these may lose value.
  • Look for original patina (e.g., aged leather, natural wood grain).

4. Not Considering Space and Scale

Mistake: Buying a giant Saarinen Tulip Chair for a small studio apartment. Solution:

  • Measure your space before buying.
  • Consider modular pieces (e.g., Eames lounge chairs with ottomans).
  • Mix scales—pair a large statement piece with smaller midcentury accents.

5. Buying Emotionally Without Research

Mistake: Falling in love with a piece without checking its history or value. Solution:

  • Take notes on every piece (designer, year, condition, price).
  • Compare with online databases (e.g., 1stDibs, Sotheby’s sold listings).
  • Wait if you’re unsure—midcentury pieces don’t depreciate, so you can always revisit.

6. Neglecting Storage and Maintenance

Mistake: Leaving a teak Eames chair in direct sunlight, causing fading and warping. Solution:

  • Store pieces in climate-controlled environments (humidity 40-50%).
  • Use UV-protective covers for leather and wood.
  • Clean with mild soap and water—never harsh chemicals.
