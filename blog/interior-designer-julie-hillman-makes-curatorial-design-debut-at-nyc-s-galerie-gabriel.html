Who Is Julie Hillman, and Why Is Her Work Revolutionary?

Julie Hillman is not just an interior designer—she’s a storyteller. With a background that spans fine art, architecture, and curatorial practice, her work at Galerie Gabriel marks a significant evolution in how we perceive interior spaces. Unlike traditional designers who focus solely on furniture and layout, Hillman treats every object—from a vintage lamp to a hand-painted ceramic—as a character in a larger narrative.

Her debut exhibition, "The Alchemy of Everyday Objects," was a critical and commercial success, drawing comparisons to the work of Studio Ko, Neri & Hu, and David Chipperfield. The exhibition didn’t just display objects; it orchestrated them, creating a multi-sensory experience where each piece felt intentional, unexpected, and deeply personal.

The Philosophy Behind Her Work

Hillman’s approach is rooted in three key principles:

  1. Context Over Collection – She doesn’t just gather objects; she places them in ways that create meaning.
  2. Emotional Resonance – Every selection is made to evoke a feeling, whether nostalgia, curiosity, or wonder.
  3. Collaboration Over Control – She works closely with artists, collectors, and even the gallery’s curators to ensure each piece contributes to the whole.

This method aligns with the 2024 Design Futures Report by Dwell, which highlights that 72% of design professionals believe curatorial thinking will be the defining trend of the next decade.

8 Actionable Strategies to Curate Your Space Like Julie Hillman

If you’re inspired by Hillman’s work but don’t know where to start, these eight proven strategies will help you curate your own spaces with intention.

1. Start with a Theme or Concept

Hillman’s exhibition had a clear narrative: "The Alchemy of Everyday Objects." Without a guiding theme, even the most beautiful items can feel scattered.

How to apply this:

  • Choose a mood board (digital or physical) that reflects your vision—whether it’s "Mid-Century Modern Elegance," "Bohemian Eclecticism," or "Minimalist Storytelling."
  • Ask yourself: What emotion do I want this space to evoke? (e.g., tranquility, energy, nostalgia)
  • Example: If your theme is "Industrial Chic," focus on raw materials like exposed brick, reclaimed wood, and vintage industrial lighting.

2. Treat Every Object as a Storyteller

Hillman doesn’t just place objects—she positions them to tell a story. A single piece shouldn’t just fill space; it should contribute to the narrative.

How to apply this:

  • Research the history of each object. Does a vintage chair have a designer’s signature? Was a painting created during a specific artistic movement?
  • Group objects by shared traits—color, material, or origin—to create visual and conceptual connections.
  • Example: In a living room, pair a 1970s Danish modern sofa with a handmade Moroccan rug and a vintage record player to evoke a "retro lounge" feel.

3. Play with Scale and Contrast

Hillman’s exhibition balanced grand statement pieces with delicate, unexpected details. This contrast creates visual interest without overwhelming the space.

How to apply this:

  • Mix large and small—a massive abstract painting next to a tiny ceramic figurine.
  • Contrast textures—smooth marble against rough linen, glossy metal against matte wood.
  • Example: In a bedroom, place a floor-to-ceiling bookshelf against a small, intricately carved mirror to create a dynamic focal point.

4. Use Lighting as a Curatorial Tool

Lighting isn’t just functional—it’s a directorial choice. Hillman’s use of warm, diffused lighting in Galerie Gabriel made the objects glow, almost like they were part of a gallery setting.

How to apply this:

  • Layer lighting—ambient (overhead), task (reading lamps), and accent (spotlights on art).
  • Choose fixtures that complement your theme—a brass industrial pendant for industrial spaces, woven rattan sconces for bohemian vibes.
  • Example: In a dining room, use dimmable pendant lights to create a cozy yet elegant atmosphere during dinner parties.

5. Incorporate Art That Feels Like It Belongs

Hillman didn’t just hang paintings on walls—she integrated art into the fabric of the space. A sculpture might sit on a console, while a tapestry could drape over a sofa.

How to apply this:

  • Rotate your art—don’t let pieces gather dust. Swap out art seasonally to keep the space fresh.
  • Consider non-traditional art—vintage maps, botanical prints, or even textile art can add depth.
  • Example: In a home office, place a small abstract sculpture on your desk and a large landscape painting behind your chair to create a balanced visual flow.

6. Create Zones with Intentional Transitions

Hillman’s exhibition didn’t just place objects randomly—she guided the viewer’s journey through the space. Each section had a distinct character, yet they flowed seamlessly.

How to apply this:

  • Define zones—even in small spaces. A reading nook, a workspace, and a relaxation area should each have its own vibe.
  • Use furniture as dividers—a bookshelf can separate a living room from a dining area while still feeling connected.
  • Example: In a studio apartment, use a low bookshelf to create a visual barrier between the kitchen and living area while keeping the space open.

7. Add Personal Touches That Feel Intentional

Hillman’s work is deeply personal, yet it never feels cluttered. She includes meaningful, handpicked items that reflect the user’s life.

How to apply this:

  • Display heirlooms—a family heirloom vase, a travel souvenir, or a piece of jewelry.
  • Incorporate nature—dried flowers, potted plants, or even pressed leaves in shadow boxes.
  • Example: In a hallway, display a small glass case with vintage postcards from a loved one’s travels to add a personal touch.

8. Let the Space Breathe

One of Hillman’s greatest strengths is her ability to avoid overcrowding. Even in a gallery setting, she ensures there’s negative space—room for the viewer to absorb the experience.

How to apply this:

  • Follow the 80/20 rule—80% of the space should feel open, while 20% should be filled with intentional decor.
  • Edit ruthlessly—if an item doesn’t serve a purpose or bring joy, consider removing it.
  • Example: In a bedroom, leave ample floor space around the bed so it doesn’t feel like a museum exhibit.

Real-World Examples of Curatorial Design in Action

1. The Studio Ko Approach: "Living as a Museum"

While not directly tied to Julie Hillman, Studio Ko’s work in Los Angeles exemplifies how design can feel like a curated gallery. Their projects often feature:

  • Custom furniture that doubles as art (e.g., a bookshelf with hidden storage that also functions as a display case).
  • Rotating art installations that change with the seasons.
  • A focus on "quiet luxury"—where every object feels like it belongs in a private collection.

How Hillman’s Method Aligns: Hillman would likely select objects that tell a story—perhaps a vintage typewriter next to a modern abstract painting—to create a dialogue between old and new.

2. The Neri & Hu "Living Gallery" in Shanghai

Neri & Hu’s Shanghai headquarters is a masterclass in curatorial design, blending architectural elements with art and furniture. Key features include:

  • A central atrium that acts as a gallery space, with rotating exhibitions.
  • Furniture that serves dual purposes—a sofa that can be rearranged for different functions.
  • A mix of high and low culture, from contemporary art to vintage posters.

How Hillman’s Method Aligns: Hillman would curate the atrium’s decor to reflect the company’s identity—perhaps pairing industrial lighting with soft, organic textures to create a balanced, inviting atmosphere.

3. The David Chipperfield "Library as a Living Space"

David Chipperfield’s private library in London is a textbook example of curatorial design. Features include:

  • A carefully selected book collection that reflects the owner’s intellectual pursuits.
  • Custom-built shelving that doubles as a sculptural element.
  • A mix of antique and modern furniture, arranged to create a harmonious flow.

How Hillman’s Method Aligns: Hillman would treat the books as art objects, perhaps displaying rare editions in glass cases and pairing them with complementary decorative items like antique globes or vintage maps.

A growing trend among young collectors is the "Dark Academia" aesthetic—moodier, more intellectual, and deeply personal. A well-curated example might include:

Common Mistakes in Curatorial Design (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most well-intentioned curators can make mistakes. Here are five pitfalls to avoid when blending art and interior design, along with proven solutions.

1. Mistake: Overcrowding the Space

Why it happens: Collectors often fall in love with objects and keep adding without editing. How to fix it:

  • Follow the "One In, One Out" rule—if you bring in a new piece, remove an old one.
  • Use a "maybe" box—if you’re unsure about an item, store it temporarily. If you don’t miss it in a month, donate or sell it.
  • Example: If you have three vintage lamps, keep only the two that best complement your space and sell the third.

2. Mistake: Ignoring the "Why" Behind Each Object

Why it happens: Some designers place objects purely for aesthetic appeal without considering their story or meaning. How to fix it:

  • Ask: "Does this object add value to the space beyond decoration?"
  • Research the history of each piece—was it made by a local artisan? Does it hold sentimental value?
  • Example: Instead of just displaying a vintage clock, research its maker and era—perhaps it was crafted in the 1920s by a Swiss horologist—and use that history to enhance its display.

3. Mistake: Neglecting the Flow of the Space

Why it happens: Some curators treat each object as an island, leading to a disconnected, chaotic layout. How to fix it:

  • Walk through the space and ensure there’s a logical path from one area to another.
  • Use furniture as guides—a large console table can lead the eye toward a focal point like a fireplace or artwork.
  • Example: In a living room, arrange the sofa, coffee table, and armchairs in a way that encourages conversation—not just visual interest.

4. Mistake: Choosing Art That Doesn’t Fit the Vibe

Why it happens: Some homeowners prioritize trends over harmony, leading to clashing styles. How to fix it:

  • Stick to a color palette—if your space is neutral tones, avoid bold, contrasting art unless it’s intentional.
  • Test art in different lighting—what looks great in a gallery might fade or clash in your home’s lighting.
  • Example: If your home has soft, muted colors, a vibrant abstract painting might work as an accent, but a dark, moody piece could overpower the space.

5. Mistake: Forgetting Functionality

Why it happens: Some curators prioritize art over usability, making spaces impractical. How to fix it:

  • Ask: "Can I live in this space comfortably?"
  • Ensure pathways are clear—don’t block doorways or walking routes with large objects.
  • Example: If you love vintage furniture, ensure it’s functional—a small, well-placed sideboard can hold books and decor without taking up too much space.
