An L.A. Artist Devoted to the Process of Paint: A Deep Dive into the Art of Creation

Introduction: The Alchemy of Art in Los Angeles

Los Angeles has long been a magnet for artists, dreamers, and visionaries—where the sun-kissed skies meet the raw energy of creativity. Among them stands an artist who doesn’t just paint but lives the process of paint. This isn’t about quick masterpieces or viral social media trends; it’s about the journey—the way pigment transforms under the artist’s hand, the way texture and emotion become one.

According to a 2023 report by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the arts and crafts industry in California alone generated $12.7 billion in economic output, with Los Angeles contributing a significant portion. Yet, behind every brushstroke lies a story of discipline, experimentation, and an unshakable devotion to the process itself.

This isn’t just about technique—it’s about mindfulness in motion, where every stroke is a meditation, every color a decision, and every canvas a living entity. In this guide, we’ll explore:

  • The philosophy behind process-driven art
  • 8 actionable strategies to deepen your engagement with paint
  • Real-world examples of artists who mastered the craft
  • Common mistakes and how to avoid them
  • A FAQ section with expert insights

By the end, you’ll understand why some artists never stop painting—not because they’re perfect, but because they’re in love with the process.

Why the Process Matters More Than the Product

Many artists fall into the trap of chasing results—a finished piece, a gallery show, or social media validation. But the most profound creators don’t measure success by what they produce; they measure it by how they engage with the act of creation.

The Science of Flow in Art

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi introduced the concept of flow—a state of deep immersion where time loses meaning. For painters, this happens when:

  • The brush moves without hesitation (automaticity)
  • The mind is fully present (no distractions)
  • The challenge matches the skill (neither too easy nor overwhelming)

A 2021 study in Frontiers in Psychology found that artists in a flow state produce work with higher emotional resonance, meaning their pieces evoke stronger responses from viewers. This isn’t about perfection; it’s about being fully alive in the moment.

The L.A. Artist’s Mindset: Less Pressure, More Play

In a city where success is often measured in dollars and clout, some artists reject the hustle. Instead, they embrace:

  • Slow painting (working on a piece for months)
  • Process journals (documenting every decision)
  • Unfinished works (leaving room for evolution)

This approach isn’t just relaxing—it’s liberating. When you stop worrying about the "end product," you open yourself to unexpected discoveries.

8 Actionable Strategies to Deepen Your Engagement with Paint

If you’ve ever stared at a blank canvas and felt paralyzed, it’s time to shift your focus from what you’re creating to how you’re creating. Here are eight strategies to make the process itself the star of your art.

1. Embrace the "No Rules" Rule

Many artists follow rigid techniques—academic training, strict color theory, or traditional methods. But some of the most innovative work comes from breaking the rules.

How to do it:

  • Start with a blank canvas and no preconceived ideas.
  • Use unconventional tools—sponges, fingers, even household objects.
  • Limit your palette to 3 colors and see what happens.

Real-world example: Artist Julie Mehretu, though not based in L.A., is known for her layered, abstract compositions that defy traditional structure. She often begins with sketches on paper, then builds up texture with paint, allowing the process to dictate the final form.

2. Work in "Process Sessions" (Not Finished Pieces)

Instead of aiming for a "perfect" painting, commit to short, focused sessions where the goal is exploration, not completion.

How to do it:

  • Set a timer for 20-30 minutes and paint without stopping.
  • Use thin, translucent layers to build slowly.
  • Don’t erase mistakes—let them become part of the piece.

Real-world example: Los Angeles-based artist Mark Bradford (though primarily a collage artist) often works in intuitive, gestural layers, where each stroke is a reaction to the previous one. His process is non-linear, meaning he may return to a piece days or weeks later, adding new elements.

3. Document Every Step (Even the "Bad" Ones)

Many artists discard failed attempts, but some of the most interesting work comes from embracing imperfection.

How to do it:

  • Keep a process journal (sketches, photos, notes).
  • Label each stage—what you were thinking, what you liked/disliked.
  • Revisit old works—sometimes, a "flaw" becomes the best part.

Real-world example: Artist David Hockney is famous for his experimental techniques, including polaroid collages and iPad drawings. He often reworks ideas across multiple mediums, showing how the process evolves over time.

4. Use "Constraint Painting" to Spark Creativity

Limiting yourself can force innovation. Try:

  • Painting only with one color (or a limited palette).
  • Working on a small scale (postcard-sized canvases).
  • Following a strict rule (e.g., "No straight lines").

How to do it:

  • Pick a random constraint (e.g., "Only use household paint").
  • Stick to it for a week and see what emerges.

Real-world example: Artist Gerhard Richter (though German) is known for his abstract "blot" paintings, where he used random splatters as a starting point. His process was controlled chaos, proving that constraints can lead to masterpieces.

5. Paint with Your Non-Dominant Hand

This forces your brain to rethink movement and control, leading to unexpected textures and marks.

How to do it:

  • Start with simple shapes (circles, lines).
  • Gradually introduce color and complexity.
  • Don’t worry about precision—embrace the awkwardness.

Real-world example: Artist Jean-Michel Basquiat often worked in a spontaneous, gestural style, where his marks felt raw and immediate. While he didn’t specifically use his non-dominant hand, his approach was intuitive rather than technical.

6. Incorporate "Found Materials" into Your Process

Using unexpected objects (fabric, sand, leaves) can add texture and depth to your work.

How to do it:

  • Collect textures (burlap, plastic wrap, old book pages).
  • Layer them under paint for unexpected effects.
  • Mix mediums (e.g., acrylic + ink + collage).

Real-world example: Artist Kara Walker uses cut-paper silhouettes to explore race and history. Her process involves layering, erasing, and reworking until the narrative emerges.

7. Work in "Silent Mode" (No Music, No Distractions)

Many artists paint with music or ambient noise, but absolute silence can heighten focus.

How to do it:

  • Turn off all distractions (phone, TV, even conversation).
  • Breathe deeply before starting.
  • Let your mind wander—sometimes, the best ideas come when you’re not "working."

Real-world example: Artist Mark Rothko (though not L.A.-based) worked in deep meditation, believing that art should evoke emotion without narrative. His process was slow, deliberate, and silent.

8. Finish Nothing (Let Pieces Evolve Over Time)

Some of the most powerful art is unfinished—leaving room for interpretation and change.

How to do it:

  • Stop before the piece feels "done."
  • Walk away for days/weeks and return with fresh eyes.
  • Add new layers based on new insights.

Real-world example: Artist Cy Twombly is known for his gestural, scribbled paintings, many of which look incomplete. His process was organic, with each mark feeling like a spontaneous decision.

Common Mistakes & How to Avoid Them

Even the most devoted artists fall into traps. Here’s how to recognize and overcome them.

1. Mistake: Chasing Perfection

Problem: You keep "fixing" a piece instead of letting it breathe. Solution:

  • Set a "good enough" threshold—when does it feel complete?
  • Accept imperfections as part of the process.

2. Mistake: Skipping the "Warm-Up"

Problem: Jumping into a big piece without priming your hands. Solution:

  • Do small sketches or gestural marks before starting.
  • Stretch your hands to improve control.

3. Mistake: Ignoring Your Process Journal

Problem: You don’t track what works (or doesn’t). Solution:

  • Write down observations after each session.
  • Compare old and new work to see patterns.

4. Mistake: Comparing Yourself to Others

Problem: You measure your process against someone else’s "perfect" method. Solution:

  • Focus on your own evolution, not external standards.
  • Remember: Every artist’s process is unique.

5. Mistake: Not Taking Breaks

Problem: Burnout leads to frustration and stagnation. Solution:

  • Work in short bursts (20-30 minutes).
  • Step away for hours/days to return with fresh eyes.
