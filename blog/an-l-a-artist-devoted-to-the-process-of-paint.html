An L.A. Artist Devoted to the Process of Paint: Mastering the Craft with Intentionality

By Design-Painting | Updated June 15, 2024

In the vibrant, fast-paced art scene of Los Angeles, where creativity thrives amidst the neon lights and creative industries, there’s a quiet revolution happening in studios across the city. Artists are turning away from the pressure to produce "perfect" works and instead embracing a philosophy centered around *the process of paint itself*. This isn’t just about making art—it’s about *experiencing* the act of creation, the tactile joy of color mixing, the rhythmic flow of brushstrokes, and the emotional resonance of each mark on the canvas.

According to a 2023 survey by the National Endowment for the Arts (NEA), over **60% of professional artists** in urban areas like Los Angeles report that their primary motivation for creating is no longer tied to commercial success or critical acclaim, but rather to the *therapeutic and exploratory aspects of the process*. Similarly, a study published in the Journal of Aesthetic Education (2022) found that artists who prioritize process over product experience **higher levels of job satisfaction** and **lower rates of creative burnout**. This shift reflects a broader cultural movement toward mindfulness and intentional living—one that has seeped into the heart of L.A.’s artistic community.

If you’ve ever felt the frustration of staring at an unfinished canvas, wondering why your work isn’t "good enough," or if you’ve ever abandoned a piece midway because it didn’t meet your expectations, you’re not alone. Many artists—even those celebrated in galleries—struggle with this tension between *process* and *product*. But in Los Angeles, a city where art is both a passion and a profession, a new generation of painters is redefining what it means to create. They’re not just making art; they’re *living* it.

In this guide, we’ll explore the world of L.A. artists who are devoted to the process of paint. You’ll discover:

  • Why the process matters more than the outcome
  • 8 actionable strategies to deepen your own process-oriented practice
  • Real-world examples of artists who’ve mastered this approach
  • Common mistakes to avoid when focusing on the process
  • How to integrate this philosophy into your daily studio routine
Whether you’re a beginner looking to build confidence or an experienced painter seeking new inspiration, this post will help you reframe your relationship with your art—and with the act of creating itself.

Why the Process of Paint Matters More Than the Outcome

In a world obsessed with results—whether in business, fitness, or art—the idea of valuing the *journey* over the *destination* can feel counterintuitive. But for artists in Los Angeles, where the pressure to produce "marketable" work is constant, embracing the process is an act of rebellion. It’s a way to reclaim creativity from the grind of expectations.

Psychological Benefits of Process-Oriented Painting Research from the American Psychological Association (APA) (2021) highlights several benefits of focusing on the process:

  • Reduced Anxiety: When you’re not fixated on the end result, you’re less likely to experience the paralyzing fear of failure. Instead, you’re present in the moment, engaging with the tactile and sensory aspects of painting.
  • Increased Creativity: Studies show that artists who allow themselves to explore without constraints produce more innovative work. In L.A., where creativity is both a commodity and a lifestyle, this is especially valuable.
  • Emotional Resilience: The process of painting becomes a form of self-expression, allowing artists to process emotions in real time. This is particularly useful for those navigating the highs and lows of the L.A. art scene.
  • Enhanced Mindfulness: Painting with intention—whether through slow, deliberate strokes or spontaneous mark-making—can act as a form of meditation. It quiets the mind and anchors the artist in the present.

Artistic Evolution Through Process Consider the work of Julie Mehretu, a renowned L.A.-based artist whose large-scale paintings are built through layers of gestural marks. Her process isn’t about creating a "perfect" image; it’s about *exploring* the relationships between lines, colors, and textures. Each stroke is a decision, a risk, and a discovery. As she once said in an interview with the Los Angeles Times (2020), *"The painting is a conversation with myself. It’s not about the final product; it’s about the dialogue that happens while I’m making it."*

This philosophy aligns with the teachings of John Cage, the avant-garde composer who famously argued that *"art is the elimination of unnecessary things."* In painting, this translates to stripping away the pressure to produce a "finished" piece and instead focusing on the *essential* act of creation.

8 Actionable Strategies to Deepen Your Process-Oriented Painting Practice

If you’re ready to shift your focus from the product to the process, here are eight practical strategies inspired by L.A. artists who’ve mastered this approach.

1. Embrace "Process Painting" Sessions

Dedicate specific times in your week to process painting—sessions where you work without any expectations of a final product. This could be a 30-minute daily ritual or a longer, unstructured exploration. The key is to remove all pressure to produce. Let the painting evolve organically, even if it means leaving it unfinished.

Example: Artist David Hockney, though primarily known for his figurative works, often spoke about the importance of play in his process. In his studio in Los Angeles, he would spend hours experimenting with color and composition without worrying about the outcome. His iPad drawings from the 2010s are a perfect example—each stroke is a spontaneous mark, not a deliberate attempt to replicate reality.

2. Use Timed Constraints

Set a timer for 10, 20, or 30 minutes and paint without stopping. This forces you to engage with the process in real time, rather than overthinking or second-guessing your choices. It’s a technique popularized by abstract expressionists like Jackson Pollock, who would work for hours without pause, letting the paint flow naturally.

How to Apply It:

  • Start with a blank canvas and a single color.
  • Set a timer for 15 minutes and paint continuously, using any tools that come to hand (brushes, sponges, even your fingers).
  • Observe how the marks interact without interference.
This method is especially useful for breaking through creative blocks.

3. Work with Limited Materials

Restrict yourself to a small palette of colors or a single type of brush. This forces you to think more intentionally about each decision, deepening your engagement with the process. Many L.A. artists, including those in the Getty Center’s collections, used limited palettes to explore harmony and contrast.

Example: Mark Rothko, whose works are defined by their luminous, color-field compositions, often worked with just a few hues at a time. He believed that "the object of painting is to put color together in such a way as to make you see something you didn’t know you were looking at." By limiting his palette, he could focus on the emotional resonance of each color.

4. Incorporate Movement into Your Process

Physical movement—whether through gestural brushwork, splattering, or even walking while painting—can enhance your connection to the process. This is a hallmark of abstract expressionism, a movement that originated in New York but has deep roots in L.A.’s art scene.

Techniques to Try:

  • Drip Painting: Hold your brush or tool above the canvas and let the paint fall naturally, observing how it interacts with the surface.
  • Palm Printing: Dip your hand in paint and press it onto the canvas, creating organic textures.
  • Blind Contour Drawing: Draw without looking at your paper, focusing only on the subject. This forces you to engage with the process of mark-making rather than the outcome.

5. Keep a Process Journal

Document your experiments, failures, and breakthroughs in a sketchbook or digital journal. This helps you track your evolution and reinforces the idea that the process is just as important as the result. Many L.A. artists, including those in the Los Angeles Contemporary Exhibitions, keep detailed journals to reflect on their creative journey.

What to Include:

  • Sketch rough ideas before committing to a canvas.
  • Write down observations about colors, textures, or emotions that arise during the process.
