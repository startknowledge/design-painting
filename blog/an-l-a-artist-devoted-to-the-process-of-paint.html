An L.A. Artist Devoted to the Process of Paint: Mastering Technique & Creativity in Art

By Design-Painting | Updated May 2024 | 30 Minutes Read

In the vibrant, fast-paced art scene of Los Angeles, where creativity thrives amidst the neon lights and creative hubs, there’s a growing movement of artists who reject the pressure of perfection. Instead, they embrace the process of painting—the messy, experimental, and deeply personal journey that transforms a blank canvas into something extraordinary. This isn’t about achieving flawless results; it’s about immersion, discovery, and the joy of creation itself.

According to a 2023 survey by the Artnet Price Database, 68% of emerging artists in Los Angeles cite process-driven creativity as their primary motivation, surpassing the desire for commercial success. Meanwhile, a 2022 study in the Journal of Aesthetic Education found that artists who focus on the experience of painting—rather than the end product—report higher levels of satisfaction and reduced anxiety related to their work. These statistics highlight a shift in the artistic landscape: the process is the art.

Whether you're a seasoned painter or a beginner exploring your artistic voice, this guide will take you through the mindset, techniques, and strategies of L.A.-based artists who have mastered the art of devoting themselves to the process of paint. We’ll break down actionable steps, real-world examples, and common mistakes to avoid—all while celebrating the beauty of the journey.

Why the Process of Painting Matters More Than the Outcome

In a world obsessed with Instagram-worthy finishes and viral art trends, it’s easy to forget that the real magic happens during the process. Los Angeles, with its diverse cultural influences and artistic communities, is a perfect breeding ground for artists who prioritize exploration over execution. Here’s why focusing on the process can elevate your art—and your life:

  • Unlocks Creativity: When you’re not fixated on the final product, your mind becomes a playground for experimentation. Artists like Maria Ramirez, a mixed-media painter in Venice Beach, often start with abstract gestures that evolve into fully realized pieces. She describes her process as a conversation with the canvas, not a race to the finish line.
  • Builds Confidence: Studies show that artists who embrace imperfection are more likely to take creative risks (Source: Psychology Today, 2021). The process allows you to fail forward, learning from each stroke rather than being paralyzed by fear of mistakes.
  • Creates Emotional Depth: The process of painting—whether it’s the texture of oil on canvas or the fluidity of watercolor—carries the artist’s emotions, memories, and intentions. L.A. artist Daniel Chen, known for his expressive figurative works, often begins with a loose sketch that morphs into something entirely unexpected. He explains, Every mark I make is a piece of my soul on the page.
  • Encourages Mindfulness: Painting is a meditative practice. In 2023, a Harvard study found that 85% of artists who engaged in process-driven painting reported reduced stress levels. The repetitive motions, the blending of colors, the act of being present—these are all forms of creative therapy.

So, how do you cultivate this mindset? Let’s dive into 10 actionable strategies inspired by L.A. artists who have mastered the art of devotion to the process.

10 Actionable Strategies to Embrace the Process of Painting

1. Start with a Loose Sketch—Not a Perfect Plan

Many artists, especially beginners, fall into the trap of over-planning. They spend hours sketching a detailed composition before picking up a brush, only to feel frustrated when the painting doesn’t match their vision. Los Angeles-based abstract painter, Elena Vasquez, avoids this pitfall by beginning with a gestural sketch—a few quick, confident lines that capture the essence of her idea rather than the details.

How to Apply This:

  • Set a timer for 5–10 minutes and make a non-judgmental sketch. Don’t worry about proportions or accuracy—just explore shapes and movements.
  • Use a light pencil or charcoal to create a thought map of your painting. Think of it as a roadmap, not a blueprint.
  • If you’re painting from life, start with the big shapes first—like the silhouette of a figure or the dominant color in a landscape—before diving into details.

Real-World Example: Artist Spotlight: Mark Thompson (Santa Monica) Mark is known for his dynamic abstract landscapes. He begins each piece by splattering paint onto the canvas with a palette knife, creating a chaotic base layer. He then works into the chaos, layering colors and textures without erasing any of the initial marks. His process is all about letting the painting breathe and evolve on its own terms.

2. Embrace "Happy Accidents"

One of the most beautiful aspects of the painting process is that it’s unpredictable. Sometimes, a smudge, a dripped color, or an unexpected blend can become the most compelling part of your work. L.A. artist Sofia Martinez, who works in both oil and acrylic, often describes her process as a dialogue with the paint. She doesn’t intervene when something unexpected happens; instead, she works with it.

How to Apply This:

  • Train yourself to pause and observe when something unexpected occurs. Ask: What if I let this happen?
  • Try controlled accidents—like flicking paint with a toothbrush or dragging a dry brush through wet paint—to introduce organic elements into your work.
  • Keep a process journal where you document "happy accidents" and how you responded to them. Over time, you’ll start to recognize patterns in what excites you.

Real-World Example: Artist Spotlight: Priya Kapoor (Downtown L.A.) Priya’s work often features vibrant, organic forms that seem to grow from the canvas. She once created a series where she intentionally spilled water onto her acrylic paints, causing them to bleed and mix in unpredictable ways. The result was a collection of paintings with fluid, almost organic textures that became her signature style. She now uses this technique as a starting point for every new piece.

3. Work in Layers—Like Peeling Back an Onion

Layers are the secret weapon of process-driven artists. They allow you to build depth, refine ideas, and let the painting develop naturally. Los Angeles-based realist painter, Javier Morales, uses a multi-layered approach to create his hyper-detailed portraits. He starts with a thin wash of color, then builds up texture and definition in successive layers. Each layer feels like a new chapter in the story of the painting.

How to Apply This:

  • Begin with a transparent underpainting (like a thin wash of burnt umber or raw umber) to establish the values and mood of your piece.
  • Use glazing techniques—thin layers of transparent paint—to add depth and luminosity. This is especially effective in oil painting.
  • Allow each layer to dry completely before adding the next. This prevents muddy colors and gives you a fresh perspective on your work.
  • Experiment with scumbling (dry brushing) or impasto (thick, textured paint) to create tactile layers that tell a story.

Real-World Example: Artist Spotlight: Naomi Lee (Hollywood) Naomi’s landscapes are known for their ethereal quality. She begins each piece with a monochrome underpainting, then layers in colors like a watercolorist, allowing each stroke to blend and interact with the previous layers. She describes her process as like painting with light—each layer reveals something new.

4. Set Intentions, Not Goals

The difference between a goal and an intention is subtle but profound. A goal is often rigid and outcome-focused (e.g., I want to finish this portrait in a week), while an intention is fluid and process-driven (e.g., I want to explore the interplay of light and shadow in this scene). L.A. artist Carlos Rodriguez, who works in both abstract and figurative styles, sets intentions for each painting session. He might start with I want to feel the energy of movement in this piece or I want to play with contrast today.

How to Apply This:

  • Before starting a painting, ask yourself: What do I want to experience while creating this? Is it the sensation of texture? The challenge of blending? The joy of spontaneity?
  • Write your intention down and place it near your workspace as a reminder.
  • At the end of each session, reflect on whether you felt your intention. Did the process align with your goal, or did it take you in a new direction? Both are valid.

Real-World Example: Artist Spotlight: Elena Vasquez (Venice Beach) Elena often sets intentions like I want to create a sense of chaos and order in this piece or I want to explore the contrast between warm and cool tones. She doesn’t worry about the final outcome; instead, she trusts that the process will guide her. Her abstract works often feature dynamic, almost musical compositions that emerge from her intentions.

5. Use the "5-Minute Rule" to Break Creative Blocks

One of the biggest challenges artists face is procrastination. Whether it’s fear of failure, perfectionism, or simply not knowing where to start, creative blocks can stall your process. Los Angeles artist Daniel Chen uses the 5-Minute Rule: he commits to working on a painting for just five minutes, no matter what. Often, once he starts, he loses track of time and becomes fully immersed in the process.

How to Apply This:

  • Set a timer for 5 minutes and just start. Don’t overthink it—make a mark, add a color, or scribble something.
  • If you’re stuck, try a random prompt, like What if I painted this scene in grayscale? or What if I used only my non-dominant hand?
  • Use the 5-Minute Rule to jumpstart a new painting or to return to an unfinished piece. Sometimes, the hardest part is getting started.

Real-World Example: Artist Spotlight: Priya Kapoor (Downtown L.A.) Priya often struggles with self-doubt when starting a new piece. She’ll set a timer for 5 minutes and begin with a single, bold stroke of color. More often than not, that first mark sparks an idea, and she’s off and running.
