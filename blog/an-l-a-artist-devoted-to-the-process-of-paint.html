Among them stands an artist whose devotion to the process of paint is as profound as the canvases they leave behind. This isn’t just about creating art; it’s about living within the strokes, the textures, and the quiet magic of transformation.

Why the Process Matters: Beyond the Final Masterpiece

Many artists fall into the trap of fixating solely on the end result—that perfect, polished painting hanging on a gallery wall. But the real magic happens in the in-between moments: the smudges, the accidental drips, the layers of emotion that seep into every brushstroke.

Consider the words of Mark Rothko, the abstract expressionist who once said: "The painting is there to make you see what you never noticed before."

This L.A. artist operates on a similar principle. For them, the process is the art. It’s about:

  • Exploring texture—how thick paint can feel like clay, how thin it can dance across the canvas.
  • Embracing imperfection—learning that flaws often lead to unexpected beauty.
  • Developing patience—understanding that some paintings take weeks, months, or even years to fully realize.

In a world where instant gratification dominates, this artist’s work is a testament to the power of slow, deliberate creation.

8 Essential Strategies to Master the Process of Paint

If you’re ready to shift your mindset from "product" to "process," these actionable strategies will help you cultivate a deeper, more intentional approach to painting.

1. Start with a "No-Brainer" Sketch (But Don’t Overthink It)

Before diving into paint, many artists spend hours refining sketches—only to abandon them when the execution doesn’t match their initial vision.

What to do instead:

  • Sketch loosely—use light pencil lines to map out composition, but don’t obsess over perfection.
  • Work in layers—begin with a rough underpainting (a thin wash of color) to establish values before adding details.
  • Trust the process—if a sketch feels "off," it’s not a failure; it’s a starting point for discovery.

Real-world example: A local L.A. artist once began a landscape piece with a single, jagged line representing a mountain. Instead of erasing it, they let it guide the rest of the painting, leading to a surprisingly dynamic final piece where the mountain’s irregularity became its strength.

2. Embrace "Happy Accidents" (And Document Them)

One of the most beautiful aspects of painting is that mistakes often become the most interesting parts of the work.

How to harness this:

  • Don’t clean your brushes immediately—let some paint dry on them to create unexpected textures.
  • Use "failed" strokes—if a color bleeds where you didn’t intend, lean into it rather than correcting it.
  • Keep a "mistake journal"—record these unintended moments so you can reference them later.

Real-world example: A street artist in Venice Beach once had a brush overflow mid-painting, creating a wild, organic splotch that ended up being the focal point of their piece. Instead of covering it up, they expanded the color around it, turning a potential error into the heart of the composition.

3. Work in "Painting Sessions" (Not Sessions of Perfection)

Many artists try to finish a piece in one sitting, leading to frustration when the result doesn’t meet their expectations.

A better approach:

  • Divide your work into sessions—paint for 20-30 minutes a day, then step away.
  • Let layers dry—this prevents smudging and allows you to see the painting evolve.
  • Trust the subconscious—sometimes, the best ideas come when you’re not actively "working."

Real-world example: A contemporary painter in Silver Lake worked on a portrait over six months, adding just one or two details per session. Each time they returned, they saw new possibilities, leading to a rich, multi-dimensional piece that felt alive.

4. Experiment with Unconventional Tools

Sticking to brushes only limits your creativity. The process becomes more exciting when you introduce new tools and techniques.

Ways to expand your toolkit:

  • Fingers & palette knives—great for thick impasto textures.
  • Sponges & rags—for blending or creating abstract backgrounds.
  • Household items—a toothbrush can make fantastic splatter effects, while a plastic bag can create interesting folds and creases.

Real-world example: An abstract expressionist in Echo Park once used a broken wine glass to drag paint across a canvas, creating organic, vein-like lines that became the centerpiece of their work. The result was unexpected yet harmonious, proving that constraints breed creativity.

5. Study the Masters (But Don’t Copy Them)

Many artists fall into the trap of imitating their favorite painters, which stifles their own voice.

How to learn from the greats without losing yourself:

  • Analyze their process—watch documentaries or read interviews about how artists like Van Gogh or Frida Kahlo worked.
  • Practice "homages"—recreate a famous painting in your own style, but add your unique twist.
  • Focus on technique, not replication—learn how they built up layers, used light, or handled brushwork.

Real-world example: A young artist in Pasadena once studied Frida Kahlo’s self-portraits but instead of copying her style, they incorporated her bold colors with their own surreal elements. The result was a hybrid piece that felt distinctly theirs—a bridge between homage and innovation.

6. Play with Color Theory (But Break the Rules)

Color theory is a tool, not a law. The best artists understand the rules so they can bend them.

Key principles to master:

  • Complementary colors (opposites on the wheel) create vibrant contrasts.
  • Analogous colors (next to each other) create harmony.
  • Monochromatic schemes (shades of one color) add depth and sophistication.

How to experiment:

  • Limit your palette—pick 3-4 colors and see what happens.
  • Use unexpected combinations—try green and orange together for a striking effect.
  • Let colors bleed—don’t fear mixing them unintentionally; sometimes the unplanned hues are the most interesting.

Real-world example: A painter in West Hollywood once intentionally mixed a "wrong" color—a deep purple where they expected blue—and discovered that the unexpected shift gave their piece emotional weight, making it feel more raw and authentic.

7. Work in Series (Not Just Single Pieces)

Creating a series of paintings on a single theme or concept forces you to explore depth, variation, and evolution—rather than getting stuck in one idea.

How to approach a series:

  • Choose a unifying theme—could be a place, emotion, or technique.
  • Vary your approach—some pieces can be loose and abstract, others detailed and realistic.
  • Document the process—take photos of each stage to see how your ideas develop.

Real-world example: A local artist in Santa Monica created a series called "Fragments of Memory"—each painting was a different interpretation of the same childhood memory, using varying textures and color palettes. The result was a cohesive yet diverse collection that told a multi-layered story.

8. Find Your "Why" (And Let It Guide Your Process)

Every great artist has a personal reason for painting—whether it’s healing, expression, or pure joy. When you connect to this core purpose, the process becomes more meaningful.

How to discover your "why":

  • Ask yourself: "Why do I paint?" (Is it for love, escape, or something deeper?)
  • Journal your thoughts—write about the emotions that arise during your sessions.
  • Create without pressure—sometimes the best work comes when you forget the "end goal."

Real-world example: A painter in Culver City once struggled with self-doubt until they realized their paintings were a way to process grief. Once they embraced this truth, their work became more vulnerable and powerful, resonating deeply with viewers who recognized their own pain in the strokes.

Common Mistakes (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most dedicated artists make mistakes. Here are five pitfalls to watch out for—and how to overcome them.

1. Over-Reliance on Reference Photos

The mistake: Using a photo as a step-by-step guide instead of a source of inspiration.

Why it’s harmful:

  • Kills creativity—you start seeing the world through a single lens.
  • Limits texture—photos flatten reality; painting should add depth and emotion.

How to fix it:

  • Use photos as a starting point, not a blueprint.
  • Close your eyes and visualize the scene in your mind before painting.
  • Steal from life—sketch from real people, landscapes, or objects rather than relying on screens.

2. Skipping the Underpainting

The mistake: Jumping straight into final details without a foundation.

Why it’s harmful:

  • Colors can look flat—an underpainting helps establish values and depth.
  • Hard to correct mistakes—once you’re in the final layers, fixing errors is much harder.

How to fix it:

  • Start with a thin wash of color (or even just black and white) to map out your composition.
  • Let it dry completely before adding details.
  • Think of it as a "scaffolding"—essential for the structure of your painting.

3. Neglecting the Background

The mistake: Focusing only on the main subject and leaving the background as an afterthought.

Why it’s harmful:

  • Distracts from the focal point—a busy background can overpower your subject.
  • Missed opportunity for texture—backgrounds can add depth and interest.

How to fix it:

  • Paint the background first—it sets the mood and context for the rest of the piece.
  • Use soft edges for a blurred, dreamy effect.
  • Experiment with color—a complementary background can make your subject pop.

4. Rushing the Process

The mistake: Trying to force a painting to be "finished" in one sitting.

Why it’s harmful:

  • Leads to frustration—when you don’t give yourself time, the work often feels rushed.
  • Misses organic development—some of the best ideas come after you’ve stepped away.

How to fix it:

  • Set a timer—work for 20-30 minutes, then stop.
  • Walk away—come back with fresh eyes.
  • Embrace "unfinished" pieces—some of the most powerful art is left with intentional gaps.

5. Ignoring Your Environment

The mistake: Painting in a cluttered, distracting, or uncomfortable space.

Why it’s harmful:

  • Kills creativity—if your mind is elsewhere, your art will reflect it.
  • Physical discomfort—bad posture or lighting can lead to strain and burnout.

How to fix it:

  • Create a dedicated workspace—even a small corner with good lighting works.
  • Keep your tools organized—nothing kills the flow like hunting for supplies.
  • Play music or nature sounds—background noise can enhance focus for some artists.

1. How do I know if I’m "good" at painting?

Answer: There’s no single definition of "good" in art. Instead of asking "Am I skilled?", ask:

  • "Does this painting make me feel something?"
  • "Did I enjoy the process?"
  • "Would I look at this in 10 years and still appreciate it?"

Remember: Even Picasso once said, "I paint what I see, not what I know." The goal isn’t perfection—it’s authenticity and growth.

2. What if I don’t have a "style"?

Answer: Style develops through experimentation, not overnight. Try these steps:

  • Mimic different techniques—practice impressionism one week, abstract expressionism the next.
  • Steal from other artists—not in a copying sense, but by absorbing their approaches.
  • Keep a sketchbook—document your evolving ideas without pressure.

Many artists don’t realize their style exists until they’ve painted enough to recognize patterns in their work.

3. How do I stay motivated when I’m stuck?

Answer: Stagnation is normal—even Leonardo da Vinci had periods of doubt. Try:

  • Switching mediums—if oils feel boring, try watercolor or digital.
  • Setting micro-goals—instead of "finish a painting," try "paint one layer today."
  • Joining a community—art groups (online or in-person) provide accountability and inspiration.

Pro tip: Sometimes, the worst paintings lead to the best breakthroughs.

4. Should I sell my work if I’m not "ready"?

Answer: You don’t need to be a "master" to share your art. Consider:

  • Local markets & pop-ups—great for low-pressure exposure.
  • Prints & digital sales—if you love a piece but don’t want to part with it, offer prints.
  • Gifts & commissions—start small with friends or family before bigger ventures.

Key insight: Many successful artists started by selling "imperfect" work—what matters is your passion, not your skill level.
