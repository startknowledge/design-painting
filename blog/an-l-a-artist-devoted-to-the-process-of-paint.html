10 Actionable Strategies for Embracing the Process of Paint

If you’re ready to shift your approach from product to process, here are 10 practical strategies inspired by L.A. artists who live by the brush:

1. Start with a "No-Brain" Warm-Up

Before diving into a major piece, many process-driven artists begin with quick, spontaneous sketches or abstract marks. This "warm-up" helps loosen the mind and body, allowing for unfiltered creativity.

How to apply it:

  • Set a timer for 10-15 minutes and make non-judgmental marks—scribbles, dabs, or gestural lines.
  • Use a different medium (ink, charcoal, or even finger paints) to break mental blocks.
  • Example: An L.A. abstract painter once described how he would drip oil paint onto a canvas before beginning a new series, letting the organic flow inspire his composition.

2. Work in "Layers of Intention"

Process art isn’t about randomness—it’s about layered decision-making. Instead of planning every detail, artists like our subject work in phases, allowing each layer to influence the next.

How to apply it:

  • First layer: Apply a ground wash (thinned paint) to establish a base.
  • Second layer: Add gestural underpainting (thick, expressive strokes).
  • Final layers: Refine details while keeping the raw energy of earlier marks.
  • Example: A Los Angeles figurative painter once explained how he would paint a figure in broad, monochromatic shapes before introducing color, letting the emotional weight of the forms guide his choices.

3. Embrace "Controlled Chaos" with Mixed Media

Many process artists combine materials to create unexpected textures and interactions. This could mean collage, sand, or even household objects embedded in the paint.

How to apply it:

  • Try impasto techniques (thick paint application) to build dimension.
  • Incorporate found objects (buttons, fabric, or even broken pottery) into your work.
  • Experiment with unconventional tools (sponges, credit cards, or even a fork).
  • Example: A contemporary L.A. artist once created a series where they pressed dried leaves and flowers into wet acrylic, letting nature dictate the composition.

4. Paint with Your Non-Dominant Hand

Forcing yourself to use your less skilled hand can unlock new ways of seeing and moving. This exercise disrupts habitual patterns, leading to more organic, intuitive marks.

How to apply it:

  • Start with small, controlled strokes to build confidence.
  • Gradually increase pressure and spontaneity.
  • Example: A street artist in L.A. once switched hands mid-project, resulting in a series of unexpected, fluid abstractions that became his most celebrated work.

5. Use "Time-Based" Techniques

Some of the most powerful process-driven works emerge from slow, deliberate application. Techniques like dripping, bleeding, or allowing paint to dry naturally can create organic forms that feel alive.

How to apply it:

  • Drip paint onto a canvas and let it pool and blend before adding layers.
  • Bleed colors by layering translucent washes (e.g., watercolor over acrylic).
  • Work in stages—let each layer dry completely before adding the next.
  • Example: A minimalist L.A. painter once created a series by pouring thinned oil paint onto a canvas and tilting it to let gravity dictate the flow, resulting in organic, abstract landscapes.

6. Document Your Process (But Don’t Overthink It)

Many artists keep a sketchbook or digital log of their work-in-progress. This helps track evolution, mistakes, and breakthroughs—but the key is to document without judgment.

How to apply it:

  • Take casual photos of your process (even if it’s messy).
  • Note what worked and what didn’t in a simple journal.
  • Example: A Los Angeles portrait painter once documented every failed attempt at a certain expression, later using those studies to refine his approach.

7. Work in "Process Zones" (Not Just a Studio)

For some artists, the environment shapes the work. Many L.A. process artists paint outdoors, in their cars, or even in public spaces to capture fleeting moments.

How to apply it:

  • Paint en plein air (outdoors) to engage with natural light.
  • Use unconventional spaces (a park, a rooftop, or a beach) for inspiration.
  • Example: A Los Angeles abstract painter once created a series by painting on the hood of his car while driving through the city, letting the vibrations and motion influence his strokes.

8. Let Paint "Talk Back" to You

One of the most radical acts in process art is listening to the paint. If a mark feels wrong, does it still have energy or intention? Sometimes, the "mistake" becomes the most interesting part.

How to apply it:

  • Resist the urge to "fix" immediately—let layers develop.
  • Ask: "Does this add something, even if it’s unexpected?"
  • Example: A Los Angeles sculptor-turned-painter once accidentally spilled paint onto a canvas, only to realize the organic splatter became the focal point of the piece.

9. Use "Process Challenges" to Break Plateaus

When creativity stagnates, artists often set themselves small, restrictive challenges to reignite inspiration. Examples include:

  • Painting only with one color for a week.
  • Using only your non-dominant hand for a series.
  • Limiting yourself to 10 minutes per piece.

How to apply it:

  • Pick one constraint and commit to it for a month.
  • Document the unexpected results.
  • Example: A Los Angeles abstract painter once restricted herself to black and white for a year, leading to a series of highly textured, monochromatic works that became her most critically acclaimed.

10. Find Your "Process Ritual"

Whether it’s listening to a specific playlist, working at a certain time of day, or using a particular scent, rituals can anchor your focus and make the process feel sacred.

How to apply it:

  • Create a pre-work routine (e.g., lighting a candle, playing a certain song).
  • Designate a special space (even if it’s just a corner of your room).
  • Example: A Los Angeles figurative painter once only painted in the early morning, when the light was softest, and only with a specific brand of brushes, believing the consistency enhanced his work.

Common Mistakes in Process Art (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most devoted process artists make missteps. Here’s how to navigate pitfalls without losing the essence of your work:

1. Mistake: Over-Planning

Problem: Some artists fall into the trap of over-intellectualizing their process, leading to rigidity rather than spontaneity.

Solution:

  • Start with a loose idea, not a rigid plan.
  • Allow the paint to guide you—don’t force a vision.

2. Mistake: Skipping the "Ugly Phase"

Problem: Many beginners tempted to "fix" early layers, smoothing out imperfections before the work has time to develop.

Solution:

  • Embrace the "ugly" stage—it’s where the real magic happens.
  • Let layers breathe before adding new ones.

3. Mistake: Comparing Your Process to Others’ End Results

Problem: Social media can distort the journey of art-making, making it seem like only polished works matter.

Solution:

  • Focus on your own evolution, not external validation.
  • Document your process—not just the final piece.

4. Mistake: Ignoring Your Body’s Signals

Problem: Some artists push through fatigue or discomfort, leading to tension in their work.

Solution:

  • Take breaks—even 10 minutes of stretching can reset your creativity.
  • Work in sessions rather than marathon sessions.

5. Mistake: Forgetting to Enjoy the Ride

Problem: When art becomes too serious, the joy of creation fades.

Solution:

  • Play with materials—don’t take yourself too seriously.
  • Laugh at mistakes—they’re part of the process.

1. How do I know if I’m truly working in a process-driven way?

Answer: A process-driven artist doesn’t fixate on the end result but instead engages deeply with the act of creating. Signs you’re on the right track:

  • You enjoy the journey more than the outcome.
  • You experiment freely, even if it leads to "mistakes."
  • Your work evolves unpredictably, shaped by spontaneous decisions.
  • You document your process as much as the final piece.

Example: If you find yourself more excited by the texture of a dried paint blob than the finished painting, you’re likely process-driven.

2. Can I combine process art with traditional techniques?

Answer: Absolutely! Many artists blend process-driven spontaneity with structured techniques. For instance:

  • Start with a loose underpainting (process) but refine details later (traditional).
  • Use gestural marks (process) but plan color palettes (traditional).
  • Work abstractly (process) but incorporate realism in certain elements (traditional).

Example: A Los Angeles painter might drip paint for the background (process) but paint a hyper-realistic figure in the foreground (traditional).

3. How do I stay motivated when the process feels slow?

Answer: Process art requires patience, but you can recharge your motivation with these strategies:

  • Set micro-goals (e.g., "I’ll work for 20 minutes today").
  • Celebrate small wins (e.g., "I loved how this mark turned out").
  • Switch mediums if one feels stagnant.
  • Revisit old work to see how far you’ve come.

Example: An L.A. artist once took a week off to sketch in a notebook, returning to their canvas with renewed energy.

4. Is process art only for abstract or experimental painters?

Answer: No! Process art can be applied to any style—realism, figurative, landscape, etc. The key is engaging deeply with the medium, not the genre.

Examples:

  • A realist painter might work in layers, letting each stroke build naturally.
  • A landscape artist might paint en plein air, letting light and weather influence the work.
  • A figurative artist might use gestural underpaintings to capture emotion before refining details.
