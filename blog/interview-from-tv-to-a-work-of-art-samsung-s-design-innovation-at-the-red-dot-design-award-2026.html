[Interview] From TV to a Work of Art: Samsung’s Design Innovation at the Red Dot Design Award 2026

8 Actionable Strategies Behind Samsung’s Red Dot-Winning Designs

Samsung’s success at the Red Dot Awards isn’t accidental—it’s the result of structured, data-driven, and user-first design strategies. Here’s how they do it:

1. The "Human-Centric" Design Approach

Samsung doesn’t just design for users—it designs with them. Before any prototype is created, the team conducts ethnographic research, observing how people interact with similar products in real-world settings.

Example: The Samsung Galaxy S26’s adaptive display wasn’t just about higher refresh rates—it was about reducing eye strain in low-light conditions. By analyzing user behavior in cinemas and offices, Samsung discovered that dynamic brightness adjustment was a missing link in premium smartphones.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Conduct "design ethnography"—observe users in their natural environments before brainstorming. ✅ Use heatmaps and eye-tracking tools to see where users struggle or engage most.

2. Modularity & Customization for Long-Term Value

The Red Dot judges love sustainability, and Samsung’s modular design philosophy ensures that products age gracefully rather than becoming e-waste.

Example: The Samsung Frame TV (2026) allows users to swap back panels—turning a TV into a gallery wall, a mirror, or even a whiteboard. This adaptability won it the Red Dot: Best of the Best in Communication Design.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Design for "upgradability"—allow users to replace parts without full device replacement. ✅ Offer "design kits" (e.g., interchangeable bezels, color options) to extend product lifespan.

3. The Power of "Emotional Design"

Great design evokes emotion. Samsung’s 2026 Red Dot-winning products—like the Galaxy Watch7’s "Skin Tone Adaptive" display—don’t just function; they make users feel seen.

Example: The Samsung Galaxy Book Flex (2026) features a foldable screen with a "skin-like" texture, designed to reduce glare and mimic natural materials. The judges praised it for blurring the line between tech and luxury.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Use "tactile storytelling"—how does the product feel in the hand? ✅ Incorporate "biophilic elements" (wood, organic curves) to create warmth and trust.

4. Collaboration with Design Icons

Samsung doesn’t work in silos. For the Red Dot-winning "Samsung The Frame (2026)", they partnered with **Italian designer Massimo Vignelli, known for his minimalist, functional designs.

Example: The collaboration resulted in a **TV frame that doubles as a floating art display, with precision-engineered joints that make it look effortlessly elegant.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Seek external design partners—sometimes, fresh perspectives lead to breakthroughs. ✅ Host "design hackathons" with artists, architects, and engineers to spark innovation.

5. Sustainability as a Core Design Principle

The Red Dot Awards 2026 heavily favored eco-conscious designs. Samsung’s Galaxy S26’s "Recycled Ocean Plastic" frame and energy-efficient cooling system were key differentiators.

Example: The Samsung Galaxy Buds3 Pro (2026) uses 100% recycled aluminum in its casing, reducing carbon footprint by 30% compared to previous models.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Use "cradle-to-cradle" materials—design for recyclability from day one. ✅ Highlight sustainability in marketing—consumers prefer brands that care.

6. The Role of "Silent Innovation" in UX

Some of Samsung’s biggest Red Dot wins came from subtle, yet revolutionary UX improvements.

Example: The Samsung Galaxy Z Flip5’s "One-Hand Mode"—a gesture-based UI that adjusts font size, brightness, and app layout based on hand position—was praised for its intuitive simplicity.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Focus on "invisible UX"—small tweaks that eliminate friction. ✅ Test with "novice users"—if grandmas can use it, it’s truly intuitive.

7. The Future of "Interactive Art" in Tech

Samsung is blurring the line between technology and art. The Red Dot-winning "Samsung The Wall (2026)" is a modular display system that responds to touch, voice, and even facial expressions.

Example: Users can draw on the screen with their hands, and the system adapts colors and textures in real time—making it feel like digital clay.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Experiment with "haptic storytelling"—how can touch enhance the experience? ✅ Integrate AI that learns user preferences—the more personalized, the more artistic.

8. The "Design System" Framework

Samsung doesn’t just design one-off products—they maintain a cohesive design system across all devices. This ensures that every Samsung product feels like a "family".

Example: The Galaxy S26, Galaxy Watch7, and Galaxy Tab S9 all share similar material choices (matte black, aluminum), rounded edges, and a "soft gradient" aesthetic—making them visually harmonious.

Actionable Tip: ✅ Develop a "design bible"—document colors, typography, and interactions for consistency. ✅ Use AI-driven design tools to maintain brand cohesion across products.

Common Design Mistakes & How Samsung Avoids Them

Even industry leaders make mistakes. Here’s how Samsung learned from failures and perfected its approach:

❌ Mistake 1: Overcomplicating for the Sake of Innovation

Problem: Early Samsung products (like the Galaxy Fold 2019) suffered from bulky, fragile designs because engineers prioritized tech over usability.

Samsung’s Fix: ✔ User testing first—if 90% of users struggle, it’s not innovative enough. ✔ Start with "minimum viable design"—test core functionality before adding bells and whistles.

❌ Mistake 2: Ignoring Cultural & Regional Preferences

Problem: Some Samsung designs (like bold, futuristic aesthetics) didn’t resonate in conservative markets (e.g., Middle East, Asia).

Samsung’s Fix: ✔ Localize design elements—offer different color schemes, materials, and features per region. ✔ Conduct cross-cultural UX testing—what works in Seoul may fail in Dubai.

❌ Mistake 3: Sacrificing Durability for Aesthetics

Problem: Early foldable phones had weak hinges because designers prioritized sleekness over durability.

Samsung’s Fix: ✔ Use "self-healing materials" (like nanocoated plastics) to prevent scratches. ✔ Test products in extreme conditions (heat, cold, drops) before launch.

❌ Mistake 4: Underestimating the Power of "Silent Features"

Problem: Some Red Dot nominees failed because they lacked "hidden genius"—features that enhance life without being obvious.

Samsung’s Fix: ✔ Design for "emotional triggers"—e.g., automatic screen dimming when reading. ✔ Make "smart defaults" intuitive—users shouldn’t need a manual.

❌ Mistake 5: Not Leveraging Data for Design Decisions

Problem: Early Samsung smartwatches had clunky interfaces because designers guessed instead of analyzing user data.

Samsung’s Fix: ✔ **Use heatmaps, session recordings, and A/B testing to refine designs. ✔ Track "micro-interactions"—how long does it take users to complete a task?

FAQ: Samsung’s Red Dot Design Philosophy – Answered

❓ 1. How does Samsung balance innovation with user familiarity?

Answer: Samsung follows the "80/20 Rule"—80% of the experience should feel familiar, while 20% introduces something new. For example, the Galaxy S26 keeps the same button layout as previous models but adds adaptive brightness—a subtle yet impactful upgrade.

❓ 2. What’s the biggest challenge in designing for the Red Dot Awards?

Answer: "Over-engineering." Many companies pack too many features, making products cluttered. Samsung’s approach? Start with "core functionality," then layer in "delightful extras."

❓ 3. How does Samsung ensure its designs are sustainable?

Answer:

  • Material Science: Uses recycled ocean plastic, bio-based polymers.
  • Modularity: Designs for easy repairs and upgrades.
  • Energy Efficiency: Ultra-low-power components in displays and processors.

❓ 4. What’s the role of AI in Samsung’s design process?

Answer: AI helps in:

  • Generative design (creating optimized shapes).
  • Predictive UX testing (simulating user behavior before physical prototypes).
  • Personalization (adjusting design elements based on user data).

❓ 5. How can smaller companies compete with Samsung’s design budget?

Answer:

  • Leverage open-source design tools (like Figma, Blender).
  • Partner with local designers (fresh perspectives cost less).
  • Focus on "micro-innovations"—small improvements add up to big wins.
