How Samsung Transformed TVs into Red Dot-Worthy Art

1. The Philosophy Behind "Design as an Experience"

Samsung’s approach to design isn’t just about visual appeal—it’s about immersive storytelling. Lee Min-ho explains:

"We believe that design should tell a story. A TV isn’t just a screen; it’s a canvas for emotions, a space for connection, and a reflection of modern living."

This philosophy led to the 2026 Red Dot-winning "Neo QLED Quantum Dot TV", which wasn’t just about picture quality but about how the design itself enhances the viewing experience.

Key Takeaways:

  • Emotional resonance over mere functionality.
  • Material selection that feels premium (e.g., ultra-thin bezels, matte black finishes).
  • Modularity—allowing users to customize their viewing environment.

2. The Role of User-Centric Research in Design

Before a single sketch was made, Samsung conducted extensive user research, including:

  • Behavioral studies on how people interact with TVs in living rooms.
  • Psychological surveys on preferred aesthetics (e.g., minimalist vs. bold designs).
  • Collaborations with interior designers to ensure TVs blend seamlessly into home decor.

"We didn’t just ask, ‘What do users want?’ We asked, ‘What do users need without realizing it?’" — Lee Min-ho

This approach resulted in ergonomic stands, adjustable angles, and even ambient lighting that syncs with room lighting—features that won the Red Dot’s "Best in Class" award.

8 Actionable Strategies from Samsung’s Red Dot Success

1. Blend Technology with Artistic Craftsmanship

Problem: Many tech products prioritize functionality over form, leading to clunky, uninspiring designs. Samsung’s Solution:

  • Handcrafted details (e.g., laser-engraved logos, textured surfaces).
  • Material innovation (e.g., self-healing coatings, biodegradable plastics).
  • Collaboration with artists to infuse abstract patterns into product designs.

How to Apply This:

  • If you’re designing a smartwatch, consider engraved metal bands or interchangeable faces that double as art.
  • For furniture, use 3D-printed textures that mimic natural wood or stone.

2. Prioritize "Silent Innovation" in Design

Problem: Consumers often overlook subtle design improvements that enhance usability. Samsung’s Solution:

  • Red Dot-winning "The Frame TV"—a picture frame that doubles as a TV, blending seamlessly into home decor.
  • Thin, lightweight frames that make TVs feel like decorative art pieces.

How to Apply This:

  • For home appliances, design sleek, minimalist controls that disappear when not in use.
  • In wearables, incorporate adjustable straps that feel like jewelry rather than tech accessories.

3. Use Color Psychology to Enhance Emotional Impact

Samsung’s 2026 Red Dot-winning "Galaxy S26" featured a limited-edition "Midnight Black" and "Celestial Blue" color scheme, chosen based on:

  • Midnight Black = Luxury, sophistication (appeals to high-end consumers).
  • Celestial Blue = Trust, calmness (ideal for business professionals).

How to Apply This:

  • For branding, use color psychology to evoke specific emotions.
  • In product design, test different color palettes with real users to see which resonates most.

4. Make Sustainability a Design Priority

The Red Dot’s "Sustainability Award" was won by Samsung’s "Eco-Packaging TV", which:

  • Uses recycled ocean plastics in the stand.
  • Features a modular design that allows users to upgrade components without replacing the entire unit.

How to Apply This:

  • Use eco-friendly materials (e.g., bamboo, recycled metals).
  • Design for longevity—ensure products can be repairable or upgradable.

5. Leverage Modularity for Customization

The Red Dot-winning "Samsung Modular Home System" allows users to mix and match furniture pieces (e.g., sofas, shelves, desks) with magnetic connectors for easy assembly.

How to Apply This:

  • For furniture brands, create interchangeable parts that let customers reconfigure spaces as their needs change.
  • In tech, design modular laptops where users can swap batteries, screens, or keyboards.

6. Focus on "Tactile Design" for Premium Feel

Samsung’s 2026 Red Dot-winning "QLED Soundbar" features:

  • Hand-rubbed aluminum finishes for a luxury feel.
  • Textured grips that prevent slipping.

How to Apply This:

  • Add tactile elements (e.g., raised buttons, smooth curves) to make products feel premium.
  • Use different materials (e.g., wood, metal, plastic) in strategic placements to guide the user’s hand.

7. Tell a Story Through Packaging

The Red Dot-winning "Samsung Galaxy Watch Packaging" was designed as a miniature art piece:

  • Foldable cardboard that transforms into a stand for the watch.
  • Engraved with the user’s name (personalized touch).

How to Apply This:

  • Make packaging an experience—e.g., interactive QR codes, hidden messages.
  • Use sustainable materials (e.g., recycled paper, biodegradable inks).

8. Collaborate with Artists to Push Boundaries

Samsung partnered with **Korean artist Kim Joon-ho to create the "Neo QLED Art Series", where:

  • Abstract patterns were embedded into the TV’s backlighting.
  • Limited-edition designs were released as collectible art pieces.

  • Invite artists to co-design products.
  • Turn products into art installations (e.g., interactive light displays).

Common Mistakes in Design Innovation (And How to Avoid Them)

1. Overlooking User Needs in Favor of "Wow" Factors

Mistake: Designing a visually stunning product that no one actually uses because it’s too complex. Samsung’s Fix:

  • User testing at every stage—even prototypes were tested with real consumers.
  • Simplifying interactions (e.g., gesture controls that are intuitive, not gimmicky).

How to Avoid:

  • Conduct usability studies before finalizing designs.
  • Avoid "design for design’s sake"—every feature should serve a purpose.

2. Ignoring Material Costs in Favor of Aesthetics

Mistake: Using premium materials that drive up costs without justifying the value. Samsung’s Fix:

  • Balancing cost and craftsmanship—e.g., using high-quality plastics that look like metal but are lighter and cheaper.
  • Modular components that reduce waste and lower production expenses.

How to Avoid:

  • Work with suppliers to find cost-effective alternatives.
  • Prioritize durability—cheap materials may save upfront costs but increase long-term returns.

3. Failing to Consider the Full Lifecycle of the Product

Mistake: Designing a beautiful product that ends up in a landfill because it’s not repairable or recyclable. Samsung’s Fix:

  • Modular designs that allow easy upgrades.
  • Recyclable materials from sourcing to disposal.

How to Avoid:

  • Adopt a "circular design" approach—plan for repair, reuse, and recycling.
  • Partner with recycling programs to ensure end-of-life sustainability.

4. Underestimating the Importance of Packaging

Mistake: Treating packaging as an afterthought rather than a brand experience. Samsung’s Fix:

  • Packaging as part of the design—e.g., the Galaxy Watch box doubles as a stand.
  • Eco-friendly materials that reduce waste.

How to Avoid:

  • Design packaging with purpose—can it be reused, recycled, or repurposed?
  • Avoid excessive plastic—opt for minimalist, sustainable alternatives.

5. Not Aligning Design with Brand Storytelling

Mistake: Creating a visually impressive product that doesn’t reflect the brand’s values. Samsung’s Fix:

  • Every design decision ties back to Samsung’s mission—innovation for a better future.
  • Limited-edition collaborations (e.g., artist-designed TVs) that enhance brand prestige.

How to Avoid:

  • Ensure design aligns with brand identity.
  • Use storytelling in marketing to highlight the design’s purpose.
