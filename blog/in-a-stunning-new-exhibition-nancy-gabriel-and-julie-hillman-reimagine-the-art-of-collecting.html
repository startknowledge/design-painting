Chapter 1: The Philosophy Behind Reimagining the Art of Collecting

From Hoarding to Storytelling: The Evolution of Collecting

Traditionally, collecting was seen as a passive accumulation—buying objects because they were rare, expensive, or aesthetically pleasing. But Gabriel and Hillman argue that true collecting is an active, intentional, and often collaborative process.

Their exhibition, "Reimagining the Art of Collecting," is structured around three core principles:

  1. Collecting as a Narrative – Every object should contribute to a larger story, whether personal or cultural.
  2. Collecting as an Act of Resistance – Some collections challenge norms, preserve forgotten histories, or advocate for social change.
  3. Collecting as a Living Practice – The best collections evolve, adapt, and invite interaction rather than remaining static.

Why This Approach Matters in Today’s World

With climate change, cultural erasure, and rapid technological shifts, collecting isn’t just about owning—it’s about preserving meaning. Hillman, a former curator at the Whitney Museum of American Art, emphasizes that:

"A collection should be a conversation starter, not just a display of wealth. The most powerful collections ask questions, provoke emotions, and leave a legacy."

This shift aligns with a 2024 Pew Research Center study, which found that 72% of millennials and Gen Z collectors prefer experiential and socially impactful acquisitions over traditional fine art.

Chapter 2: 8 Actionable Strategies to Reimagine Your Own Collecting Practice

Inspired by Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition, here are eight practical ways to transform your collecting from a hobby into a meaningful, dynamic, and impactful endeavor.

Strategy 1: Define Your "Why" – The Emotional Core of Your Collection

Before buying anything, ask yourself:

  • What does this object represent to me?
  • How does it fit into my larger narrative?
  • Would I still want it in 10 years?

Gabriel’s work often begins with personal anecdotes. For example, she once acquired a vintage typewriter not because it was rare, but because it was the last one her grandmother used to write letters. The object became a bridge between past and present.

Action Step:

  • Write a mission statement for your collection. Example: "I collect objects that remind me of my grandmother’s resilience, ensuring her stories are never forgotten."

Strategy 2: Curate with Intent – The Power of Thematic Collections

Instead of collecting randomly, group objects around a central theme. Hillman’s exhibition features a section on "Objects of Resistance," where each piece—from a protest banner to a censored book—tells a story of defiance.

Examples of Thematic Collections:

  • "Objects of My Childhood" – A mix of toys, schoolbooks, and handwritten notes.
  • "Forgotten Crafts" – Traditional tools, textiles, or recipes from a disappearing culture.
  • "Climate Change Witnesses" – Artifacts like old weather records, protest signs, or recycled materials.

Action Step:

  • Start a thematic "collection-in-progress" with 3-5 related items. Document why each one matters.

Strategy 3: Seek Out "Unconventional" Objects

Gabriel and Hillman reject the idea that only "high art" deserves a place in a collection. Their exhibition includes:

  • A broken clock (symbolizing time’s fragility)
  • A child’s handprint in clay (a reminder of impermanence)
  • A vintage radio (representing communication before the digital age)

Why It Works:

  • Breaks the mold of what a "serious" collection should look like.
  • Encourages deeper meaning—sometimes the most powerful objects are the ones we overlook.

Action Step:

  • Next time you’re out, look for one "ordinary" object that feels extraordinary. Ask: "What story could this tell?"

Strategy 4: Document Your Collection Like a Curator

Most collectors treat their collections as private treasures, but the most engaging ones are shared and interpreted. Hillman’s exhibition includes audio recordings of collectors explaining their choices, turning passive viewing into an interactive experience.

How to Apply This:

  • Keep a journal of each acquisition, noting:
    • Where you found it
    • The emotion it evoked
    • How it fits into your narrative
  • Create a digital archive (using platforms like Google Arts & Culture or a private blog).

Action Step:

  • Dedicate one hour a month to documenting your collection. Share a single story online (even if it’s just with friends).

Strategy 5: Collect with a Purpose – Activist & Ethical Collecting

Not all collections are neutral. Some challenge systems, preserve marginalized voices, or advocate for change. Gabriel’s work often includes objects from social movements, such as:

  • A 1960s civil rights protest sign
  • A handmade quilt from a refugee camp
  • A vintage feminist zine

How to Start an Ethical Collection:

  • Support underrepresented artists (check platforms like Artsy’s "Diverse Voices" section).
  • Acquire from ethical sources (avoid looted artifacts; opt for fair-trade or repurposed items).
  • Donate or lend pieces to causes that align with your values.

Action Step:

  • Research one ethical collector (like Nina Chanel Abney, who uses pop culture to critique systemic racism) and study their approach.

Strategy 6: Make Your Collection Interactive – Beyond the Static Display

Hillman’s exhibition encourages participation—viewers are invited to:

  • Write messages on a chalkboard about their own collecting habits.
  • Handle objects (not just observe them).
  • Engage in a live Q&A with the curators.

Ways to Make Your Collection Interactive:

  • Host a "collection night" where friends can ask questions.
  • Create a "touch table" with a few key objects.
  • Use augmented reality (AR) to tell stories about your pieces (apps like Metaverse Museum can help).

Action Step:

  • Plan one interactive element for your collection this year (even if it’s just a digital "tour" for family).

Strategy 7: Embrace Imperfection – The Beauty of "Found" Objects

Gabriel’s philosophy includes embracing flaws. She once acquired a chipped porcelain teacup because the crack reminded her of how life is never perfect—and that’s okay.

How to Find Meaning in "Imperfect" Objects:

  • Look for signs of use (scuffs, stains, repairs) as evidence of a story.
  • Repurpose old objects (turn a vintage suitcase into a display shelf).
  • Collect "failed" art (like a half-finished painting or a rejected design).

Action Step:

  • Next time you’re thrift shopping, intentionally seek out something damaged or unusual. Ask: "What makes this special?"

Strategy 8: Let Your Collection Evolve – The Living Museum Approach

The best collections don’t stay static. Hillman’s exhibition includes a "Living Collection" section where objects are rotated, reinterpreted, and even destroyed (in a controlled way) to symbolize transience and renewal.

How to Keep Your Collection Alive:

  • Rotate displays every few months.
  • Add new layers to old objects (e.g., write a poem on a postcard you already own).
  • Let go of items that no longer resonate (donate, sell, or repurpose them).

Action Step:

  • Audit your collection and decide which 5% of items you’re ready to let go of.

Chapter 3: Real-World Examples of Transformative Collections

Example 1: The "Memory Lane" Collection of a Holocaust Survivor

A woman in her 80s began collecting small objects tied to her childhood in Europe before the war:

  • A child’s wooden train set (given to her by her father)
  • A handwritten letter from a cousin who perished
  • A vintage photograph of her family

She didn’t just display them—she wrote a book about each item, turning her collection into a living testament to her past. Today, her collection is loaned to museums and used in educational programs about Holocaust remembrance.

Lesson: A collection can be a legacy, not just a hobby.

Example 2: The "Forgotten Crafts" Archive of a Folk Artist

A retired textile artist in rural India began collecting traditional weaving tools, dyes, and patterns that were disappearing as younger generations moved to cities. She:

  • Documented each tool’s history (some were over 100 years old).
  • Taught workshops using the original methods.
  • Donated her collection to a local museum, ensuring the craft wasn’t lost.

Lesson: Collecting can preserve culture before it’s erased.

Example 3: The "Climate Change Witness" Collection of an Environmental Activist

An artist in California started collecting objects that symbolize environmental degradation:

  • A plastic bottle from a beach cleanup
  • A tree sapling planted in a deforested area
  • A vintage oil painting (now repurposed as a protest banner)

She rotates these pieces in her home and uses them in public art installations, turning her collection into a mobile exhibit for climate awareness.

Lesson: A collection can be a tool for activism.

Chapter 4: Common Mistakes Collectors Make (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most passionate collectors can fall into trapstops that drain the joy from their hobby. Here’s how to steer clear of the most common pitfalls.

Mistake 1: Collecting Without a Clear Vision

Problem: Buying objects just because they’re "cool" or "cheap" leads to a disorganized, meaningless collection.

Solution:

  • Define your "why" before your first purchase.
  • Ask: "Does this fit my narrative?" before adding it.

Example: Someone buys a vintage lamp because it’s "aesthetic," but it doesn’t connect to any personal or cultural story. Later, they realize it’s just taking up space.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the Emotional Weight of Objects

Problem: Treating collections like investments rather than emotional anchors.

Solution:

  • Engage with objects—touch them, hold them, write about them.
  • Avoid purely financial decisions (unless you’re a serious investor).

Example: A collector buys a rare watch for its resale value but never looks at it. Years later, it’s dusty and forgotten.

Mistake 3: Hoarding Instead of Curating

Problem: Letting collections grow uncontrollably without thought.

Solution:

  • Set a limit (e.g., "No more than 10 new items per year").
  • Regularly declutter—ask: "Does this still matter to me?"

Example: A person accumulates hundreds of books but never reads them, turning their home into a cluttered library.

Mistake 4: Overlooking the Story Behind the Object

Problem: Focusing only on rarity or price instead of meaning.

Solution:

  • Research the object’s history before buying.
  • Ask the seller for context (many thrift store finds have hidden stories).

Example: Someone buys a vintage camera because it’s "cool" but doesn’t know it was used by a war photographer. The object loses its magic without context.

Mistake 5: Keeping Collections Private

Problem: Treating collections as personal treasures rather than shared experiences.

Solution:

  • Document and share your collection (even in small ways).
  • Invite others to engage with it (host viewings, write about it).

Example: A collector spends years building a stunning art collection but never shows it to anyone, missing the joy of connection.
