Why the Art of Collecting Is Changing: A New Era of Meaning

From Hoarding to Storytelling: The Shift in Collecting Mindsets

For decades, collecting was often seen as a passive activity—accumulating objects without deeper intention. However, a 2021 study by the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) found that 78% of millennials and Gen Z collectors prioritize emotional and narrative value over pure investment potential.

Nancy Gabriel and Julie Hillman embody this shift. Their work doesn’t just showcase objects; it weaves them into a larger story. Whether through immersive installations, digital archives, or collaborative curation, they prove that the best collections are those that evoke curiosity, spark conversation, and transcend mere possession.

The Role of Technology in Modern Collecting

The digital age has democratized collecting. NFTs, augmented reality (AR) catalogs, and AI-driven discovery tools now allow collectors to engage with their holdings in ways previously unimaginable.

  • Blockchain verification ensures provenance, reducing forgery risks.
  • Virtual exhibitions (like those hosted on Google Arts & Culture) let collectors experience curated displays from anywhere.
  • Social media platforms (Instagram, TikTok, and Pinterest) have turned collecting into a shared cultural experience, where curation is as much about aesthetic alignment as it is about personal taste.

Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition leverages these tools, blending physical and digital elements to create a multi-sensory experience. Their approach suggests that the future of collecting lies not just in what you own, but in how you share and interpret it.

8 Actionable Strategies to Reimagine Your Own Collection

Inspired by Gabriel and Hillman’s methods, here are eight practical ways to transform your collecting from a passive hobby into an active, meaningful, and engaging pursuit.

1. Curate with a Narrative in Mind

What it means: Instead of collecting randomly, build a story around your pieces. Ask yourself:

  • What themes unite my collection?
  • How can I arrange these objects to tell a cohesive tale?
  • What emotions or memories do they evoke?

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition doesn’t just display objects—it maps their journey through time, culture, and personal significance. For example, one section features vintage typewriters alongside modern digital tools, framing them as tools of communication across eras.

Action step:

  • Start a collection journal where you document each piece’s origin, meaning, and place in your larger narrative.
  • Use Pinterest boards or digital scrapbooking to visually map your collection’s evolution.

2. Blend Physical and Digital Collecting

What it means: The best collections today exist both in the physical world and online. This means:

  • Digitizing your collection (via high-quality photos, metadata, and virtual tours).
  • Using AR/VR to create immersive experiences (e.g., a 3D model of your gallery space).
  • Sharing your collection on social media in a way that invites others to engage.

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition includes a QR code gallery, where visitors can scan to access expanded stories, artist interviews, and historical context for each piece. Some objects are also augmented with AR filters, allowing viewers to "see" them in different settings.

Action step:

  • Create a simple website or blog (using platforms like Squarespace or WordPress) to document your collection.
  • Use Instagram Reels or TikTok to showcase "behind-the-scenes" moments of your curation process.

3. Prioritize Rarity Over Value (Sometimes)

What it means: While investment potential is important, the most meaningful collections often focus on uniqueness and personal significance rather than market value.

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition includes lesser-known artists and overlooked objects, proving that true value lies in the story, not just the price tag. For instance, they feature a 1970s ceramic mug from a small studio in Japan, explaining its cultural context rather than its auction potential.

Action step:

  • Research niche markets—sometimes, the most interesting objects are those that aren’t mainstream.
  • Attend local auctions, estate sales, and pop-up markets where hidden gems often appear.

4. Make Your Collection Interactive

What it means: The best collections invite engagement. This could mean:

  • Hosting viewing parties for friends.
  • Creating a "guestbook" where visitors can leave notes about their favorite pieces.
  • Using interactive displays (like touchscreens or QR codes) to provide deeper insights.

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition includes "touch tables" where visitors can handle replicas of historical objects, along with audio recordings of artists discussing their work. Some displays even feature interactive light projections that change based on viewer movement.

Action step:

  • If you have a physical space, design a "visitor experience"—even if it’s just a small area where friends can sit and discuss your collection.
  • Use Google Forms or a shared digital album to let others contribute their thoughts on your pieces.

5. Collect Experiences, Not Just Objects

What it means: The most fulfilling collections often include experiences—tickets to concerts, concertina programs, travel souvenirs, or even digital collectibles like virtual concert passes.

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition includes a section on "ephemeral collecting," featuring concert tickets, movie posters, and festival programs—objects that document moments rather than just things.

Action step:

  • Start a "memory collection"—include tickets, menus, postcards, or photos from trips, events, or milestones.
  • Document your collecting journey with videos, podcasts, or written essays about why each piece matters to you.

6. Collaborate with Other Collectors

What it means: The best collections are often co-created. This could involve:

  • Swapping items with other collectors.
  • Co-curating an exhibition (even a small one).
  • Joining a collecting club or online community (like Reddit’s r/Collecting or Facebook groups).

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition was partially shaped by input from other collectors, who contributed personal stories and alternate arrangements for certain pieces.

Action step:

  • Reach out to local collectors and propose a pop-up exhibition at a café or gallery.
  • Participate in online forums where you can trade knowledge and recommendations.

7. Use Color, Light, and Space to Enhance Your Collection

What it means: The presentation of your collection is just as important as the objects themselves. Play with:

  • Color palettes (e.g., a monochromatic display for a minimalist collection).
  • Lighting (warm tones for cozy vibes, cool tones for modern aesthetics).
  • Negative space (letting objects breathe rather than cramming them together).

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition uses dynamic lighting to highlight different themes—soft amber for nostalgia, bold blues for modernity. They also rotate pieces seasonally, keeping the display fresh.

Action step:

  • Experiment with different layouts in your space (even if it’s just a shelf or a table).
  • Use LED strips or smart bulbs to adjust lighting based on the mood you want to create.

8. Document and Preserve for Future Generations

What it means: The most lasting collections are those that outlive their owners. This involves:

  • Creating a digital archive (with high-res photos, provenance documents, and stories).
  • Writing a legacy letter explaining your collection’s significance.
  • Donating or lending pieces to museums or educational institutions.

How Gabriel and Hillman apply it: Their exhibition includes a "legacy section," where visitors can leave digital legacies for future collectors—audio messages, written notes, or even AI-generated "digital heirlooms" that evolve over time.

Action step:

  • Start a family archive where you document your collection’s history.
  • Consider donating a piece to a local museum or university—it’s a way to ensure your collection lives on.

Real-World Examples: How Nancy Gabriel and Julie Hillman Reimagine Collecting

Example 1: The "Unseen Collection" – Curating What Was Overlooked

One of the most striking aspects of their exhibition is the "Unseen Collection"—a curated selection of objects that were once discarded or forgotten.

  • The Object: A 1950s vintage sewing machine from a small-town tailor.
  • The Story: Gabriel and Hillman discovered it in a garage sale, where it was about to be thrown away. They researched its local history, found the original owner’s daughter, and reconstructed its journey from a family heirloom to a near-forgotten artifact.
  • The Display: Instead of placing it on a pedestal, they mounted it on a rotating platform, with audio recordings of the tailor’s daughter sharing memories. A QR code links to a digital timeline of the machine’s life.

Why It Matters: This approach proves that the most meaningful collections aren’t always the most valuable—they’re the ones that tell untold stories.

Example 2: The "Living Archive" – Blending Physical and Digital

Their "Living Archive" section is a hybrid of physical objects and digital storytelling.

  • The Object: A first-edition book from a rare literary series.
  • The Story: The book itself is displayed, but touching it triggers an AR experience where visitors can see the author’s handwritten notes and watch a video of the publisher discussing its printing history.
  • The Interactive Element: Visitors can leave digital "bookmarks" (short notes or drawings) that appear in a shared online gallery.

Why It Matters: This shows how technology can enhance, rather than replace, the tactile experience of collecting.

Example 3: The "Collaborative Curator" – Letting Others Shape the Experience

In one gallery, visitors were invited to participate in the curation process.

  • The Concept: A "Choose Your Own Adventure" display where visitors could vote on which objects to feature next.
  • The Result: The exhibition evolved in real-time, with some pieces being rotated based on public interest.
  • The Takeaway: This proves that the best collections are those that invite participation, making the experience personal and dynamic.

Why It Matters: It challenges the idea that collecting is a solitary activity—instead, it’s a shared, evolving conversation.

Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most passionate collectors can fall into pitfalls that diminish the joy of their hobby. Here are five common mistakes—and how to steer clear of them.

1. Collecting Without a Clear Vision

The Mistake: Buying objects randomly without a theme, budget, or long-term plan.

How to Avoid It:

  • Define your "why"—are you collecting for investment, nostalgia, or aesthetic pleasure?
  • Set a budget and stick to it.
  • Start small—build a mini-collection before expanding.

2. Overlooking Provenance and Authenticity

The Mistake: Assuming an object is genuine or valuable without proper research.

How to Avoid It:

  • Always ask for documentation (certificates of authenticity, appraisals).
  • Use trusted platforms (like 1stDibs, Artsy, or local auction houses) that verify provenance.
  • Consult experts before making high-value purchases.

3. Neglecting the Physical Space

The Mistake: Displaying objects haphazardly, leading to a cluttered, uninspiring collection.

How to Avoid It:

  • Designate a specific area (even a small shelf or wall) for your collection.
  • Use proper lighting and framing to highlight key pieces.
  • Rotate displays seasonally to keep things fresh.

4. Collecting Only for Investment

The Mistake: Treating collecting as a financial strategy rather than a passion.

How to Avoid It:

  • Balance investment pieces with personal favorites.
  • Remember that the best collections are those that bring you joy.
  • Diversify—don’t put all your eggs in one market (e.g., only rare coins or NFTs).

5. Not Documenting or Sharing Your Collection

The Mistake: Keeping your collection private, missing out on community and legacy.

How to Avoid It:

  • Start a blog, Instagram, or YouTube channel to document your journey.
  • Join collecting groups (online or in-person) to learn and share.
  • Consider donating or lending pieces to museums or educational programs.
