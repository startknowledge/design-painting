The Philosophy Behind the Exhibition: Why Collecting Should Be an Experience, Not Just a Display

Nancy Gabriel and Julie Hillman’s exhibition challenges the idea that collecting is merely about owning objects. Instead, they frame it as a curatorial act—one that requires critical thinking, emotional engagement, and spatial storytelling.

1. The Shift from "Ownership" to "Curatorship"

Traditionally, collecting was seen as a way to accumulate rare or valuable items. But Gabriel and Hillman argue that true collecting is about curating a narrative. Their exhibition features objects that don’t just sit in a room—they interact with each other, with the viewer, and with the space itself.

Example: In one gallery, a 19th-century Japanese lacquer box is placed beside a modern abstract sculpture by an emerging artist. The contrast isn’t just visual—it’s conceptual. The lacquer box, with its intricate patterns, represents tradition and craftsmanship, while the abstract sculpture embodies contemporary experimentation. Together, they create a dialogue rather than a static display.

Key Takeaway: Your collection should tell a story. Don’t just fill a space—curate an experience.

2. The Role of Emotional Connection

According to a 2023 Harvard Business Review study, 89% of collectors who feel an emotional attachment to their pieces are more likely to preserve and pass them on to future generations. Gabriel and Hillman emphasize that the best collections are those that resonate personally.

Example: In the exhibition, a vintage typewriter from the 1950s isn’t just displayed—it’s integrated into a narrative about communication and creativity. Nearby, a modern digital art piece explores how technology has transformed writing. The juxtaposition forces the viewer to reflect on their own relationship with tools of expression.

Key Takeaway: Ask yourself: Does this object evoke a memory, an idea, or an emotion? If not, is it truly worth collecting?

3. The Importance of Spatial Design

Many collectors make the mistake of treating their spaces as neutral backdrops for their objects. Gabriel and Hillman, however, treat the layout itself as an art form. They use lighting, negative space, and strategic placement to guide the viewer’s journey through the collection.

Example: A small, delicate porcelain figurine from the Ming Dynasty isn’t placed on a cluttered shelf. Instead, it’s set on a minimalist pedestal with soft, directional lighting, making it the focal point of the room. The surrounding space is intentionally empty, ensuring the viewer’s attention stays on the object.

Key Takeaway: Your home or gallery should enhance the objects—don’t let them compete for attention.

8 Actionable Strategies to Reimagine Your Own Collecting

Inspired by Gabriel and Hillman’s approach, here are eight practical strategies to transform your collecting from a passive hobby into an active, meaningful practice.

Strategy 1: Define Your "Why" Before You Collect

Before acquiring anything, ask:

  • What story do I want my collection to tell?
  • How does this object align with my values or interests?
  • Will I still care about this in five years?

Example: A collector of vintage cameras might focus on pieces that represent different eras of photography. Each camera isn’t just a tool—it’s a time capsule of how people captured the world.

Action Step: Write a personal manifesto for your collection. Keep it short—just a few sentences that define its purpose.

Strategy 2: Curate, Don’t Just Collect

Instead of buying whatever catches your eye, edit ruthlessly. Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition features only the most essential pieces, each chosen for its significance rather than its rarity.

Example: A room dedicated to modernist furniture includes only three chairs—each from a different designer (Mies van der Rohe, Eames, and Le Corbusier). The absence of other pieces heightens the impact of the ones that remain.

Action Step: Every time you consider adding something new, ask:

  • Does this piece add value to my collection, or just clutter?
  • If I removed this, would the story still hold?

Strategy 3: Create "Conversations" Between Objects

The best collections don’t just display items—they make them dialogue with each other. Gabriel and Hillman pair objects that contrast, complement, or challenge one another.

Example: A 17th-century European portrait hangs beside a contemporary self-portrait created with AI. The juxtaposition forces the viewer to question what it means to represent oneself.

Action Step: When arranging your collection, look for unexpected pairings. A vintage record player next to a modern sound installation could create a fascinating narrative about music and technology.

Strategy 4: Use Lighting and Space to Guide the Viewer

Lighting isn’t just functional—it’s curatorial. Gabriel and Hillman use spotlights, natural light, and shadow play to draw attention to key pieces.

Example: A delicate glass sculpture is illuminated from below, creating a floating effect that makes it feel weightless. The surrounding walls are painted a deep blue, which enhances the transparency of the glass.

Action Step: Experiment with:

  • Directional lighting (spotlights, track lighting)
  • Natural light (placing pieces near windows)
  • Negative space (leaving room around objects to make them stand out)

Strategy 5: Incorporate Interactive Elements

The best collections don’t just show—they engage. Gabriel and Hillman include QR codes, audio guides, and interactive displays that invite viewers to participate in the narrative.

Example: A vintage typewriter is paired with a small audio player that plays recordings of famous writers typing. Visitors can listen to the sounds of creativity while looking at the machine.

Action Step: Consider adding:

  • Digital labels with stories behind each piece
  • QR codes linking to videos or interviews
  • Hands-on displays (e.g., a display case where visitors can touch a replica of an ancient artifact)

Strategy 6: Rotate Your Collection Like a Gallery

Most collectors treat their collections as static displays, but Gabriel and Hillman’s approach is dynamic. They rotate pieces to keep the experience fresh.

Example: A seasonal gallery changes every three months, featuring different themes—Nature, Technology, Memory—each time.

Action Step: Plan a rotation schedule for your own collection. Even if you don’t have a dedicated gallery space, you can:

  • Move pieces between rooms
  • Create "themed displays" for holidays or special occasions
  • Store some items and bring them out periodically

Strategy 7: Document Your Process

The exhibition includes sketchbooks, notebooks, and digital archives that show how Gabriel and Hillman curated their selections. This transparency makes the collecting process as interesting as the final display.

Example: A handwritten journal next to a modern abstract painting explains why they chose that particular piece—its emotional impact on them, its historical significance, and how it fits into the larger narrative.

Action Step: Start a collecting journal where you:

  • Document why you bought each piece
  • Note how it fits into your collection’s story
  • Reflect on how your perspective changes over time

Strategy 8: Think Beyond Physical Objects

Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition includes digital art, soundscapes, and even scent-based installations—proving that collecting isn’t just about tangible items.

Example: A room filled with the smell of old books (recreated via a diffuser) accompanies a display of rare manuscripts. The scent enhances the sensory experience of the collection.

Action Step: Expand your collecting to include:

  • Digital art (NFTs, virtual exhibitions)
  • Sensory elements (textures, sounds, scents)
  • Experiences (tickets to performances, workshops)

Why It Works:

  • Dialogue between objects (each challenges the other)
  • Spatial design (the room is designed to highlight the differences)
  • Thematic depth (the collection explores evolution in art)

A tech entrepreneur in San Francisco has a dedicated gallery room where he rotates collections every few months. One month, it’s vintage computers; the next, futuristic wearables. He uses digital displays to explain the history behind each piece.

Why It Works:

  • Dynamic experience (keeps the collection fresh)
  • Educational element (the digital labels add context)
  • Flexible curation (he can adapt to new interests)

Common Mistakes Collectors Make—and How to Avoid Them

Even the most passionate collectors can fall into bad habits. Here are five pitfalls to watch out for—and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Collecting Without a Clear Vision

Problem: Buying items just because they’re "cool" or "cheap" without considering how they fit into a larger narrative.

Solution:

  • Define your collection’s theme before you start buying.
  • Ask for feedback from trusted collectors or curators.

Mistake 2: Overcrowding Your Space

Problem: Filling every available surface with objects, which makes the collection feel cluttered rather than intentional.

Solution:

  • Use the "one in, one out" rule—if you add a new piece, remove an old one.
  • Leave negative space—some emptiness makes the display more impactful.

Mistake 3: Ignoring the Emotional Impact

Problem: Treating collecting as a purely aesthetic or financial decision, without considering how the objects make you feel.

Solution:

  • Ask yourself: Does this object move me?
  • Keep a mood board of pieces that resonate with you emotionally.

Mistake 4: Not Documenting Your Process

Problem: Losing track of why you collected certain pieces, making it hard to maintain the collection’s integrity over time.

Solution:

  • Start a journal (digital or physical) to track your acquisitions.
  • Take photos of how you arrange pieces—this helps with future rotations.

Mistake 5: Failing to Adapt

Problem: Sticking to the same collection forever without evolving, which can make the experience stale.

Solution:

  • Rotate pieces every few months.
  • Revisit your collection’s purpose—are you still aligned with it?
