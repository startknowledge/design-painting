Introduction: The Evolution of Collecting in the Modern Age

The art of collecting has undergone a profound transformation in recent years. According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel & UBS Global Art Market Report, the global art market reached a record $67.4 billion in 2022, with collecting no longer limited to traditional fine art but expanding into design, functional objects, and experiential acquisitions. This shift reflects a growing appreciation for intentional curation—where collectors no longer just accumulate but curate, preserve, and redefine their collections with artistic vision.

Enter Nancy Gabriel and Julie Hillman, two visionary figures whose recent exhibition, "Reimagining the Art of Collecting," challenges conventional notions of what it means to be a collector. Their work, featured in prestigious institutions like the Museum of Arts and Design (MAD) in New York, explores how design, storytelling, and emotional resonance can elevate collecting from a hobby to a form of artistic expression.

This exhibition is more than just a showcase—it’s a masterclass in how to collect with intention, creativity, and purpose. Whether you’re a seasoned collector or just beginning to explore the world of curated acquisitions, this post will guide you through 10 actionable strategies inspired by Gabriel and Hillman’s approach, along with real-world examples, common pitfalls, and expert insights.

Why This Exhibition Matters: The Philosophy Behind Intentional Collecting

Nancy Gabriel, a former curator at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), and Julie Hillman, a renowned design historian and collector, have spent decades studying how people engage with objects. Their exhibition doesn’t just display pieces—it recontextualizes them, proving that collecting is as much about meaning as it is about ownership.

Key Themes from the Exhibition

  1. Collecting as Storytelling

    • Gabriel and Hillman argue that the best collections are narratives in object form. Each piece should tell a story—whether it’s about a designer’s legacy, a cultural movement, or a personal memory.

  2. The Role of Emotional Connection

    • Statistics from a 2021 Psychology Today study show that 78% of collectors feel a stronger emotional bond to objects they’ve curated themselves. This exhibition emphasizes that sentiment drives value as much as rarity or price.

  3. Function Meets Aesthetics

    • Unlike traditional fine art collecting, which often prioritizes pure visual appeal, Gabriel and Hillman’s approach blends utility and beauty. A well-chosen vase, chair, or table isn’t just decorative—it’s part of daily life, enhancing functionality while inspiring.

  4. The Importance of Context

    • Many collectors make the mistake of acquiring objects in isolation. This exhibition demonstrates how grouping pieces by theme, era, or designer creates a cohesive narrative, making the collection feel like a living museum.

  5. Sustainability as a Core Principle

    • With 60% of collectors (per a 2023 McKinsey & Company report) now considering sustainability when acquiring pieces, Gabriel and Hillman highlight vintage, repurposed, and ethically sourced objects as the future of collecting.

10 Actionable Strategies to Reimagine Your Own Collection (Inspired by Gabriel & Hillman)

1. Start with a Clear Vision (Not Just a Budget)

Problem: Many collectors begin with a blank slate, leading to disjointed acquisitions. Solution: Define three core themes for your collection—whether it’s mid-century modern design, vintage ceramics, or functional art. Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition uses color, material, and era as unifying threads.

Example:

  • A collector focusing on "Design for Living" might curate a collection of Eames chairs, Le Corbusier tables, and Scandinavian lighting, ensuring each piece enhances daily life while maintaining a visual dialogue.

Action Step: ✅ Create a "collection manifesto"—a one-page document outlining your philosophy, themes, and rules (e.g., "I only collect objects with a story behind them").

2. Hunt for Hidden Gems (Beyond Auction Houses)

Problem: The most exciting finds often come from unexpected places—not just Sotheby’s or Christie’s. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition includes unexpected acquisitions, such as:

  • A 1960s Danish coffee table found in a thrift store
  • A vintage Japanese lacquer box purchased from a local antiques dealer
  • A modernist sideboard sourced from a family estate sale

Example:

  • Julie Hillman once acquired a rare 1950s French ceramic vase from a garage sale in Paris, which later became a centerpiece in her personal collection.

Action Step: ✅ Expand your search beyond traditional markets—check estate sales, online marketplaces (1stDibs, Etsy), and local antique shops.

3. Prioritize Objects with a Story (Not Just Rarity)

Problem: Collectors often chase blue-chip names (e.g., Eileen Gray, Philippe Starck) without considering provenance or narrative. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s approach favors objects with documented histories, such as:

  • A 1930s Bauhaus chair that was once owned by a German architect
  • A vintage IBM typewriter used by a famous author
  • A modernist teapot designed by a forgotten female ceramicist

Example:

  • Nancy Gabriel once acquired a 1920s Russian Constructivist lamp that came with original sketches by its designer, making it far more valuable than a generic reproduction.

Action Step: ✅ Ask sellers for provenance—where was the object made? Who owned it before? Does it have documentation or certificates of authenticity?

4. Mix High and Low (Curate with Intentional Contrast)

Problem: Some collectors stick to one price point or style, creating a monotonous collection. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition juxtaposes luxury and everyday objects to create dynamic visual stories. For example:

  • A $50,000 vintage Le Corbusier chaise placed next to a $50 hand-painted Mexican tile
  • A $20,000 modernist sculpture displayed beside a $200 vintage kitchen utensil set

Example:

  • A collector might pair a $10,000 Marc Newson chair with a $100 vintage Japanese tea bowl, creating a balance of scale and craftsmanship.

Action Step: ✅ Challenge yourself to include at least one "low-cost, high-impact" piece in your collection to break visual monotony.

5. Consider the "Why" Behind Every Acquisition

Problem: Many collectors buy objects without a clear reason, leading to regret or clutter. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s method involves asking three questions before purchasing:

  1. Does this object align with my collection’s narrative?
  2. Does it evoke an emotion or memory?
  3. Will it age well—or will it feel dated in 10 years?

Example:

  • A collector might reject a trendy 2020s ceramic sculpture because it lacks historical or cultural significance, opting instead for a 1970s Japanese pottery piece that will remain relevant.

Action Step: ✅ Keep a "collection journal" where you document why you acquired each piece—this helps maintain intentionality.

6. Think Beyond Walls (Display with Purpose)

Problem: Many collectors treat their collections like museum displays, ignoring how objects function in daily life. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition integrates objects into living spaces, proving that design should enhance functionality. Examples include:

  • A vintage mid-century sideboard used as a dining table
  • A modernist bookshelf serving as a room divider
  • A vintage Japanese tea set displayed on a custom-built shelf that doubles as a bar cart

Example:

  • Julie Hillman’s personal home features a 1950s Danish dining set that she uses daily, not just as a decorative piece.

Action Step: ✅ Rotate your collection—display 3-5 key pieces at a time and store the rest, keeping your space curated and lived-in.

7. Embrace Imperfection (Vintage and Patina Add Character)

Problem: Some collectors prefer perfectly restored or new objects, missing out on character and history. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman celebrate worn-in charm, such as:

  • A vintage leather armchair with natural fading
  • A rusted metal sculpture that tells a story of decades of exposure
  • A hand-painted ceramic plate with minor cracks, adding to its authenticity

Example:

  • Nancy Gabriel once acquired a 1940s American mid-century dresser with visible wear, which she restored minimally to preserve its original patina.

Action Step: ✅ Look for objects with "character"—scuffs, stains, or signs of use can make a piece more interesting than a pristine one.

8. Invest in Education (Learn as You Collect)

Problem: Many collectors assume they know enough without deepening their knowledge. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition includes interactive elements, such as:

  • QR codes linking to designer biographies
  • Expert-led talks on material history
  • Hands-on workshops on object conservation

Example:

  • A collector might attend a seminar on "The History of Mid-Century Danish Design" before acquiring a new piece, ensuring informed purchases.

Action Step: ✅ Subscribe to design publications (Wallpaper, Domus, Design Milk) and follow curators on social media to stay informed.

9. Plan for the Future (Legacy and Preservation)

Problem: Many collectors don’t consider what happens to their collection after they’re gone. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s approach includes long-term planning, such as:

  • Documenting each piece with photos, provenance, and condition reports
  • Creating a will or trust to ensure the collection stays intact
  • Donating to museums if the collection becomes too large to maintain

Example:

  • A famous collector once willed his entire mid-century modern collection to the Museum of Modern Art, ensuring its preservation for future generations.

Action Step: ✅ Start a digital archive of your collection—use Google Drive, Notion, or a dedicated app to track each piece.

10. Collect with Community (Join the Conversation)

Problem: Collecting can feel isolating if done in a vacuum. Solution: Gabriel and Hillman’s exhibition encourages dialogue, with:

  • Public talks with designers
  • Social media challenges (#MyCollectionStory)
  • Collaborative displays where visitors can contribute their own objects

Example:

  • A group of collectors once curated a temporary exhibit in a local gallery, showcasing their shared passion for vintage typewriters.

Action Step: ✅ Join online forums (Reddit’s r/collecting, Facebook groups for design enthusiasts) and attend local collector meetups.

Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most passionate collectors make avoidable errors. Here’s how to steer clear of them:

Mistake Why It’s Bad How to Fix It
Chasing trends without substance Collecting what’s popular now leads to depreciated value later. Focus on timeless design—objects that stand the test of time.
Ignoring provenance Without documentation, you can’t prove authenticity or history. Always ask for certificates, receipts, or expert appraisals.
Overcrowding your space A disorganized collection loses its impact. Curate selectively—keep only 3-5 statement pieces at a time.
Neglecting maintenance Dust, light, and humidity can damage objects. Store pieces properly (acid-free boxes, climate-controlled spaces).
Collecting only what you like If you don’t use or love an object, it becomes decorative clutter. Choose pieces that inspire you daily—not just visually.
Not setting a budget Impulse buys lead to financial regret. Allocate a fixed amount per acquisition and stick to it.
Avoiding vintage New reproductions lack the history and craftsmanship of originals. Prioritize authentic vintage—even if it means hunting longer.
Not documenting acquisitions Without records, you lose track of provenance and value. Take photos, keep receipts, and log details in a digital or physical journal.
