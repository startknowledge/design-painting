Introduction: The Evolution of Collecting in the 21st Century

The art of collecting has undergone a profound transformation in the last decade. According to a 2023 report by Artnet, global art spending reached $67.4 billion in 2022, with 68% of collectors now prioritizing meaningful connections over mere financial returns. This shift reflects a broader cultural movement—one where collecting is no longer just about ownership but about storytelling, curation, and emotional resonance.

Enter Nancy Gabriel and Julie Hillman, two visionary figures in contemporary art and design, whose latest exhibition—"Reimagining the Art of Collecting"—has redefined how we think about assembling a collection. This groundbreaking show, now on display at The Museum of Contemporary Art (MOCA) in Los Angeles, challenges traditional notions of curation by blending historical artifacts, avant-garde installations, and interactive digital elements into a cohesive narrative.

But what makes this exhibition so revolutionary? And how can collectors—whether seasoned or aspiring—apply its principles to their own spaces? Why This Exhibition Matters: The Philosophy Behind the Curatorial Vision

Nancy Gabriel, a former curator at The Whitney Museum of American Art, and Julie Hillman, a leading designer and founder of Hillman Design, approached this project with a radical departure from conventional collecting. Their philosophy centers on three pillars:

  1. Context Over Commodity – Instead of treating art as isolated objects, they frame pieces within temporal and cultural narratives.
  2. Interdisciplinary Connections – They blend fine art, design, and even ephemera (like vintage postcards or architectural sketches) to create layered experiences.
  3. The Collector as Storyteller – The exhibition encourages viewers to engage actively, suggesting that a collection should evolve with its owner’s life.

"A collection isn’t just a room full of things," Hillman explains. "It’s a living dialogue between the past, present, and future."

This approach aligns with a **2024 study by the Coleccionismo Magazine, which found that 72% of modern collectors seek emotional and intellectual fulfillment over traditional prestige.

10 Actionable Strategies to Reimagine Your Own Collection

Inspired by Gabriel and Hillman’s methods, here are 10 practical ways to curate a collection that is as meaningful as it is visually impactful.

1. Start with a Narrative, Not a Budget

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: Instead of beginning with a price range, they crafted a thematic arc—moving from post-war abstraction to digital surrealism—and then sourced pieces that fit within that story.

How to Apply It:

  • Define a central theme (e.g., "Modernism and Nature," "The Female Gaze in Design," or "Technology in Everyday Life").
  • Research artists, designers, and makers who align with your theme.
  • Build flexibility—allow room for unexpected discoveries that fit the narrative.

Real-World Example: A collector in Chicago curated a collection around "The Aesthetics of Movement." They began with Marcel Duchamp’s Nude Descending a Staircase (a conceptual piece about motion) and expanded to modern kinetic sculptures and vintage dance photography. The result? A dynamic space where each piece feeds into the next, creating a sense of fluidity.

2. Mix High and Low Culture Intentionally

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition includes a 1950s ceramic teapot by Peter Voulkos alongside a digital NFT by Refik Anadol. This juxtaposition forces viewers to question what constitutes "art" and how objects relate to one another.

How to Apply It:

  • Include "everyday" objects that have emotional or historical significance (e.g., a grandmother’s embroidered quilt, a vintage typewriter, or a customized vintage car).
  • Pair fine art with functional design—for example, a modernist chair by Eileen Gray next to a hand-painted ceramic bowl.
  • Use ephemera (postcards, ticket stubs, old letters) to anchor pieces in memory.

Real-World Example: A New York-based designer collected mid-century modern furniture but wanted to break the mold. They added a 1970s vinyl record sleeve by Grace Jones, a handwritten letter from a favorite poet, and a vintage Polaroid camera. The result? A personalized, eclectic space that tells a story about music, art, and nostalgia.

3. Prioritize "Soft Collecting" Over Hard Assets

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: They included tactile, immersive elements—like a sound installation and a touch-sensitive wall—to engage visitors sensually, not just visually.

How to Apply It:

  • Add sensory elements to your collection:
    • Textures (linen, leather, woven fabrics)
    • Sounds (a vinyl record player, ambient music)
    • Scents (a diffuser with a fragrance that evokes a specific era)
  • Create interactive displays—even small ones, like a rotating book display or a magnetic poetry wall.
  • Consider "living collections"—plants, a small aquarium, or a bird feeder to bring nature indoors.

Real-World Example: A London-based artist transformed their loft into a multisensory experience. They hung vintage tapestries with textured weavings, played classical music on a record player, and placed scented candles that matched the era of each artwork. The effect? A tactile, immersive journey rather than a static gallery.

4. Use Lighting as a Curatorial Tool

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition employs dynamic lighting—some pieces are bathed in warm amber, others in cool blue—to guide the viewer’s experience and highlight emotional shifts.

How to Apply It:

  • Layer lighting to create moods:
    • Warm, soft lighting for cozy, intimate spaces.
    • Dramatic spotlights for statement pieces.
    • Adjustable LED strips to change the atmosphere seasonally.
  • Avoid harsh overhead lights—opt for wall sconces, floor lamps, or track lighting instead.
  • Experiment with color temperature (kelvin scale) to enhance or mute colors in your collection.

Real-World Example: A Los Angeles collector used smart lighting to rotate the ambiance of their living room. During the day, natural light filters through sheer curtains, while at night, warm Edison bulbs highlight their mid-century modern furniture. For special occasions, they use color-changing LEDs to match the mood of the event.

5. Incorporate Digital and Physical Hybrids

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The show features AR (augmented reality) elements—visitors can scan QR codes to see historical context or artist interviews—while also displaying physical artifacts side by side.

How to Apply It:

  • Add digital layers to your collection:
    • QR codes linking to artist statements, vintage films, or personal stories.
    • Digital frames that cycle through rotating images (e.g., a family photo gallery alongside a modern painting).
    • Smart mirrors that display curated content when not in use.
  • Use projection mapping to animate objects (e.g., projecting water effects onto a glass sculpture).
  • Maintain balance—don’t let digital elements overpower the physical experience.

Real-World Example: A tech-savvy collector in Berlin installed smart art frames in their home. Each frame rotates between a physical painting and a digital version of the same piece, with artist interviews playing in the background. They also used voice-activated lighting to change the mood with a simple command.

6. Curate for Emotional Resonance, Not Just Aesthetics

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition includes personal mementos—like a childhood sketchbook—to humanize the art and invite viewers into the collector’s world.

How to Apply It:

  • Include objects with sentimental value:
    • A gift from a loved one.
    • A ticket stub from a meaningful event.
    • A handwritten note from an artist.
  • Tell a story—even if it’s personal. For example, a vintage camera could represent "a moment of freedom" or "a connection to travel."
  • Rotate pieces to reflect life changes (e.g., adding a newborn’s swaddle to a collection about family).

Real-World Example: A San Francisco-based writer curated a collection around "The Art of Memory." They included:

  • A typewriter (their first job was in publishing).
  • A vintage Polaroid of their first book signing.
  • A handwritten letter from a mentor. The result? A deeply personal space that evolves with their career.

7. Play with Scale and Proportion

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition contrasts a massive, immersive installation with tiny, delicate objects (like miniature sculptures) to create visual tension.

How to Apply It:

  • Mix large and small pieces:
    • A grand piano next to a tiny ceramic figurine.
    • A floor-to-ceiling tapestry alongside a single, framed postcard.
  • Use negative space—don’t overcrowd. Leave room for breathing.
  • Experiment with height:
    • Hang some pieces high (like wall sculptures).
    • Place others low (like tabletop art or sculptural objects).

Real-World Example: A Miami-based interior designer used scale play in their modern loft. They paired a massive, abstract sculpture with a delicate, vintage teacup on a shelf. The contrast creates visual interest while keeping the space balanced.

8. Consider the "Collection as a Living Entity"

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition is not static—it includes interactive elements where visitors can add their own contributions (like a guestbook for future collectors).

How to Apply It:

  • Make your collection interactive:
    • A guestbook where visitors can leave notes about their favorite pieces.
    • A digital archive where you document additions over time.
  • Rotate pieces regularly—even if it’s just seasonal changes.
  • Invite others to contribute (e.g., friends can lend a piece for a month).

Real-World Example: A New York gallery owner created a "Rotating Collection" where local artists could swap pieces every few months. This keeps the space fresh and dynamic, while also supporting emerging talent.

9. Think Beyond the Walls: Outdoor and Functional Spaces

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition extends beyond the gallery walls, with outdoor installations and functional art (like a bench that doubles as a sculpture).

How to Apply It:

  • Bring art outside:
    • A garden sculpture.
    • Outdoor lighting that enhances the nighttime view.
  • Make functional pieces artistic:
    • A custom coffee table with embedded LED lights.
    • Furniture with hidden storage that also serves as display space.
  • Use art in unexpected places:
    • Art on the ceiling.
    • Wallpaper with a subtle pattern.

Real-World Example: A California homeowner transformed their backyard into a mini gallery. They installed:

  • A stone fountain (functional + artistic).
  • Outdoor string lights that highlight a sculpture.
  • A custom bench made from reclaimed wood.

10. Document and Share Your Process

The Gabriel & Hillman Approach: The exhibition includes a curatorial journal where visitors can read about the thought process behind each piece.

How to Apply It:

  • Keep a collection journal:
    • Note why you bought each piece.
    • Document how it fits into your narrative.
  • Share your journey:
    • Blog about your collection.
    • Post on social media (Instagram, Pinterest).
    • Host a "collection night" for friends.
  • Create a digital archive:
    • A private online gallery (using Google Arts & Culture or Notion).
    • A YouTube channel with walkthroughs.

Real-World Example: A London-based collector started a blog called "The Thoughtful Collector", where they documented their journey—from first purchase to final arrangement. They also hosted monthly "collection talks" where friends could discuss art and design.

Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most passionate collectors can fall into trapstops that undermine their vision. Here are five pitfalls—and how to steer clear of them.

1. Collecting Without a Purpose

The Mistake: Buying pieces just because they’re "cool" without considering how they fit into your space or story.

How to Fix It:

  • Ask yourself: "Does this piece serve a narrative, or is it just filling space?"
  • Take a step back before buying—visualize it in your home.
  • Start small—build a mini collection first to test your theme.

2. Overcrowding Your Space

The Mistake: Thinking more is always better, leading to a cluttered, chaotic environment.

How to Fix It:

  • Follow the "one in, one out" rule—if you add a new piece, remove an old one.
  • Use vertical space (shelves, wall mounts) to keep floors clear.
  • Edit regularly—ask: "Does this piece still excite me?"

3. Ignoring the Emotional Impact

The Mistake: Treating collecting as a financial investment rather than an emotional experience.

How to Fix It:

  • Buy what moves you, not just what’s trendy.
  • Include personal mementos—they add depth to your collection.
  • Rotate pieces to keep the energy fresh.

4. Neglecting Lighting and Layout

The Mistake: Assuming any piece will look good in any setting, leading to visual mismatches.

How to Fix It:

  • Test lighting before finalizing a piece.
  • Use a scale model (or even digital tools like Roomstyler) to plan layouts.
