How an Image of Washington at Prayer Became a Touchstone for the Right: A Deep Dive into Its Cultural and Political Legacy

Introduction: The Enduring Power of a Single Image

In the vast landscape of American political iconography, few images have resonated as deeply—or as divisively—as George Washington at Prayer. This 1851 painting by Christian Schussele, now housed in the Smithsonian American Art Museum, has transcended its original artistic purpose to become a symbol of conservative values, religious patriotism, and even a rallying cry for the modern right.

But how did a 19th-century portrait evolve into a 21st-century political touchstone? How has it been repurposed, reinterpreted, and weaponized by movements from the Moral Majority to Donald Trump’s America First rhetoric? And why does this image still spark such emotional and ideological fervor today?

This post explores the cultural evolution of Washington at Prayer, its political repurposing, and how artists, politicians, and activists have weaponized its symbolism. We’ll also examine actionable strategies for understanding—and even leveraging—such powerful imagery in modern discourse.

The Origins of Washington at Prayer: A Painting Born of Romantic Nationalism

Before it became a right-wing icon, Washington at Prayer was a product of its time—a Romantic-era tribute to America’s founding father.

The Artist: Christian Schussele and the 19th-Century American Imagination

Christian Schussele, a German-American painter, created the work in 1851, during a period when patriotic sentiment was at its peak. The United States was still grappling with regional divisions (the Civil War would erupt just a decade later), and artists like Schussele sought to unify the nation through idealized depictions of its leaders.

The painting shows Washington kneeling in prayer, his hands clasped, his gaze directed upward—an image meant to evoke humility, divine favor, and moral leadership. Schussele’s work was part of a broader trend in American art where religious and national identity were intertwined.

Why Was This Image Commissioned?

The painting was commissioned by Henry Griswold, a New York merchant and art patron, as part of a series of portraits of American leaders. Unlike traditional portraiture, which often focused on worldly power, Washington at Prayer emphasized spiritual devotion—a deliberate choice to frame the Founding Father as more than just a military leader but as a man of faith.

At the time, religion was central to American identity. The Second Great Awakening (a Protestant revival movement) had swept the nation, and many Americans saw Washington’s life as a divine endorsement of the new republic. The painting reinforced this narrative by blending secular leadership with sacred devotion.

The First Political Repurposing: The Civil War and the Birth of a Symbol

The Civil War (1861–1865) marked the first major political repurposing of Washington at Prayer. Both Union and Confederate forces appropriated the image to justify their causes.

The Union’s Use: Washington as a Symbol of Divine Providence

Northern abolitionists and Union supporters reinterpreted the painting to suggest that God was on their side. They argued that Washington’s prayerful demeanor proved that the United States was a nation under divine protection—and thus, the Confederacy’s rebellion was a sin against God.

Speeches, sermons, and propaganda frequently cited the painting, framing the war as a moral crusade. One notable example was President Abraham Lincoln’s 1863 Gettysburg Address, where he invoked Washington’s legacy to unify a fractured nation. While Lincoln didn’t directly reference the painting, the spiritual framing of leadership it represented aligned with his own moralistic vision of the war.

The Confederacy’s Counter-Narrative: Washington as a Southern Patriot

Southerners, however, rejected this interpretation. They argued that Washington—a Virginian—would have opposed secession but would have respected the rights of states to govern themselves. Some Confederate propagandists even distorted the painting to suggest that Washington was praying for the Confederacy’s success, though this was a stretch of historical evidence.

This early political weaponization set a precedent: Washington at Prayer was no longer just an artwork—it was a weapon in ideological battles.

The 20th Century: From Moral Majority to Modern Conservatism

By the late 20th century, Washington at Prayer had been reclaimed by the religious right as a symbol of Christian nationalism. Organizations like the Moral Majority (founded in 1979 by Jerry Falwell) and later the Christian Coalition began using the image to fuse patriotism with evangelical faith.

The Moral Majority and the Birth of Christian Nationalism

In the 1980s, as secularism grew and abortion rights became a political battleground, conservative Christians sought to reassert religion’s role in public life. The Washington at Prayer image was perfect for this mission—it showed a founder in prayer, implying that America was meant to be a Christian nation.

Jerry Falwell and other leaders of the Moral Majority frequently cited the painting in speeches, arguing that Washington’s faith was the foundation of American greatness. They framed separation of church and state as a betrayal of the nation’s original intent, using the image to mobilize voters who believed in a theocratic vision of America.

The Reagan Era: Politics and Pop Culture

President Ronald Reagan, a devout Christian, embodied this fusion of faith and patriotism. His 1984 presidential campaign featured religious imagery, including prayers at campaign rallies and references to Washington’s spiritual legacy. While he never directly used Washington at Prayer, his administration normalized the idea that American leadership was inherently tied to faith.

Meanwhile, conservative media (including Fox News, which launched in 1996) began repeating the narrative that Washington was a Christian, despite scholars debating his actual religious beliefs. The painting became a visual shorthand for this idea.

The 21st Century: Trump, QAnon, and the Weaponization of History

The 2016 election of Donald Trump marked a new phase in the repurposing of Washington at Prayer. Trump’s America First rhetoric, his evangelical support base, and his distrust of "elite" institutions made the image even more potent for the right.

Trump’s Use of the Image: From Campaign to Legacy

During his 2016 campaign, Trump frequently invoked Washington’s legacy, though he rarely mentioned the painting directly. However, his rhetoric about "making America great again" and his appeals to religious conservatives aligned with the Christian nationalist narrative that Washington at Prayer embodied.

After his election, Trump’s administration (particularly Education Secretary Betsy DeVos) promoted the idea that Washington was a Christian, using the painting in official communications. The National Archives even featured the image in exhibits on American founding documents, reinforcing its political symbolism.

QAnon and the Far Right: Turning History into Conspiracy

The QAnon movement, which emerged around 2017, took the repurposing of Washington at Prayer to extreme levels. Conspiracy theorists argued that Washington was secretly a Christian mystic who communicated with God to guide the nation. Some even claimed that Trump was the "chosen one" to fulfill Washington’s divine mission.

While this was far-fetched, it showed how even fringe groups could weaponize historical imagery to mobilize followers. The painting became a symbol of resistance against what they saw as a corrupt, secular establishment.

Why Does Washington at Prayer Still Resonate Today?

So, why does this 170-year-old painting still spark such strong reactions? Several key factors explain its enduring power:

  1. It Reinforces a Narrative of American Exceptionalism – The image suggests that America was "chosen" by God, which appeals to patriots who believe in the nation’s unique destiny.
  2. It Fuses Religion and Patriotism – In an era of declining church attendance, the painting offers a visual shortcut for conservatives to connect faith with national identity.
  3. It’s Easy to Distort – Unlike complex historical documents, a single image can be reinterpreted to fit any political agenda.
  4. It Appeals to Emotion Over Fact – The painting evokes awe and reverence, making it harder to debate logically than, say, a statistical argument.
  5. It’s Part of a Larger Symbolic Arsenal – The right has weaponized other images (e.g., Uncle Sam, the Statue of Liberty, the American flag) in similar ways, making Washington at Prayer just one piece of a larger strategy.

8 Actionable Strategies for Understanding and Leveraging Powerful Political Imagery

If you’re studying political iconography, conservative messaging, or even art history, here are eight actionable strategies to decode and engage with images like Washington at Prayer:

1. Trace the Image’s Historical Context

What was happening in America when this painting was created?

  • For Washington at Prayer (1851): The U.S. was divided by sectionalism, and religion was central to national identity.
  • For modern repurposings: Civil War, Moral Majority, Trumpism—each era reinterpreted the image to fit its ideological needs.

Action Step: Always research when and why an image was created. Context is key to understanding its meaning.

2. Identify Who Commissioned and Promoted It

Who paid for the painting? Who later used it?

  • Schussele was commissioned by a New York merchant, but later groups (Moral Majority, Trump supporters) reclaimed it.
  • Ask: Who benefits from this image’s existence?

Action Step: Look for patrons, political figures, or media outlets that amplify the image. This reveals who controls its narrative.

3. Analyze the Symbolism in the Painting

What visual elements are being emphasized?

  • Washington’s posture (kneeling in prayer)
  • The setting (a simple, sacred space)
  • The absence of modern elements (no flags, no technology—just timeless faith)

Action Step: Break down the composition, colors, and gestures to see how they reinforce a message.

4. Study How It’s Been Repurposed Over Time

How has the image been used in different eras?

  • Civil War: Union vs. Confederacy interpretations
  • 20th century: Moral Majority’s Christian nationalism
  • 21st century: Trump’s "America First" rhetoric

Action Step: Create a timeline of how the image has been reused, distorted, or ignored in different political climates.

5. Compare It to Other Political Icons

How does Washington at Prayer compare to other symbols?

  • Uncle Sam (patriotism)
  • The Statue of Liberty (immigration)
  • The American flag (nationalism)

Action Step: Contrast the image with others to see how it fits into a larger symbolic ecosystem.

6. Examine the Emotional Triggers It Evokes

What feelings does the image provoke?

  • Reverence (for its historical weight)
  • Anger (if someone opposes its message)
  • Nostalgia (for a "simpler" America)

Action Step: Ask focus groups or poll respondents how they react to the image. Emotional responses drive political action.

7. Investigate Who Controls Its Distribution

Where is the image used? Who owns the rights?

  • Smithsonian Museum (official display)
  • Conservative media outlets (Fox News, Breitbart)
  • Political campaigns (Trump’s rallies)

Action Step: Track where the image appears—museums, social media, political ads—to see who is shaping its meaning.

8. Test Its Effectiveness in Modern Discourse

Does the image still "work" today? How?

  • Does it mobilize voters?
  • Does it spark debates?
  • Is it being used in new ways?

Action Step: Run A/B tests (if possible) or analyze social media engagement to see how different audiences respond.

Real-World Examples: How Washington at Prayer Has Been Used

Example 1: The Moral Majority’s Use in the 1980s

In the 1980s, the Moral Majority frequently cited Washington at Prayer in their anti-abortion and pro-family campaigns. They argued that Washington’s faith proved that America was meant to be a Christian nation, and thus government should reflect that.

How They Did It:

  • Speeches referencing Washington’s "divine leadership"
  • Posters featuring the painting with slogans like "America Was Founded on Prayer"
  • TV ads showing the painting alongside anti-communist rhetoric

Result: The image reinforced their narrative that secularism was a threat to American values.

Example 2: Trump’s 2016 Campaign and the "Washington Legacy"

During his 2016 campaign, Trump rarely mentioned the painting directly, but his rhetoric about "America’s greatness" aligned with the Christian nationalist vision it represented.

How It Was Used:

  • Campaign rallies featured flags and patriotic imagery, including subtle nods to Washington’s legacy.
  • Evangelical leaders (like Paula White) linked Trump to Washington’s "divine mission."
  • Conservative media (Fox News) compared Trump to Washington, framing him as a new "founder."

Result: The association with Washington’s spiritual leadership helped mobilize evangelical voters.

Example 3: QAnon’s Conspiracy-Theory Repurposing

The QAnon movement took the image to extreme levels, arguing that:

  • Washington was a secret Christian mystic
  • He communicated with God to guide the nation
  • Trump was the "chosen one" to fulfill this mission

How They Used It:

  • Memes distorting the painting to show Washington "speaking to God"
  • YouTube videos claiming the painting was proof of a "hidden Christian America"
  • Social media posts linking the image to end-times prophecy

Result: The image became a symbol of fringe conspiracy, showing how even sacred symbols can be weaponized for extremism.
