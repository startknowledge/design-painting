How the SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival Turns Forsyth Park into a Living Canvas

Forsyth Park, established in 1870, is Savannah’s most iconic green space, featuring manicured gardens, a historic fountain, and the famous "Forsyth Fountain"—a centerpiece that has inspired countless artists over the decades. The SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival, organized by the Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD), reimagines this park as an ever-changing gallery where art, music, and culture collide.

Key Elements That Make the Festival a Success

  1. A Rotating Gallery of Large-Scale Installations

    • Unlike traditional art festivals that rely on static displays, SCAD’s event features site-specific installations that respond to the park’s architecture and history. For example, one year saw a giant, interactive "Tree of Life" sculpture made of recycled materials, where visitors could climb branches and leave messages on hanging paper leaves.
    • Another standout was a mural depicting Savannah’s maritime history, painted directly onto a 10-foot-tall wooden panel that visitors could walk through, creating an immersive storytelling experience.

  2. Interactive & Participatory Art Experiences

    • The festival thrives on community involvement. In 2023, an installation called "Echoes of the Ogeechee" invited visitors to record their voices and have them projected onto a giant, reflective water feature, blending sound and visual art.
    • A "Paint the Park" station allowed families to contribute to a collaborative mural, with artists guiding them in creating a unified, evolving piece over the weekend.

  3. A Fusion of Traditional & Contemporary Art Forms

    • While some installations were high-tech (think projection-mapped sculptures that changed color with movement), others stayed true to classic public art techniques, such as chalk drawings on the pavement that told Savannah’s ghost stories.
    • A live street performance featured breakdancers choreographed to a mural that was painted in real-time, blending kinetic art with physical movement.

  4. Strategic Placement for Maximum Impact

    • The festival avoids cluttering the park by spacing installations thoughtfully. For instance:
      • Near the Forsyth Fountain, a sound installation played recordings of Savannah’s past, creating an auditory journey.
      • Along the walkways, light-up sculptures guided visitors at night, extending the festival’s magic into evening hours.
      • In the shaded areas, textile art installations (like hanging tapestries) provided a cooler, tactile experience for those seeking respite from the sun.

  5. Cultural & Historical Storytelling

    • Many installations wove Savannah’s history into their designs. One year, a giant book sculpture displayed excerpts from historic letters written by Savannah’s founders, inviting visitors to read and reflect as they walked.
    • Another project, "The River’s Memory," used floating lanterns to represent enslaved people’s journeys along the Savannah River, turning the park into a space for difficult but necessary conversations.

  6. Accessibility & Inclusivity

    • The festival ensures all visitors can engage, regardless of ability. Braille murals, sensory-friendly art stations, and wheelchair-accessible installations were key features.
    • A "Art for All Ages" zone included sensory bins for children, adaptive painting tools for people with limited mobility, and storytelling sessions for those who preferred verbal engagement over visual art.

  7. Sustainability & Eco-Conscious Design

    • Many installations used recycled materials, such as:
      • Upcycled plastic bottles transformed into shimmering wind chimes.
      • Reclaimed wood carved into interactive puzzles that told environmental stories.
      • Biodegradable paints for murals that could be washed away after the festival, leaving no trace.

  8. A Multi-Sensory Experience Beyond Visual Art

    • While painting and sculpture dominated, the festival also incorporated:
      • Aromatic art (scents like sweetgum trees and saltwater to evoke Savannah’s atmosphere).
      • Tactile installations where visitors could feel raised textures representing the city’s brick sidewalks and oak tree roots.
      • Soundscapes that changed based on foot traffic, creating an alive, breathing environment.

8 Actionable Strategies for Creating Your Own Public Art Festival

Inspired by SCAD’s success, here are eight proven strategies to organize a public art festival in your own community—whether in a park, downtown, or public square.

1. Choose a Site with Historical & Cultural Significance

Why it works: Forsyth Park’s 19th-century history gives the festival depth and context. Without a strong connection to the location, art can feel disconnected from the community.

How to apply it:

  • Research your site’s history, legends, and cultural stories.
  • Example: If your festival is in a former industrial area, feature installations that honor workers’ contributions or explore urban renewal.
  • Action step: Interview local historians, elders, and community leaders to uncover hidden narratives that can inspire art.

2. Mix Large-Scale Installations with Small, Interactive Pieces

Why it works: SCAD’s festival balances grand statements (like the Tree of Life) with intimate, touchable experiences (like chalk drawings). This keeps visitors engaged at all levels.

How to apply it:

  • Big installations (e.g., a giant mural, a sound sculpture) draw crowds.
  • Small, participatory art (e.g., a "wish tree" where people hang paper leaves, a paint-your-own tile station) encourages longer stays.
  • Action step: Allocate 30% of space to large works and 70% to interactive, hands-on art.

3. Make Art Accessible for All Abilities

Why it works: Inclusivity expands the festival’s reach. SCAD’s Braille murals and adaptive tools ensure everyone can participate.

How to apply it:

  • Partner with disability advocacy groups to identify barriers in your design.
  • Offer:
    • Large-print signage for visually impaired visitors.
    • Sensory-friendly zones with soft textures and calming colors.
    • Adaptive art tools (e.g., easels with foot pedals for wheelchair users).
  • Action step: Host a community workshop to gather feedback on accessibility needs.

4. Use Storytelling to Connect Art to Local Culture

Why it works: When art tells a story, it emotionally resonates with visitors. SCAD’s "Echoes of the Ogeechee" installation didn’t just look beautiful—it invited reflection.

How to apply it:

  • Work with local writers, historians, and oral historians to develop themes.
  • Example themes:
    • "Voices of the Underground Railroad" (if in a historic abolitionist town).
    • "Industrial Legacy" (if in a former factory district).
    • "Future of the River" (if near a waterway).
  • Action step: Create a "story prompt" for artists to base their work on local history.

5. Incorporate Multi-Sensory Elements

Why it works: People remember experiences, not just what they see. SCAD’s aromatic art and soundscapes made the festival unforgettable.

How to apply it:

  • Sound: Use live musicians, sound projections, or ambient recordings.
  • Smell: Partner with perfumers or essential oil artists to create themed scents.
  • Touch: Install textured walls, raised reliefs, or interactive light panels.
  • Taste (if appropriate): Some festivals offer local food pairings with art installations.
  • Action step: Survey your community to see which senses they’d most enjoy engaging with.

6. Plan for Sustainability & Low Waste

Why it works: Eco-conscious design shows respect for the space and reduces cleanup costs. SCAD’s biodegradable paints and upcycled materials set a great example.

How to apply it:

  • Materials:
    • Use recycled wood, metal, or plastic.
    • Opt for natural, non-toxic paints and dyes.
  • Energy:
    • Power installations with solar panels or kinetic energy (e.g., wind turbines).
  • Post-festival:
    • Donate or repurpose materials after the event.
    • Seed bombs or native plant installations can leave a lasting green legacy.
  • Action step: Partner with local environmental groups to co-design sustainable solutions.

7. Create a Clear Visitor Journey

Why it works: A well-mapped path ensures visitors don’t feel overwhelmed. SCAD’s festival guides people through themes (history → nature → community → future).

How to apply it:

  • Theme-based zones:
    • Zone 1: History & Legacy (e.g., murals of past events).
    • Zone 2: Nature & Ecology (e.g., living installations with plants).
    • Zone 3: Community & Future (e.g., participatory art for ideas).
  • Wayfinding:
    • Use landmarks (like the fountain) as anchors.
    • Provide digital maps (QR codes) and physical guides.
  • Action step: Test the flow of the festival with a small pilot event before the big day.

8. Leverage Local Talent & Collaborations

Why it works: SCAD’s festival elevates local artists while bringing in national talent. This boosts the economy and fosters pride.

How to apply it:

  • Local artists: Offer prizes, exposure, or stipends to attract them.
  • Corporate sponsors: Partner with local businesses for funding or in-kind donations (e.g., a restaurant donates food trucks).
  • Schools & universities: Involve art students in designing or assisting with installations.
  • Action step: Host a "Call for Artists" with clear guidelines on theme, size, and sustainability requirements.

Real-World Examples of Public Art Success (Described in Detail)

While we can’t show images, these vibrant, immersive public art projects from around the world (and inspired by SCAD’s festival) demonstrate how public spaces can become cultural hubs.

1. The High Line, New York City – A Park Built on Abandoned Rails

Concept: When an elevated railway was slated for demolition in the 1980s, artists and activists pushed to turn it into a public park. Today, the High Line is a 2.3-mile-long greenway with site-specific art installations.

How it works:

  • Art as landscape: Some sections feature native plants arranged in geometric patterns, creating living murals.
  • Interactive sculptures: A wind-powered chime system plays different melodies depending on the breeze.
  • Seasonal changes: Artists rotate installations to reflect spring blooms, autumn leaves, and winter textures.
  • Community engagement: The High Line’s "Art on the High Line" program invites local artists to propose temporary installations.

Lesson: Public art doesn’t have to be permanent—temporary installations can revitalize spaces without long-term commitment.

2. The Vessel, Hudson Yards, New York – A Climbing Maze of Public Art

Concept: Designed by Thomas Heatherwick, The Vessel is a 15-story, honeycomb-like structure in NYC’s Hudson Yards. It’s not just a sculpture—it’s an interactive playground.

How it works:

  • Climbing & exploration: Visitors can scale its steps and platforms, creating ever-changing perspectives.
  • Light & shadow play: At night, projections turn the structure into a dynamic light show.
  • Community events: The space hosts yoga sessions, art workshops, and performances.
  • Economic impact: Since its opening, Hudson Yards has seen a 30% increase in foot traffic, proving how public art drives tourism.

Lesson: Functional art (like climbing structures) encourages movement and social interaction.

3. The River of Light, Miami – A Floating Art Installation

Concept: For Art Basel Miami, Tino Sehgal created "The River of Light", a floating platform where artists performed for visitors while projected light turned the water into a canvas.

How it works:

  • Immersive sound & light: Underwater speakers played ambient music, while projections made the river glow.
  • Live performances: Dancers, musicians, and storytellers engaged visitors in real-time interactions.
  • Temporary impact: The installation lasted only a few days, but its viral fame brought thousands of visitors to Miami.

Lesson: Temporary, high-impact installations can create buzz without long-term maintenance costs.
