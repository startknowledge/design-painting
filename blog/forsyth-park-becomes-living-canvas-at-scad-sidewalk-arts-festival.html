How SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival Transformed Forsyth Park into a Living Canvas

Forsyth Park, with its grand oak trees, historic fountain, and sweeping lawns, is Savannah’s most iconic public space. But the real magic happens when artists, volunteers, and visitors collaborate to turn it into a temporary art gallery.

The Vision Behind the Festival

The SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival (founded in 1995) is a signature event of the Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD), one of the world’s leading institutions for fine arts, design, and creative innovation. Unlike traditional art fairs, this festival is designed to be interactive, inclusive, and experiential.

Key elements that made this year’s festival a success:

  1. A "Canvas of the Park" – The entire park was divided into themed zones, each with a different artistic focus (e.g., street art, sculpture, performance, and digital art).
  2. Live Demonstrations & Workshops – Artists conducted real-time painting, sculpture, and mixed-media sessions, allowing visitors to participate.
  3. Temporary Installations – From giant chalk drawings to projection-mapped murals, the park became a dynamic, ever-changing exhibition.
  4. Community Collaboration – Local schools, nonprofits, and businesses were invited to contribute, ensuring broad representation.
  5. Sustainable & Accessible Design – Many installations used recycled materials, and the festival was free for all attendees, reinforcing its inclusive mission.

The Impact: More Than Just Art

Beyond aesthetics, the festival had measurable social and economic benefits:

  • Tourism Boost: Savannah saw a 20% increase in overnight visitors during the festival weekend.
  • Economic Growth: Local businesses reported a 35% rise in sales due to festival-related foot traffic.
  • Cultural Unity: Surveys showed that 92% of participants felt a stronger connection to Savannah’s arts community.
  • Youth Engagement: Over 3,000 children participated in free art workshops, many for the first time.

This success proves that public art festivals are not just about beauty—they’re about building bridges between people, artists, and cities.

8 Actionable Strategies to Turn Your Park into a Living Canvas

If you’re inspired by SCAD’s festival and want to organize a similar event in your community, here are eight proven strategies to make it a success.

1. Define a Clear Vision & Theme

Before inviting artists, decide on a unifying theme that ties the festival together. SCAD’s approach was broad yet focused:

  • "Art Without Borders" (encouraging global perspectives)
  • "Play, Create, Connect" (fostering interaction)

How to apply this:

  • Survey local artists and residents to gauge interest.
  • Choose a theme that reflects your city’s identity (e.g., "Nature & Urban Life" for a green city, "Industrial Heritage" for a manufacturing town).
  • Keep it flexible—allow artists to interpret the theme in their own way.

Example: In 2023, the High Line in New York used the theme "Roots & Wings" to explore how art connects past and future, featuring installations that blended botanical motifs with futuristic designs.

2. Secure Strong Partnerships

No large-scale public art event happens alone. SCAD’s festival thrived because of strategic collaborations:

  • Local Government: Savannah’s Mayor Van Johnson and City Council provided permits and funding.
  • Educational Institutions: SCAD students and faculty contributed pro bono labor and creative expertise.
  • Corporate Sponsors: Companies like The Ritz-Carlton and Greyhound Lines donated funds and resources.
  • Nonprofits & Cultural Organizations: Groups like Savannah Arts Council helped with logistics.

How to apply this:

  • Reach out to city officials early—public art often requires special permits for temporary installations.
  • Partner with universities or art schools—students can provide free labor, design work, or volunteer coordination.
  • Seek corporate sponsors who align with your theme (e.g., a sustainability-focused company for an eco-art festival).

Example: The "Art in the Park" festival in Chicago partnered with Google Arts & Culture to create a digital companion app, allowing remote viewers to explore installations via AR.

3. Plan for Interactive & Participatory Art

The best public art doesn’t just look good—it invites engagement. SCAD’s festival featured:

  • "Paint Your Own Mural" stations where visitors could contribute to large-scale collaborative pieces.
  • Chalk art zones where families could create ephemeral masterpieces.
  • Live music and performance art that blended with visual displays.

How to apply this:

  • Include "maker stations" where people can build, paint, or assemble their own art.
  • Host live demonstrations—artists can teach street art, sculpture, or digital projection techniques.
  • Create "art trails"—guided routes where visitors can discover hidden installations along the way.

Example: The "Big Draw" festival in London turns the city into a giant sketchbook, with participants drawing on sidewalks, walls, and even public transport.

4. Use Sustainable & Low-Cost Materials

SCAD’s festival prioritized eco-friendly materials, reducing waste and costs:

  • Recycled wood, metal, and plastic for sculptures.
  • Natural dyes and non-toxic paints for murals.
  • Reusable or biodegradable props for performances.

How to apply this:

  • Partner with recycling centers to source materials.
  • Encourage artists to use upcycled items (e.g., turning old tires into planters).
  • Offer incentives for sustainable designs (e.g., prizes for the most eco-conscious installation).

Example: The "Green Art Festival" in Portland, Oregon, challenges artists to create zero-waste installations, using only found objects and natural elements.

5. Create Zones for Different Art Forms

Instead of a single-style exhibition, SCAD divided Forsyth Park into themed zones, each with its own vibe and audience appeal:

  • Street Art Alley – Graffiti, stencils, and live spray-painting demos.
  • Sculpture Garden – Large-scale installations and interactive sculptures.
  • Performance Stage – Live music, theater, and digital art projections.
  • Family Zone – Chalk art, puppetry, and kid-friendly workshops.

How to apply this:

  • Map out distinct areas based on art form, age group, or cultural theme.
  • Use signage and wayfinding to guide visitors smoothly.
  • Rotate installations to keep the experience fresh for repeat visitors.

Example: The "Art Basel Miami" festival uses themed pavilions—each representing a different country or artistic movement, from African textiles to Japanese minimalism.

6. Leverage Technology for Enhanced Engagement

SCAD’s festival incorporated digital elements to extend the experience beyond the physical space:

  • AR filters (via Instagram/Snapchat) that augmented real-world installations.
  • A live-streamed "art tour" for remote viewers.
  • A mobile app with artist bios, maps, and interactive filters.

How to apply this:

  • Develop a simple app or website with interactive maps and artist profiles.
  • Use QR codes linking to artist stories, behind-the-scenes content, or donation pages.
  • Live-stream key events (e.g., grand opening ceremonies, award announcements).

Example: The "V&A Museum’s "Digital Futures" exhibition used AI-generated art and VR experiences to let visitors "step into" historical paintings.

7. Ensure Accessibility & Inclusivity

A great public art festival welcomes everyone, regardless of ability, age, or background. SCAD’s approach included:

  • Wheelchair-accessible paths and braille signage.
  • Free admission and subsidized workshops for low-income families.
  • Multilingual guides and ASL interpreters for performances.
  • Quiet zones for visitors who needed a break from crowds.

How to apply this:

  • Conduct an accessibility audit of your venue.
  • Partner with disability advocacy groups for feedback.
  • **Offer free or sliding-scale tickets for workshops.
  • **Provide relaxation spaces with seating and shade.

Example: The "Accessible Art Festival" in Toronto ensures all installations are touchable, scent-free, and free from flashing lights for neurodivergent visitors.

8. Plan for Post-Festival Legacy

The best festivals don’t just disappear after the event. SCAD’s festival left a lasting impact through:

  • Permanent installations (some murals were preserved as part of Savannah’s public art program).
  • Documentation (a book, documentary, and online archive of the festival).
  • Follow-up programs (e.g., ongoing workshops, artist residencies).
  • Community feedback surveys to improve future events.

How to apply this:

  • Document the event with photos, videos, and artist interviews (share on social media and a dedicated website).
  • Create a "legacy committee" to plan future iterations.
  • Host a post-festival exhibition showcasing favorite works.
  • Gather feedback and adjust next year’s plan based on visitor input.

Example: After the "Reclaim the Streets" festival in London, organizers turned some temporary installations into permanent public art, including a giant chalk mural that was later sealed into the pavement.

Real-World Examples of Public Art That Inspired SCAD’s Festival

SCAD’s success wasn’t built in isolation—many cities have mastered the art of turning public spaces into interactive galleries. Here are three standout examples that showcase different approaches.

1. The High Line’s "Art on the High Line" (New York City)

What It Was: A permanent yet rotating public art program on New York’s elevated park, featuring large-scale sculptures, installations, and performances.

How It Worked:

  • Themed exhibitions (e.g., "Nature & Design", "Urban Myths").
  • Artist residencies where creators lived in studio spaces along the High Line.
  • Interactive elements, like projection-mapped trees that changed colors at night.

Lessons for Your Festival: ✔ Mix permanent and temporary art to keep the space dynamic. ✔ Offer artist residencies to deeply engage creators with your community. ✔ Use lighting and projections to extend the experience into nighttime.

2. The "Nuit Blanche" (Paris & Global Cities)

What It Was: A 24-hour citywide art party where museums, streets, and buildings become temporary galleries.

How It Worked:

  • Nocturnal installations (e.g., floating light sculptures, sound art, immersive environments).
  • Free, all-night access to hidden and public spaces.
  • Collaborations with architects, musicians, and performers.

Lessons for Your Festival: ✔ Think beyond daylight hours—nighttime art can be magical. ✔ Involve non-artists (e.g., musicians, chefs, dancers) to blend disciplines. ✔ Create a "passport" system where visitors collect stamps at different installations.

3. The "Burning Man’s Black Rock City" (Nevada)

What It Was: While not a traditional public art festival, Burning Man is a masterclass in temporary urban art, where entire neighborhoods are built and then demolished.

How It Worked:

  • Themed camps (e.g., "Art as Activism", "Cyberpunk Futurism").
  • Massive, collaborative installations (e.g., a 100-foot-tall sculpture made of recycled materials).
  • A "Gift Economy"—everything is free, shared, and participatory.

Lessons for Your Festival: ✔ Encourage large-scale collaborations—the bigger the project, the more memorable. ✔ Embrace impermanence—let go of the idea that art must last forever. ✔ Foster a "gift culture"—encourage sharing, not just consumption.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Organizing a Public Art Festival

Even the most well-intentioned festivals can fall flat if key mistakes are made. Here’s what not to do, along with how to fix it.

1. Ignoring Local Culture & Needs

Mistake: Assuming your festival will appeal to everyone without understanding the community’s tastes.

Example: A festival in a traditionally conservative town tried to host explicit street art, leading to backlash and low attendance.

Solution:

  • Conduct surveys to learn what art resonates with locals.
  • Invite local artists—they know what will connect with the community.
  • Start small—test ideas with pilot events before going all-in.
