The Evolution of SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival: From Humble Beginnings to a Global Model

A Brief History: How It All Started

The SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival was founded in 1996 by Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD) as a way to democratize art and make creativity accessible. Originally a small, local event, it has since grown into one of the largest sidewalk art festivals in the world, attracting artists from 40+ countries and millions of visitors annually.

The festival’s signature approach—large-scale, interactive installations—was pioneered by SCAD’s public art program, which emphasizes collaboration, sustainability, and community engagement. Unlike traditional art fairs, SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival doesn’t just display art—it invites participation, turning spectators into co-creators.

Why Forsyth Park? The Perfect Backdrop for Artistic Transformation

Forsyth Park, Savannah’s most famous green space, spans 12 acres and is home to:

  • The iconic Forsyth Fountain (a 19th-century landmark)
  • Historic oak trees (some over 300 years old)
  • Open lawns, walking paths, and riverfront views

The park’s central location, historic charm, and natural beauty make it an ideal canvas for large-scale art installations. Unlike indoor galleries, Forsyth Park allows artists to work with light, weather, and movement, creating dynamic, ever-changing art experiences.

Fun Fact: In 2023, the festival’s "Living Wall" installation—where 100+ volunteers painted a 50-foot-tall mural in real time—drew record crowds, with 92% of attendees reporting increased sense of community after participating.

8 Actionable Strategies to Turn Any Public Space into a SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival

If you’re looking to replicate the success of SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival in your own city, here are proven strategies used by organizers, artists, and local governments to maximize impact, engagement, and sustainability.

1. Choose the Right Location: Prioritize Accessibility & Symbolism

Why it matters: The best public art festivals don’t just attract visitors—they become a cultural landmark.

How SCAD did it:

  • Forsyth Park was selected for its central location (walkable from downtown Savannah) and historical significance (a beloved local gathering spot).
  • The festival avoided high-traffic roads, ensuring minimal disruption to daily life.

Actionable steps for your city: ✔ Map potential sites using tools like Google Earth to assess:

  • Foot traffic (near parks, transit hubs, or tourist attractions)
  • Weather exposure (avoid areas prone to flooding or extreme heat)
  • Symbolic value (historic landmarks, community centers, or riverfronts) ✔ Partner with local government to secure temporary permits for installations. ✔ Host a "location audit" with artists to test visibility, safety, and logistical feasibility.

Real-world example: In Austin, Texas, the "Austin Sidewalk Arts Festival" (inspired by SCAD) chose Lady Bird Lake as its venue. By integrating art into a natural waterfront setting, organizers created a unique, Instagram-worthy experience that drew 200,000+ visitors in its first year.

2. Curate a Diverse Artist Lineup: Balance Local Talent with National Stars

Why it matters: A mix of established and emerging artists ensures broad appeal while keeping costs manageable.

How SCAD did it:

  • 50% local Savannah artists (supporting the community)
  • 30% national/international artists (attracting out-of-town visitors)
  • 20% student artists (from SCAD and local schools)

Actionable steps: ✔ Launch a "Call for Artists" early (3–6 months before the festival) with:

  • Clear themes (e.g., "Climate Change," "Cultural Fusion")
  • Budget ranges (to attract both solo artists and teams)
  • Technical requirements (e.g., "Must be able to work with natural light") ✔ Host a jury process with local art critics, gallery owners, and community leaders. ✔ Offer tiered participation fees to ensure accessibility for all.

Real-world example: The "Baltimore Sidewalk Arts Festival" (2023) featured:

  • A street mural by local graffiti artist "Invader" (who later sold a piece for $12,000)
  • A collaborative "human sculpture" performance by Baltimore Ballet dancers
  • A DIY "paint-your-own tile" station run by high school art students

This diverse mix kept costs low while maximizing engagement.

3. Design for Interaction: Make Art Experiential, Not Passive

Why it matters: The best public art doesn’t just look good—it makes people feel something.

How SCAD did it:

  • "The Breathing Wall" – A biomorphic sculpture that responded to wind and movement, encouraging visitors to touch, climb, and explore.
  • "Soundscapes of Savannah" – An interactive audio installation where visitors could record their own sounds to contribute to a collaborative composition.
  • "Giant Puppet Parade" – A live, participatory performance where the public helped operate oversized puppets.

Actionable steps to create interactive art: ✔ Incorporate "touchable" elements (sand tables, clay stations, projection mapping). ✔ Use technology (AR filters, QR codes linking to artist stories, live-streamed performances). ✔ Schedule "maker workshops" where visitors can create their own art (e.g., tie-dye, stencil painting). ✔ Plan "performance slots" (street theater, live painting, music) to keep energy high.

Real-world example: At the "Brooklyn Street Art Festival", visitors could:

  • Paint a section of a giant mural (which was later auctioned for charity)
  • Test their balance on a "human tightrope" led by circus performers
  • Record a "sound poem" using a custom app that mixed their voice with ambient city sounds

This multi-sensory approach kept attendees engaged for hours.

4. Leverage Local Culture & History: Make Art Relevant

Why it matters: The most memorable public art tells a story—it’s not just decoration, it’s cultural storytelling.

How SCAD did it:

  • "Haunted Savannah" – A ghostly light installation inspired by the city’s folklore, featuring projected spirits along historic paths.
  • "Rooted in Savannah" – A living sculpture made from recycled shipping containers, symbolizing the city’s maritime history.
  • "The River’s Memory" – A sound installation using historical recordings of Savannah’s Gullah-Geechee community.

Actionable steps to tie art to local culture: ✔ Research your city’s history (archives, museums, oral histories). ✔ Partner with historians, elders, and cultural organizations to co-create themes. ✔ Use local materials (e.g., reclaimed wood, native plants, regional fabrics). ✔ Feature "hidden stories" (e.g., unsung heroes, forgotten landmarks, oral traditions).

Real-world example: The "Denver Sidewalk Arts Festival" (2024) focused on "Mountains & Memory", incorporating:

  • A "story wall" where visitors could write and read personal anecdotes tied to Denver’s Rocky Mountain history
  • A "land art" installation using local rocks and wildflowers to represent Indigenous land stewardship
  • A "music from the mountains" concert featuring folk artists from the region

This deep cultural connection made the festival feel uniquely Denver.

5. Plan for Sustainability: Eco-Friendly Art That Lasts

Why it matters: 78% of festival-goers (per a 2023 survey) prefer events that prioritize sustainability. SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival has made green practices a core part of its mission.

How SCAD did it:

  • "Eco-Brick Mural" – A wall made entirely from recycled plastic bottles, teaching visitors about waste reduction.
  • "Seed Bomb Station" – Attendees could make and take home biodegradable seed bombs to plant native flowers.
  • "Solar-Powered Light Sculptures" – Installations that charged during the day and glowed at night without electricity.

Actionable eco-friendly strategies: ✔ Use biodegradable or upcycled materials (e.g., pallets, old tires, natural dyes). ✔ Offer "zero-waste" stations (compost bins, refillable water stations). ✔ Partner with local environmental groups for cleanup efforts before/after the festival. ✔ Document sustainability efforts for marketing (e.g., "90% of our materials were recycled!").

Real-world example: The "Portland Sidewalk Arts Festival" (2023) introduced:

  • "The Green Canvas" – A giant mural painted with non-toxic, water-based paints that biodegrades naturally.
  • "Upcycled Umbrella Village" – A playful installation made from donated old umbrellas, teaching repurposing skills.
  • "Carbon-Neutral Performances" – Artists used solar-powered sound systems and bike-powered generators.

This eco-conscious approach resonated with millennial and Gen Z audiences, boosting attendance by 45%.

6. Create a Strong Marketing Plan: Social Media, PR & Community Buzz

Why it matters: Even the best art won’t draw crowds if people don’t know about it.

How SCAD did it:

  • TikTok Challenges – Artists and visitors posted #SCADSidewalk videos, leading to 10M+ views in 2023.
  • Local Media Partnerships – Savannah’s WSAV News and The Savannah Morning News ran daily countdowns.
  • "Artist Takeovers" – Social media accounts were handed over to featured artists for a day, giving them platform exposure.

Actionable marketing strategies: ✔ Launch a "Countdown Series" on Instagram/Facebook with:

  • Artist spotlights (interviews, behind-the-scenes content)
  • Visitor testimonials from past events
  • Countdown timers (e.g., "3 days until the festival!") ✔ Partner with influencers (local micro-influencers, travel bloggers, art Instagram accounts). ✔ Offer "Early Bird" discounts for local residents to boost grassroots attendance. ✔ Create a hashtag (e.g., #YourCitySidewalkArt) and encourage user-generated content.

Real-world example: The "Miami Street Art Festival" (2024) used:

  • "The Mural Map" – An interactive Google Map where visitors could check off completed murals as they explored.
  • "Live Painting Streams" – Facebook Live sessions where artists painted in real time, drawing 50,000+ viewers.
  • "Artist vs. AI" – A social media challenge where humans competed with AI-generated art, sparking viral debates.

This digital-first approach doubled pre-event engagement compared to previous years.

7. Ensure Accessibility & Inclusivity: Art for Everyone

Why it matters: Public art should not exclude—it should welcome all ages, abilities, and backgrounds.

How SCAD did it:

  • "Sensory-Friendly Hours" – Quiet times for neurodivergent visitors.
  • "Wheelchair-Accessible Installations" – Ramps, tactile paths, and elevated viewing platforms.
  • "Free Admission for Seniors & Veterans" – A community-focused gesture.
  • "Multilingual Signage" – Information in Spanish, Creole, and ASL.

Actionable inclusivity strategies: ✔ Host a "Community Advisory Board" with disability advocates, elders, and parents. ✔ Offer "Baby-Friendly Zones" (stroller parking, nursing areas). ✔ **Provide sign language interpreters for performances. ✔ Create "Quiet Art Zones" for introverted or overwhelmed visitors.

Real-world example: The "Chicago Sidewalk Arts Festival" (2023) introduced:

  • "The Touch Tour" – A guided experience where visually impaired visitors could explore textured art installations.
  • "The Story Wall" – A braille and large-print mural where anyone could add their voice.
  • "The Baby Art Station" – A low-mess painting area for toddlers, complete with high chairs and changing tables.

This inclusive design led to a 30% increase in family attendance.
