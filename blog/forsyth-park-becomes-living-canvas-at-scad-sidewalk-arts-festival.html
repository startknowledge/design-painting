How the SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival Turns Forsyth Park into a Living Canvas

Forsyth Park, with its 130-year-old oak trees, historic fountain, and sprawling lawns, is already a beloved Savannah landmark. But during the SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival, the park undergoes a metamorphosis—becoming a three-dimensional art exhibition where visitors can walk through, touch, and even contribute to the creations.

The Festival’s Evolution: From Small Beginnings to a Global Phenomenon

The festival began as a student-led initiative by the Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD) in 2010, designed to give emerging artists a platform. Today, it’s a collaboration between SCAD, local government, and private sponsors, drawing international artists, street performers, and food vendors.

Key elements that make the festival a public art success story:

  • Temporary vs. Permanent Installations – While some pieces are meant to be one-time experiences, others (like the Forsyth Park Fountain’s annual light show) become annual traditions.
  • Interactive & Immersive Experiences – Unlike static museum exhibits, the festival encourages participation, whether through live painting, augmented reality (AR) installations, or community murals.
  • Sustainability & Local Impact – Many artists use recycled materials (e.g., pallets, scrap metal, and upcycled textiles) to minimize waste, aligning with Savannah’s eco-conscious tourism goals.

Why Forsyth Park? The Perfect Backdrop for Large-Scale Art

The park’s central location, open spaces, and historical significance make it an ideal canvas. Unlike a traditional gallery, Forsyth Park allows for: ✔ Large-scale murals (e.g., a 100-foot-long collaborative painting along the fountain) ✔ Mobile art installations (e.g., giant inflatable sculptures that visitors can climb) ✔ Sound and light performances (e.g., projected animations onto tree trunks at night)

This flexibility is what sets public art festivals apart from conventional exhibitions—they adapt to the environment rather than forcing the environment to adapt to the art.

8 Actionable Strategies to Create a Public Art Festival Like SCAD Sidewalk Arts

If you’re inspired by the SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival and want to organize your own public art event, here are eight proven strategies to ensure success.

1. Define Your Vision & Audience Before Planning

Problem: Many public art projects fail because they lack a clear purpose—are they meant for families, artists, tourists, or all of the above?

Solution:

  • Identify your primary audience (e.g., SCAD targets students, locals, and tourists).
  • Set a theme (e.g., SCAD’s festival often revolves around "community, creativity, and sustainability").
  • Create a mission statement—e.g., "To make art accessible, interactive, and inclusive for all."

Example: The Burning Man Festival (though not a public art festival) uses a "10 Principles" framework to guide its temporary city. Similarly, SCAD’s festival has an unwritten rule: "Art should engage, not just observe."

2. Secure Permits & Legal Protections Early

Problem: Without proper permits, your festival could be shut down mid-event, leading to legal fees and lost revenue.

Solution:

  • Research local regulations (e.g., Savannah requires special event permits for public gatherings).
  • Work with city officials to define safety zones, noise restrictions, and vendor rules.
  • Get insurance for artists, performers, and visitors.

Real-World Example: The Nashville Public Art Festival faced delays in 2022 because organizers underestimated parking and crowd-control permits. By securing approvals six months in advance, SCAD avoided similar issues.

3. Curate a Mix of Local & International Artists

Problem: A festival with only local talent may lack innovation, while too many outsiders can alienate the community.

Solution:

  • Balance 60% local artists (to foster community pride) with 40% emerging/international talent (to attract visitors).
  • Offer stipends or free booths to low-income artists to ensure diversity.
  • Feature a "Rising Star" competition to discover new talent.

Example: At SCAD Sidewalk Arts, student artists from SCAD’s Savannah campus often collaborate with professional muralists from New York and Berlin, creating a dynamic exchange of styles.

4. Make Art Interactive—Not Just Observational

Problem: Passive art (e.g., static sculptures) can feel unengaging to casual visitors.

Solution:

  • Encourage participation through:
    • Live painting sessions (e.g., a giant canvas where visitors add brushstrokes)
    • AR installations (e.g., smartphone apps that animate murals)
    • Workshops (e.g., "Paint Your Own Tile" stations)
  • Use gamification (e.g., "Find the Hidden Artwork" scavenger hunts)

Real-World Example: The "Forsyth Park Fountain Light Show" (a festival staple) uses projected animations that change with the time of day, making the same fountain feel like a new experience every night.

5. Plan for Accessibility & Inclusivity

Problem: If your festival is only for able-bodied, young, or affluent visitors, it won’t reflect the community it serves.

Solution:

  • Offer ASL interpreters for performances.
  • Provide wheelchair-accessible booths and braille signage.
  • Include art for all ages (e.g., child-friendly coloring stations alongside complex installations).

Example: SCAD’s festival partners with local nonprofits to offer free art supplies for children and discounted tickets for seniors, ensuring broad participation.

6. Leverage Technology for Engagement & Marketing

Problem: Without digital promotion, even the best festival can go unnoticed.

Solution:

  • Create a dedicated website with artist bios, schedules, and ticketing.
  • Use social media teasers (e.g., Instagram Stories with behind-the-scenes content).
  • Launch an app with interactive maps, artist interviews, and AR filters.
  • Partner with influencers (e.g., local art bloggers, travel vloggers).

Real-World Example: The Sydney Festival (Australia) uses a real-time crowd-sourced map where visitors can check in at installations, creating a social feed of the event.

7. Sustainability: Reduce Waste & Carbon Footprint

Problem: Large festivals often generate tons of plastic waste from food packaging and disposable materials.

Solution:

  • Ban single-use plastics (e.g., compostable cups, reusable utensils).
  • Encourage artists to use recycled materials (e.g., pallets, scrap wood, upcycled fabrics).
  • Offer public transit discounts to reduce car emissions.
  • Plant trees or sponsor green spaces as a festival legacy.

Example: At SCAD Sidewalk Arts, many installations are made from salvaged materials, such as old shipping containers turned into interactive sculptures.

8. Create a Legacy Beyond the Festival

Problem: Most public art festivals disappear after one event, leaving no lasting impact.

Solution:

  • Document the festival (e.g., photographs, videos, and artist statements for a permanent archive).
  • Donate artworks to local museums or schools.
  • Plant a "memory garden" where visitors can leave artwork or messages.
  • Plan a follow-up event (e.g., a year-round public art walk in the park).

Real-World Example: The Edinburgh Festival Fringe (Scotland) leaves behind permanent murals in neighborhoods, ensuring the art remains part of the city’s identity.

Common Mistakes in Public Art Festivals (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned festivals can fall flat if they don’t account for these pitfalls.

Mistake #1: Overlooking Logistics (Permits, Safety, Crowd Control)

  • Problem: Without clear pathways, emergency exits, and security, festivals can become chaotic.
  • Solution:
    • Hire professional event planners to manage crowd flow.
    • Post clear signs for restrooms, first aid, and exits.
    • Have a backup plan for rain, heatwaves, or power outages.

Example: The Coachella Valley Music & Arts Festival faced crowd-surge incidents in 2023 due to poor crowd-control strategies. SCAD avoids this by limiting booth sizes and designating "quiet zones" for relaxation.

Mistake #2: Ignoring Artist Needs (Payment, Supplies, Space)

  • Problem: Artists may withdraw last-minute if they feel undervalued or unsupported.
  • Solution:
    • Offer fair compensation (even for volunteers).
    • Provide tools, easels, and materials (don’t assume artists will bring everything).
    • Give clear setup/dismantle schedules to avoid conflicts.

Example: At SCAD, artists receive a "Festival Artist Kit" with brushes, paints, and a portable easel, ensuring they can focus on creating, not logistics.

Mistake #3: Failing to Market to the Right Audience

  • Problem: If your festival is only promoted to art lovers, you’ll miss families, tourists, and first-time visitors.
  • Solution:
    • Target local schools (offer free family days).
    • Partner with hotels and tour agencies for group discounts.
    • Use humor and nostalgia in ads (e.g., "Remember playing in Forsyth Park as a kid? Now it’s a giant art playground!").

Example: The Sydney Festival runs radio ads on family stations and social media campaigns featuring kids interacting with art.

Mistake #4: Underestimating Costs

  • Problem: Unexpected expenses (e.g., weather damage, last-minute permits) can bankrupt a festival.
  • Solution:
    • Create a 30% contingency fund.
    • Get multiple vendor quotes (e.g., catering, security, sound systems).
    • Apply for grants (e.g., NEA, local arts councils).

Example: SCAD’s festival diversifies funding through sponsorships (local businesses), ticket sales, and alumni donations.

Mistake #5: Not Measuring Success

  • Problem: Without data, you won’t know what worked and what didn’t.
  • Solution:
    • Track attendance, social media engagement, and sales.
    • Survey visitors (e.g., "What was your favorite part?").
    • Analyze artist feedback (e.g., "Would you participate again?").

Example: After the 2023 SCAD Sidewalk Arts Festival, organizers noticed that interactive installations had the highest engagement, so they increased their presence in 2024.
