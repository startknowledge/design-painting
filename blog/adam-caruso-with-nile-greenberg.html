Who Are Adam Caruso and Nile Greenberg?

Before diving into techniques, let’s set the stage with who these artists are and why their collaboration matters.

Adam Caruso: The Master of Hyper-Realism

Adam Caruso is a New York-based painter whose work has been exhibited in galleries worldwide, including the National Academy of Design and Salon Caruso. His portraits are renowned for their unmatched lifelike quality, often blurring the line between painting and photography. Caruso’s process is meticulous—he spends hundreds of hours on a single piece, layering glazes and fine details to achieve an almost tactile realism.

What sets Caruso apart is his philosophy of painting as a dialogue between artist and subject. He believes that the best portraits don’t just look real—they feel real, capturing the essence of a person’s soul through brushwork and color.

Nile Greenberg: The Digital Innovator

Nile Greenberg is a digital artist, educator, and founder of the popular YouTube channel Nile Greenberg Art School, where he teaches thousands of students how to paint digitally. His work spans character design, concept art, and hyper-realistic portraits, often using tools like Photoshop, Procreate, and Clip Studio Paint. Greenberg’s approach is accessible yet advanced, making complex techniques understandable for both beginners and professionals.

His collaboration with Caruso highlights a fusion of old and new—where traditional painting meets digital precision. Together, they’ve explored how digital tools can enhance (not replace) classical techniques, offering artists a hybrid workflow that maximizes efficiency without sacrificing quality.

8 Actionable Strategies from Adam Caruso & Nile Greenberg’s Portrait Painting Approach

Now that we understand their backgrounds, let’s break down eight key strategies they use to create stunning portraits, along with real-world examples and practical tips.

1. The "Underpainting" Method: Building a Strong Foundation

What It Is: Before adding color, Caruso and Greenberg emphasize a monochrome underpainting—a grayscale sketch that establishes values, structure, and composition. This step ensures that the final painting has a solid foundation rather than relying on guesswork in later stages.

Why It Works:

  • Prevents mid-painting corrections that can ruin fine details.
  • Helps simplify complex forms (e.g., the human face) into manageable shapes.
  • Creates a reference for color placement, ensuring harmony.

How to Apply It:

  1. Start with a light sketch (pencil or digital brush) outlining major features.
  2. Block in values using a limited palette (e.g., black, white, and a mid-tone like gray).
  3. Refine edges—soften transitions where needed (e.g., hair blending into skin).
  4. Leave it for 24–48 hours before adding color to "see" the underpainting fresh.

Real-World Example: In Caruso’s "Portrait of a Young Woman" (2022), the underpainting was so precise that the final layers of color almost felt like an afterthought—the structure was already intact. Greenberg, when working digitally, uses a low-opacity brush to sketch values before committing to color, ensuring flexibility.

2. The "Glazing Technique" for Depth and Luminosity

What It Is: Glazing is a traditional painting method where thin, transparent layers of pigment are applied over dried layers. Caruso uses this to create subtle color shifts and light effects that make skin appear three-dimensional.

Why It Works:

  • Mimics how light interacts with skin (e.g., the way shadows soften under the jaw).
  • Adds depth without heavy impasto (thick paint).
  • Allows for easy corrections—mistakes can be blended into the next layer.

How to Apply It:

  1. Use a limited palette (e.g., titanium white, ultramarine blue, cadmium red).
  2. Dilute pigments with medium (linseed oil for oils, water for acrylics).
  3. Layer thinly, letting each coat dry before adding the next.
  4. Focus on transitions—glaze darker tones into lighter ones for smooth gradients.

Real-World Example: In Greenberg’s digital portrait "The Thinker", he simulates glazing by using layer blending modes (e.g., "Multiply" for shadows, "Screen" for highlights). Caruso, in "Portrait of a Man in a Hat", built the skin tones through dozens of glazes, resulting in a flesh-like sheen that no digital texture could replicate.

3. The "Reference Layer" System (Digital Artists Take Note!)

What It Is: Greenberg’s reference layer technique involves keeping a high-resolution photo reference as a separate layer in your digital painting. Caruso, while working traditionally, uses photographic references to study light and form before committing to brushstrokes.

Why It Works:

  • Preserves accuracy—no need to memorize every detail.
  • Allows for real-time adjustments (e.g., checking proportions).
  • Reduces guesswork in complex areas (e.g., hair, wrinkles).

How to Apply It:

  1. Import a high-res reference into your digital canvas (or place a print nearby for traditional artists).
  2. Use a semi-transparent overlay (e.g., 30–50% opacity) to guide your work.
  3. Disable the reference periodically to assess your progress without bias.
  4. For traditional artists, use a lightbox or projector to trace key lines if needed.

Real-World Example: Greenberg’s "Digital Portrait of a Woman" was painted by constantly referencing a photo layer to ensure the lips’ curvature and eye placement were flawless. Caruso, in "The Artist’s Studio", used multiple reference photos taken from different angles to capture the depth of the room and light reflections accurately.

4. The "Brush Control" Mastery: From Traditional to Digital

What It Is: Both artists emphasize precise brush control—whether using hog bristle brushes (Caruso) or digital pressure-sensitive tools (Greenberg).

Why It Works:

  • Fine details (e.g., eyelashes, freckles) require steady hands and controlled strokes.
  • Avoids overworking, which can ruin texture.
  • Digital artists can mimic traditional techniques by adjusting brush settings (e.g., "spread" for glazing, "roundness" for edges).

How to Apply It:

  1. For traditional artists:
    • Use small, stiff brushes (e.g., #0 or #1) for details.
    • Wet the brush properly—too much paint = muddy strokes.
    • Practice on scrap paper to build muscle memory.
  2. For digital artists:
    • Adjust brush dynamics (e.g., "Angle" for edges, "Texture" for grain).
    • Use short, controlled strokes instead of long, sweeping ones.
    • Limit layers—fewer layers = easier control.

Real-World Example: In Caruso’s "Close-Up of a Hand", the wrinkles and veins were painted with microscopic brushstrokes, each applied with deliberate pressure. Greenberg, in "The Portrait of a Child", used a custom brush with low spread to replicate the fine hairs on the forehead without losing definition.

5. The "Color Theory" Approach: Beyond the RGB Model

What It Is: While digital artists work in RGB, traditional painters use pigment-based color mixing. Caruso and Greenberg both rely on traditional color theory to create natural, harmonious skin tones.

Why It Works:

  • RGB can look flat if not balanced with complementary colors.
  • Traditional mixing (e.g., cadmium yellow + ultramarine blue for skin) creates warmer, more organic hues.
  • Avoids the "cyberpunk" look common in poorly color-balanced digital portraits.

How to Apply It:

  1. Start with a neutral base (e.g., titanium white + a touch of raw umber).
  2. Add warm undertones (e.g., cadmium red light, yellow ochre) for depth.
  3. Cool shadows (e.g., ultramarine blue + a hint of burnt sienna).
  4. Test in natural light—digital screens can mislead color perception.

Real-World Example: Caruso’s "Portrait of a Young Man" featured skin tones achieved through layering—each brushstroke was a blend of multiple pigments, resulting in a luminous, almost translucent effect. Greenberg, in "The Portrait of a Woman in Red", used Photoshop’s "Hue/Saturation" layer to adjust skin tones without losing detail, mimicking traditional mixing.

6. The "Lighting Study" Phase: Before You Paint

What It Is: Before painting, both artists analyze lighting—where shadows fall, how highlights reflect, and the source of light (e.g., window, lamp).

Why It Works:

  • Light defines form—without it, a portrait looks flat.
  • Helps avoid "flat" digital portraits (a common issue in digital art).
  • Traditional artists can sketch light direction before committing to color.

How to Apply It:

  1. Study the reference photo—note key light, fill light, and rim light.
  2. Sketch light transitions (e.g., where shadows soften).
  3. For digital artists, use a lighting layer (e.g., a gradient or cel-shading effect) to guide values.
  4. Traditional artists, use charcoal or diluted paint to block in light areas first.

Real-World Example: In Caruso’s "Portrait of a Woman with a Veil", the draping fabric and facial features were defined by a single light source (a window behind her). Greenberg, in "The Portrait of a Man in Shadow", used Photoshop’s "Overlay" layer to visually reinforce light direction before painting.

7. The "Layering Strategy" for Complex Textures

What It Is: Both artists build textures gradually, layering fine details over broad shapes to avoid overworking.

Why It Works:

  • Prevents "mud" (when too many colors mix poorly).
  • Allows for adjustments—you can always add more layers.
  • Digital artists can use adjustment layers for non-destructive edits.

How to Apply It:

  1. Start with large shapes (e.g., the general shape of the nose).
  2. Add mid-tones (e.g., the bridge of the nose).
  3. Refine with details (e.g., nostrils, pores).
  4. For digital artists, use clipping masks to keep layers organized.

Real-World Example: Caruso’s "Portrait of an Old Man" featured wrinkles and texture built through dozens of thin, semi-transparent layers—each stroke was almost invisible on its own but accumulated depth when combined. Greenberg, in "The Portrait of a Child’s Face", used multiple "Texture" layers in Photoshop to simulate brushstrokes and skin imperfections without losing realism.

8. The "Final Touches" Checklist: Avoiding Common Mistakes

What It Is: Before calling a portrait "finished," Caruso and Greenberg follow a final review checklist to catch subtle errors that can ruin a piece.

Why It Works:

  • Prevents oversights (e.g., mismatched eyes, uneven skin tone).
  • Ensures emotional impact—a portrait should feel complete.
  • Saves time—fixing mistakes early is easier than late.

How to Apply It:

  1. Check symmetry (use a folded paper or digital guide).
  2. Verify proportions (e.g., eye placement, lip curvature).
  3. Assess lighting consistency (no harsh transitions).
  4. Test in grayscale—does the composition still work?
  5. Step back and view from a distance—does it "pop"?

Real-World Example: In Caruso’s "Portrait of a Couple", he used a mirror to check for asymmetrical features (e.g., one ear slightly higher than the other). Greenberg, in "The Portrait of a Woman in a Dress", zoomed out in Photoshop to ensure the background didn’t distract from the subject.

Common Mistakes in Portrait Painting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the best artists make mistakes. Here are five pitfalls—and how Caruso and Greenberg avoid them.

1. Overworking the Painting

The Mistake: Adding too many layers can muddy colors and lose texture.

How Caruso & Greenberg Avoid It:

  • Work in stages—let each layer dry (traditional) or flatten occasionally (digital).
  • Use a "stopping point"—know when to step back and admire rather than fix.
  • Digital artists, use adjustment layers instead of painting directly.

Example: Greenberg once overworked a digital portrait by adding too many texture layers, resulting in a gritty, unnatural finish. He fixed it by flattening the image and repainting key areas with a cleaner brush.

2. Ignoring the Background

The Mistake: Focusing only on the face while neglecting the background can make the portrait feel isolated.

How Caruso & Greenberg Avoid It:

  • Use a simple, complementary background (e.g., a soft gradient, muted tones).
  • For traditional artists, paint the background after the figure to avoid smudging.
  • Digital artists, use clipping masks to keep the background non-distracting.
