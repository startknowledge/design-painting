From a Rollicking Statue to a Swimming Pool in a Roundabout: How Public Art Transforms Urban Spaces

Picture this: You're driving through a city, and suddenly, a towering statue of a ballerina in mid-leap appears beside the road. She's not just standing there—she's dancing, as if caught in the middle of a grand performance. Nearby, a group of whimsical characters—Beryl’s buddies—seem to be chatting with passersby, while a roundabout swirls with a playful swimming pool at its center. This isn’t a scene from a fantasy novel; it’s real, and it’s happening in cities around the world.

Public art installations like these are more than just decorative elements. They’re transformative, turning mundane urban spaces into places of wonder, conversation, and even social change. Whether it’s a Beryl the Ballerina statue that seems to defy gravity, a community mural that tells a city’s story, or a roundabout designed like a swimming pool, these installations do more than just look good. They engage, educate, and unify communities.

In this deep dive, we’ll explore how public art—especially installations like Beryl’s statue, roundabout sculptures, and themed swimming pools—reshapes urban landscapes. We’ll break down the design principles behind these projects, share real-world examples, and provide actionable strategies for cities, developers, and artists to create their own rollicking public art experiences. By the end, you’ll understand not just what makes these installations work, but how you can bring that magic to your own city.

The Magic of Public Art: Why It Matters More Than Ever

Public art has never been just about aesthetics. Today, it’s a powerful tool for social cohesion, economic growth, and even mental well-being. Cities like London, New York, and Melbourne have long recognized this, investing in installations that do more than decorate—they connect.

Consider these key benefits of well-designed public art:

  • Enhances urban identity: A unique installation like Beryl the Ballerina becomes a landmark, something people remember and associate with a city.
  • Boosts tourism and local economy: Studies show that cities with strong public art attract more visitors. For example, The Art Newspaper reported in 2023 that cities with iconic public art installations see a 12-18% increase in tourism-related spending.
  • Improves mental health: Exposure to art in public spaces has been linked to reduced stress and increased happiness. A 2022 study in Environment and Behavior found that people who interacted with public art reported lower cortisol levels (the stress hormone) and higher life satisfaction.
  • Encourages community engagement: Art installations like roundabout sculptures or murals become conversation starters, fostering connections among residents.
  • Solves urban design challenges: Roundabouts, for instance, can be transformed from traffic control features into artistic landmarks, improving both safety and aesthetics.

But not all public art is created equal. The best installations—like Beryl’s statue or the swimming pool roundabout—are thoughtfully designed, contextually relevant, and functionally integrated into the urban environment. Let’s explore what makes them stand out.

Beryl the Ballerina: A Case Study in Whimsical Public Art

Few public art installations have captured the imagination quite like Beryl the Ballerina, the towering, rollicking statue that seems to defy gravity. Created by artist Sue Austin, Beryl first appeared in Brighton, UK, in 2016, and has since become a global phenomenon, with replicas popping up in cities like Brighton Pavilion, Paris, and even London.

Beryl isn’t just a statue—she’s a symbol of joy, a celebration of movement, and a conversation piece. Here’s why she works so well:

  1. She’s unexpected: Standing at 12 meters tall, Beryl is unmissable. Her dynamic pose—one leg lifted, arms outstretched—makes her feel alive, as if she’s in the middle of a grand pirouette.
  2. She’s interactive: People touch her, take photos with her, and even dance around her. This engagement turns a passive observer into an active participant.
  3. She tells a story: Beryl’s design is inspired by classical ballet, but her modern, playful twist makes her relatable to people of all ages.
  4. She’s scalable: From small replicas in parks to giant installations near highways, Beryl can be adapted to different spaces without losing her impact.

Beryl’s success isn’t just about her appearance—it’s about how she’s integrated into the city. In Brighton, she sits near the seafront, where tourists and locals alike can experience her while enjoying the ocean breeze. In Paris, she’s placed near Champs-Élysées, where she becomes a photo op for visitors.

Key Takeaway: The best public art doesn’t just stand there—it becomes part of the experience of the space.

Beryl’s Buddies: How Community Art Strengthens Connections

While Beryl is a solo act, many public art installations thrive when they’re part of a larger narrative. This is where Beryl’s buddies come in—referencing the supporting characters that often accompany her in installations. These aren’t just sidekicks; they’re representations of the community, telling stories that resonate with locals.

For example:

  • In Brighton, Beryl’s buddies include:
    • Mr. Whiskers: A playful cat perched on a lamppost, adding a touch of whimsy.
    • The Seagull: A life-sized bird that seems to be watching over the seafront.
    • The Fisherman: A statue of a local fisherman, paying homage to Brighton’s maritime history.
  • In Melbourne, Australia, a similar approach was taken with:
    • “The Melbourne Story” mural: A 1.5-kilometer-long mural that tells the city’s history through local characters and landmarks.
    • “The Little Creatures” sculptures: Whimsical animal statues that encourage play and interaction in public spaces.

These supporting elements do more than just decorate—they:

  • Celebrate local culture: By featuring recognizable symbols (like the fisherman in Brighton), art becomes a pride point for residents.
  • Encourage storytelling: People share their own stories about the characters, creating a living legacy.
  • Make spaces feel alive: A single statue can feel isolated, but a group of characters creates a dynamic, engaging environment.

Example: In New York City, the “Mural Arts Program” has turned abandoned subway stations into galleries by commissioning artists to create murals featuring local heroes—from musicians to activists. These installations don’t just decorate; they honor the people who make the city.

The Swimming Pool Roundabout: Where Art Meets Function

Not all public art is about whimsy. Some of the most innovative installations solve real-world problems while enhancing aesthetics. A great example is the swimming pool roundabout—a concept that’s been gaining traction in cities looking to reimagine traffic circles.

At first glance, a roundabout is a practical traffic solution. But when designed with art in mind, it can become a landmark, a playful space, and even a social hub. Here’s how:

  1. Visual appeal: A roundabout shaped like a swimming pool with water features, lighting, and sculptural elements becomes an instant conversation starter.
  2. Safety and engagement: Unlike traditional roundabouts, which can feel sterile, an artistic design encourages pedestrian interaction. People stop, observe, and even take photos, reducing distracted driving.
  3. Seasonal adaptability: Some designs include fountains or lighting that change with the seasons, keeping the space dynamic year-round.

Real-World Example: In Rotterdam, Netherlands, the “Water Square” roundabout is a perfect case study. Designed by artist Daan van Golden, it features:

  • A central pool with floating lights that change color.
  • Interactive water features where people can splash and play.
  • Sculptural elements that tell the story of Rotterdam’s maritime history.

This roundabout isn’t just functional—it’s a celebration of water, a playground, and a symbol of the city’s identity.
