Chapter 1: The Philosophy Behind "A Life by Design"

What Does "Living by Design" Really Mean?

Terrance Osborne doesn’t just talk about making art—he talks about designing existence. For him, "living by design" is the deliberate curation of experiences, habits, and relationships that reflect your highest self. It’s not about rigid control; it’s about creating space for creativity, growth, and connection while avoiding the pitfalls of distraction and burnout.

Osborne draws inspiration from multiple disciplines:

  • Artistic process (how discipline shapes creativity)
  • Design thinking (solving life’s problems like a designer)
  • Philosophy (Stoicism, existentialism, and the search for meaning)
  • Neuroscience (how habits and environment shape behavior)

His approach is practical yet profound, blending the structured rigor of an artist’s studio with the fluidity of a life well-lived.

The Three Pillars of Osborne’s Philosophy

Osborne identifies three foundational pillars that must align for a truly designed life:

  1. Intentionality – Knowing what you want and why you want it.

    • Without clarity, even the most structured life becomes a series of reactive choices.
    • Example: An artist who paints because they must (external pressure) vs. one who paints because they choose (internal drive).

  2. Balance – The harmony between structure and spontaneity.

    • Too much rigidity leads to stagnation; too much chaos leads to exhaustion.
    • Osborne’s solution? Rhythms—repeating patterns that create stability without suffocation.

  3. Becoming – The ongoing evolution of self through art, work, and relationships.

    • Life isn’t static; neither should your design be.
    • This pillar encourages self-reflection, adaptation, and the courage to reinvent.

Chapter 2: Eight Actionable Strategies to Design Your Life

Now that we understand the philosophy, let’s dive into practical, step-by-step strategies to apply Osborne’s principles in your own life.

Strategy 1: The "Why" Audit – Reconnecting with Your Core Purpose

Problem: Many people go through life on autopilot, chasing goals without truly understanding why they matter.

Osborne’s Solution: Conduct a "Why" Audit—a deep dive into what truly motivates you.

How to Do It:

  1. List your top 5 values (e.g., creativity, family, freedom, mastery).
  2. Rank them by importance—which three are non-negotiable?
  3. Audit your current life: Are your daily actions aligned with these values?
    • If not, what’s getting in the way?

Real-World Example: A freelance graphic designer once told Osborne she was "burning out" because she spent 60 hours a week on client work but felt unfulfilled. Her "Why" Audit revealed that creativity and personal growth were her top values—but her schedule left no room for them. By blocking two non-negotiable "creative hours" weekly (even if unpaid), she regained passion for her work.

Common Mistake: Assuming you know your purpose without ever questioning it. Fix: Revisit your "Why" every six months—life changes, and so should your priorities.

Strategy 2: The Rhythm Method – Structuring Freedom, Not Just Time

Problem: Most people think "balance" means rigid schedules. Osborne argues it’s about rhythms—repeating patterns that create predictability without constraint.

Osborne’s Solution: Design three key rhythms in your life:

  1. Daily (habits that anchor your day)
  2. Weekly (patterns that prevent burnout)
  3. Monthly/Quarterly (long-term reflection and adaptation)

How to Implement:

  • Daily Rhythm: Example:
    • Morning (60 min): Creative work (painting, writing, brainstorming).
    • Midday (90 min): Deep focus on a high-priority task.
    • Evening (30 min): Movement (walking, stretching) + digital detox.
  • Weekly Rhythm: Example:
    • Monday: Admin tasks (emails, planning).
    • Tuesday-Thursday: Creative work + client projects.
    • Friday: "Play day" (no work—just exploration).
  • Monthly Rhythm: Example:
    • First Sunday: Review progress, adjust goals.
    • Last Friday: "Reset day" (no meetings, just reflection).

Real-World Example: A struggling musician was drowning in gigs but felt creatively empty. Osborne helped her implement a "Weekly Rhythm" where:

  • Tuesdays and Thursdays were for composing new music.
  • Saturdays were for performing (but only if she felt inspired).
  • Sundays were for complete rest—no music, no planning.

Within three months, she doubled her creative output because she was no longer reacting to demand but designing her own flow.

Common Mistake: Treating rhythms like chains instead of frameworks. Fix: Allow flexibility within structure. If a rhythm isn’t working, tweak it—not abandon it.

Strategy 3: The "No" Framework – Protecting Your Creative Energy

Problem: Saying "yes" to everything (even good things) drains your energy and dilutes your focus.

Osborne’s Solution: Adopt a "No" Framework—a set of non-negotiable boundaries to protect your time and mental space.

How to Apply:

  1. Identify your "Energy Zones" (e.g., deep work, socializing, creative play).
  2. Set hard limits on what drains these zones.
    • Example: "I will not take on more than two client projects at a time."
    • "I will not check emails after 7 PM."
  3. Automate or delegate tasks that don’t align with your top priorities.

Real-World Example: A painter was overwhelmed by gallery requests, social media demands, and side hustles. Osborne helped her implement:

  • "No" to unsolicited gallery shows unless they aligned with her vision.
  • "No" to social media except for one scheduled post per week.
  • "No" to late-night work—her best ideas came in the morning.

The result? More time for painting, less stress, and higher-quality work.

Common Mistake: Feeling guilty for saying "no." Fix: Remember: Saying "no" is a form of self-respect. It’s not selfish—it’s strategic.

Strategy 4: The "Third Space" – Creating Sacred Time for Creativity

Problem: Many artists and creatives work in their studios, homes, or cafes—but none of these spaces feel sacred.

Osborne’s Solution: Design a "Third Space"—a place that’s neither work nor home, where creativity flows freely.

How to Create One:

  • Physical Space: A small studio, a park bench, a co-working space—anywhere that feels neutral and inspiring.
  • Mental Space: A ritual to transition into this space (e.g., lighting a candle, playing a specific song).
  • Time Block: Dedicate at least 90 minutes weekly to this space, uninterrupted.

Real-World Example: A writer felt stuck because she only wrote at home, where distractions (dishes, kids, noise) constantly interrupted her. Osborne suggested she:

  • Turned a local library into her "Third Space."
  • Set a ritual: Arrive at 7 AM, order coffee, and write for two hours straight.
  • Protected this time like a sacred appointment.

Within a month, she completed her first novel draft—something she’d been avoiding for years.

Common Mistake: Assuming creativity happens only in a "perfect" environment. Fix: Any space can be sacred if you treat it that way. The key is intention, not perfection.

Strategy 5: The "Feedback Loop" – Using Art to Understand Yourself

Problem: Many people live without self-awareness, making decisions based on habit rather than insight.

Osborne’s Solution: Use art as a feedback mechanism—create something, then analyze it to uncover deeper truths about yourself.

How to Do It:

  1. Make something (a painting, a song, a written piece) without overthinking.
  2. Ask yourself:
    • What emotions did this evoke?
    • What themes kept reappearing?
    • What did I resist creating?
  3. Use these insights to adjust your life.

Real-World Example: A therapist was feeling unfulfilled in her work but couldn’t pinpoint why. Osborne suggested she:

  • Paint abstractly for a week, letting her hand guide her.
  • Analyzed the results: She noticed she kept painting dark, swirling colors—symbolizing her inner turmoil.
  • Realized: She needed to integrate more joy into her practice (not just healing).

She then started a "joy practice"—writing gratitude lists and taking dance classes—and saw a 30% improvement in her well-being.

Common Mistake: Treating art as just an output, not a tool for self-discovery. Fix: Engage with your work deeply. What’s it really saying about you?

Strategy 6: The "Slow Start" – Mastering the Art of Beginning

Problem: Many people procrastinate because they overthink the start of a project.

Osborne’s Solution: Adopt a "Slow Start" method—begin with tiny, low-stakes actions to build momentum.

How to Apply:

  1. Break projects into "micro-starts."
    • Example: Instead of "I’ll write a novel," start with "I’ll write one sentence."
  2. Use the "5-Minute Rule." Commit to just five minutes of work—often, you’ll keep going.
  3. Remove friction. Have your materials ready before you begin.

Real-World Example: A musician was paralyzed by the idea of composing a new song. Osborne advised:

  • "Just play one chord."
  • "Sing one line."
  • "Write one lyric."

She did—and within 20 minutes, she had a full draft. Momentum replaced paralysis.

Common Mistake: Waiting for "perfect conditions" to begin. Fix: Start imperfectly. Progress > perfection.

Strategy 7: The "Boundary Guard" – Protecting Your Creative Time

Problem: Even with the best intentions, life interrupts creativity.

Osborne’s Solution: Assign a "Boundary Guard"—a person or system that enforces your creative time.

How to Set One Up:

  • For solo creators: Use automated tools (e.g., Freedom app to block distractions, Calendly for scheduling).
  • For teams: Designate a "Creative Champion" who holds others accountable.
  • For personal life: Tell loved ones, "This is my sacred time—no interruptions."

Real-World Example: A digital artist was constantly interrupted by family and clients. Osborne helped her:

  • Set up a "Do Not Disturb" sign on her studio door.
  • Scheduled all creative work for 9–11 AM, when her kids were at school.
  • Used a voice recorder to dictate ideas if interrupted.

Her productivity doubled because she finally had unbroken creative blocks.

Common Mistake: Assuming others will respect your boundaries naturally. Fix: Communicate clearly. Boundaries are only as strong as the people who uphold them.

Strategy 8: The "Quarter-Life Review" – Designing for Long-Term Fulfillment

Problem: Many people live in the moment without considering the long-term impact of their choices.

Osborne’s Solution: Conduct a quarterly "Life Review"—a structured check-in to assess progress and adjust course.

How to Do It:

  1. Review your rhythms: Are they still serving you?
  2. Assess your energy: Are you burning out or thriving?
  3. Adjust goals: What needs to change?
  4. Celebrate wins: Acknowledge progress, no matter how small.

Real-World Example: A photographer was stuck in a creative rut. Every quarter, she:

  • Asked: "What’s draining me?" (Answer: Too many client shoots.)
  • Adjusted: Reduced client work by 30% and started a personal project.
  • Result: Her art became more meaningful, and her stress levels dropped.

Common Mistake: Waiting for annual reviews instead of checking in often. Fix: Small, frequent adjustments > one big annual overhaul.
