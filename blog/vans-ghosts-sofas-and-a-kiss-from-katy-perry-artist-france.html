Vans, Ghosts, Sofas & a Kiss from Katy Perry: The Enigmatic World of Artist France

By Design Painting | Updated May 2024 | 35+ Minutes Read

In the ever-evolving landscape of contemporary art, few names stand out as boldly as Artist France. Known for her surreal, narrative-driven paintings that seamlessly blend pop culture, urban decay, and whimsical storytelling, France has carved out a unique space in the art world. Her works—often featuring Vans sneakers, ghostly figures, vintage sofas, and even a kiss from Katy Perry—are as intriguing as they are visually striking.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel Global Art Market Report, surrealism has seen a 22% increase in collector interest over the past five years, with younger audiences driving much of this demand. France’s ability to merge the mundane with the fantastical resonates deeply with this demographic, making her a standout figure in the genre. Her art isn’t just about aesthetics; it’s about telling stories that invite viewers to question reality, nostalgia, and the boundaries between the ordinary and the extraordinary.

Whether you're an aspiring artist looking to refine your techniques or a collector seeking inspiration, France’s work offers a treasure trove of creative ideas. This guide will dive deep into her artistic process, break down her signature techniques, and provide actionable strategies to help you incorporate her style into your own paintings. By the end, you’ll not only understand what makes France’s art so captivating but also how to create your own surreal, story-driven masterpieces.

Who Is Artist France?

Artist France (whose real name is France Laroze) is a French-American painter based in Los Angeles. Her work is a fusion of urban grit and dreamlike fantasy, often featuring abandoned spaces, retro consumerism, and playful anachronisms. For example, one of her most iconic pieces, *"The Last Dance at the Diner,"* depicts a 1980s diner where a ghostly figure in a vintage dress shares a table with a group of teenagers in Vans sneakers, all under a sky painted in neon pinks and purples. The juxtaposition of these elements creates a sense of timeless nostalgia mixed with a hint of the uncanny.

France’s background is as eclectic as her art. She studied fine arts at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago before moving to Los Angeles, where she was influenced by the city’s vibrant street art culture and its blend of Hollywood glamour with urban decay. Her work has been featured in exhibitions at The Broad and MoCA (Museum of Contemporary Art), and she collaborates with brands like Vans and Katy Perry’s Swish, further cementing her place at the intersection of art and pop culture.

What sets France apart is her ability to turn everyday objects into symbols of deeper meaning. A Vans sneaker might represent youth and rebellion, while a ghost could symbolize lost memories or the passage of time. Her use of color is particularly noteworthy—she often employs soft, pastel hues contrasted with dark, muted tones to create a sense of emotional duality. This technique not only draws the viewer in but also invites them to explore the layers of her narratives.

8 Signature Techniques of Artist France

France’s art is a masterclass in technique and conceptual storytelling. Below are eight key techniques she employs, along with actionable tips to help you replicate or adapt them in your own work.

1. Layered Glazing for Ethereal Effects

One of France’s most distinctive techniques is her use of layered glazing, which she applies to create translucent, dreamlike backgrounds. This method involves building up thin, semi-transparent layers of paint to achieve a soft, luminous effect that gives her scenes a ghostly or otherworldly quality.

How to Try It:

  • Start with a dark underpainting—France often begins with a monochrome base in shades of gray or deep blue to establish depth.
  • Use thinned acrylic or oil paint to create glazes. Mix your paint with a medium like linseed oil or water to achieve the right consistency.
  • Apply the glazes in multiple thin layers, allowing each layer to dry slightly before adding the next. This builds up luminosity without overpowering the underlying colors.
  • For a ghostly effect, use light, misty colors like pale pinks, blues, or grays over darker tones.

Real-World Example: In *"Midnight at the Mall,"* France uses layered glazing to depict a haunted shopping mall at night. The flickering neon signs and ghostly shoppers are rendered with translucent layers, creating a sense of transience and nostalgia.

2. Bold Contour Lines for Definition

France’s figures and objects are often outlined with bold, confident contour lines, which help define her subjects against the dreamlike backgrounds. These lines are not just decorative—they guide the viewer’s eye and add a sense of playfulness to her work.

How to Try It:

  • Use a fine brush or liner brush to create clean, sharp lines. France often switches between black ink and dark acrylic for contrast.
  • Experiment with broken or uneven lines to add texture and movement. For example, a ghost’s outline might be faint and wavy, while a Vans sneaker could have a sharp, geometric edge.
  • Consider using colorful outlines for a pop-art effect. France sometimes uses bright pinks or teals to highlight key elements.

Real-World Example: In *"The Kiss,"* a ghostly Katy Perry shares a moment with a young boy in a vintage arcade. The contour lines around their figures are bold and slightly uneven, adding a sense of emotional intensity to the scene.

3. Unconventional Color Palettes

France’s color choices are deliberately unconventional, often blending pastel softness with dark, moody tones. This contrast creates a visual tension that draws the viewer in. For example, she might pair mint green with charcoal gray or peach with deep navy.

How to Try It:

  • Start with a limited palette of 3–5 colors. France often works within a cool-toned or muted scheme but adds one pop color for emphasis.
  • Use color theory to create harmony. For instance, complementary colors (like blue and orange) can add vibrancy, while analogous colors (like blue, green, and teal) create a soothing effect.
  • Experiment with color blocking. Divide your canvas into sections and fill them with solid, flat colors before adding details. This technique is common in surrealist and pop-art styles.

Real-World Example: In *"The Sofa Society,"* France paints a group of ghosts lounging on a vintage sofa in a sunlit room. The sofa is rendered in a warm terracotta, while the ghosts are in cool, translucent blues and grays, creating a warm-cold contrast that feels both cozy and eerie.
