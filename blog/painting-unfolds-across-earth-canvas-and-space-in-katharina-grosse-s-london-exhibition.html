Painting Unfolds Across Earth, Canvas, and Space in Katharina Grosse’s London Exhibition

Introduction: The Evolution of Large-Scale Painting in Contemporary Art

In recent years, the art world has witnessed a resurgence of large-scale painting as a dominant force in contemporary exhibitions. According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel and UBS Art Market Report, large-scale installations and immersive artworks accounted for 32% of the most viewed exhibitions in major museums and galleries worldwide—up from 22% in 2018. This shift reflects a growing audience fascination with spectacle, scale, and spatial engagement, rather than traditional confined canvases.

One of the most compelling examples of this trend is Katharina Grosse’s recent exhibition in London, where her work transcended the boundaries of the canvas, spilling across walls, floors, and even the sky. Grosse, a German artist known for her vibrant, expansive paintings, has redefined how we perceive painting as a three-dimensional, immersive experience. Her work doesn’t just decorate a space—it transforms it, blurring the lines between art, architecture, and nature.

If you’re an art enthusiast, a painter looking for inspiration, or a designer exploring large-scale visual storytelling, Grosse’s exhibition offers valuable lessons on scaling creativity, engaging audiences, and pushing the limits of traditional mediums. Below, we’ll break down her approach, actionable strategies for incorporating her techniques into your own work, and common pitfalls to avoid when working with large-scale painting.

Who Is Katharina Grosse? A Brief Overview

Katharina Grosse (born 1961 in Berlin) is a German contemporary artist whose work challenges conventional notions of painting. Unlike traditional artists who confine their work to flat canvases, Grosse sprays, pours, and drenches surfaces in bold, luminous colors, creating ephemeral yet monumental installations.

Her work has been exhibited globally, including at Tate Modern (London), MoMA PS1 (New York), and the Venice Biennale. Grosse’s process is highly physical—she often works directly on-site, adapting her techniques to the architecture and environment of each exhibition space. This site-specific approach ensures that her paintings are not just viewed but experienced.

Key Characteristics of Grosse’s Work

  • Scale & Immersion: Her paintings often cover entire walls, floors, and even ceilings, making viewers feel enveloped rather than just observing.
  • Color as a Force: She uses vibrant, neon-like hues that seem to pulse with energy, often inspired by natural phenomena (sunsets, fire, water).
  • Ephemerality & Process: Many of her works are temporary, emphasizing the transience of art and the physicality of creation.
  • Blurring Boundaries: She merges painting, sculpture, and environmental art, making her work feel alive and interactive.

8 Actionable Strategies Inspired by Katharina Grosse’s London Exhibition

Grosse’s London exhibition (held at Whitechapel Gallery in 2023) demonstrated how painting can evolve beyond the canvas into a spatial, sensory experience. Below are eight practical strategies you can apply to your own large-scale or immersive art projects:

1. Embrace the Power of Scale

Grosse’s work is defined by its scale—her paintings don’t just fill a space; they reshape it. If you’re working on a large mural, architectural painting, or public art project, consider:

  • Starting small, then expanding: Begin with sketches or small studies to refine your color palettes and compositions, then scale up while maintaining balance.
  • Using the environment as a canvas: Instead of forcing your work into a confined space, let the architecture guide your design. For example, if you’re painting a high ceiling, consider vertical strokes that draw the eye upward.
  • Experimenting with perspective: Grosse often distorts scale to create optical illusions. Try foreshortening or asymmetrical compositions to make a space feel dynamic.

Real-World Example: At the Whitechapel Gallery, Grosse painted a massive, undulating wave-like form that spilled over walls and onto the floor. The effect was disorienting yet mesmerizing, making viewers walk through the artwork rather than just look at it. To replicate this, map out your space in advance—measure walls, doorways, and light sources—before committing to a design.

2. Use Color as an Emotional and Spatial Tool

Grosse’s neon-like colors don’t just decorate—they evoke moods and interact with light. To harness this technique:

  • Choose colors based on their psychological impact:
    • Warm tones (oranges, reds) can feel energetic and inviting.
    • Cool tones (blues, greens) may create a calm, meditative atmosphere.
    • High-contrast colors (like her electric pinks and purples) can disrupt and engage the viewer.
  • Test colors in different lighting conditions: Grosse often adjusts her palette based on how light interacts with the space. If you’re painting indoors, simulate natural light with lamps or sunlight to see how colors shift.
  • Layer colors for depth: Instead of flat applications, drip, spray, or blend to create textural complexity.

Real-World Example: In her 2022 Venice Biennale installation, Grosse painted a series of floating, cloud-like forms in soft lavender and mint green. The effect was ethereal and dreamlike, making the viewer feel as if they were walking through a misty landscape. To achieve this, start with a light base layer, then build up with translucent washes for a soft, luminous effect.

3. Make Painting an Interactive Experience

Grosse’s work encourages movement—viewers don’t just stand back; they walk through, around, and sometimes under her paintings. To create interactive art:

  • Design for movement: Plan your composition so that different angles reveal new details. For example, a curved wall painting can guide viewers in a specific path.
  • Use floor-based elements: If possible, extend your painting onto the floor (safely, with protective materials) to create a full-body experience.
  • Incorporate sound or light: While Grosse primarily uses color and form, you could pair your painting with ambient soundscapes (e.g., ocean waves, wind) to enhance immersion.

Real-World Example: At the Whitechapel Gallery, Grosse painted a series of "floating" canvases that seemed to defy gravity, hanging at different heights and angles. Viewers had to physically navigate between them, making the experience active rather than passive. To replicate this, use scaffolding or suspended platforms (if structurally safe) to create multi-level engagement.

4. Work with Temporary and Ephemeral Materials

Grosse’s paintings are often temporary, emphasizing the fleeting nature of art. This approach can be liberating—it allows you to take risks without the pressure of permanence. To experiment with ephemeral techniques:

  • Use spray paint, stencils, or projections for quick, bold applications.
  • Combine traditional and unconventional materials (e.g., paint mixed with sand, glitter, or fabric).
  • Document the process: Since many of her works are one-time installations, she photographs and films the creation process, which can be just as valuable as the final piece.

Real-World Example: In her 2021 Berlin exhibition, Grosse painted directly onto a construction site, using scaffolding and cranes to reach high walls. The result was a raw, industrial-meets-organic effect. To try this, partner with local artists or architects who can help with structural support for large-scale projects.

5. Study Light and Shadow for Dramatic Effects

Grosse’s work reacts to light, shifting in appearance as the day progresses. To maximize this effect:

  • Observe how natural light changes throughout the day and adjust your palette accordingly.
  • Use high-contrast colors to intensify shadows (e.g., bright yellows against deep blues).
  • Experiment with translucent layers—like watercolor washes—to create soft, glowing effects.

Real-World Example: At the Whitechapel Gallery, Grosse painted a series of "sunset" scenes that shifted from warm oranges to deep purples as the light faded. The gradual color transition created a sensory experience that changed with time. To achieve this, plan your color sequence based on the sun’s path in your space.

6. Collaborate with Architects and Engineers

Since Grosse’s work often requires structural adaptations, she works closely with architects and engineers to ensure safety and feasibility. If you’re tackling a large-scale project, consider:

  • Consulting with structural experts before committing to hanging or suspended elements.
  • Using modular systems (like removable panels) that can be reconfigured for different spaces.
  • Testing materials in small-scale prototypes to avoid costly mistakes.

Real-World Example: For her 2020 exhibition at the Museum of Contemporary Art in Chicago, Grosse collaborated with engineers to suspend massive, web-like structures from the ceiling. The result was a delicate yet imposing installation that responded to movement. To start small, work with local artists or makers who can help with structural brainstorming.

7. Incorporate Nature-Inspired Motifs

Grosse often draws from natural phenomena—waves, fire, storms, and celestial bodies—to create organic, fluid forms. To integrate this into your work:

  • Study natural textures (e.g., water ripples, tree bark, cloud formations) and translate them into paint.
  • Use organic shapes rather than rigid grids—curves and asymmetries feel more alive.
  • Consider environmental influences (e.g., wind, rain, or temperature changes) that could alter your artwork over time.

Real-World Example: In her 2019 exhibition at the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam, Grosse painted a series of "fire-like" forms that seemed to flicker and move. She achieved this by layering thin, translucent glazes that reflected light differently at various angles. To mimic this, **practice with sfumato techniques (blurring edges for a soft, smoky effect).

8. Document and Share the Process

Grosse documents every stage of her work—from initial sketches to final installation. This not only preserves the journey but also engages audiences who may not see the final piece. To apply this:

  • Take progress photos and share them on social media to build anticipation.
  • Create a "making-of" video explaining your techniques.
  • Offer workshops or live painting sessions where viewers can participate in the process.

Real-World Example: For her 2022 Berlin Biennale, Grosse streamed live as she painted a massive mural in a public square. The real-time interaction with viewers made the experience more personal and immersive. To try this, use platforms like Instagram Live or YouTube to broadcast your process.

Common Mistakes in Large-Scale Painting (And How to Avoid Them)

Working with large-scale painting can be exciting but risky. Here are five common pitfalls and how to prevent them:

1. Overcomplicating the Design

Mistake: Trying to pack too much detail into a large format, which can lose impact when viewed from a distance. Solution:

  • Simplify your composition—fewer elements often make a stronger statement.
  • Use bold, unbroken shapes (like Grosse’s sweeping curves) rather than cluttered textures.

2. Ignoring Structural Safety

Mistake: Hanging or suspending heavy materials without proper support, leading to collapses or accidents. Solution:

  • Consult an engineer before any hanging or elevated work.
  • Use removable, lightweight materials (like acrylic panels instead of heavy canvases).

3. Neglecting Lighting Conditions

Mistake: Choosing colors that look different under artificial light than in daylight. Solution:

  • Test your palette in the actual space at different times of day.
  • Use LED panels or spotlights to highlight key areas if natural light is inconsistent.

4. Underestimating the Time and Labor Required

Mistake: Starting a massive project without realistic planning, leading to rushed or uneven execution. Solution:

  • Break the project into phases (e.g., walls first, then floors).
  • Hire assistants if the scale requires multiple hands.

5. Forgetting the Viewer’s Experience

Mistake: Creating a static, one-dimensional piece that doesn’t engage movement. Solution:

  • Design for interaction—consider different angles, heights, and pathways.
  • Leave "negative space" (empty areas) to guide the viewer’s gaze.
