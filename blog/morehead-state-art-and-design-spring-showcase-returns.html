8 Actionable Strategies to Maximize Your Experience at the Showcase

Attending the Morehead State Art & Design Spring Showcase is an opportunity to learn, network, and inspire. To get the most out of it, follow these strategies to engage deeply with the art, artists, and industry trends.

1. Research the Exhibits Before You Go

Why? Many artists provide previews of their work on the university’s website or social media. Knowing what to expect helps you focus on key pieces and ask the right questions.

How?

  • Check the official showcase website for artist bios, project descriptions, and special events.
  • Follow Morehead State’s art department on Instagram and LinkedIn for teasers and behind-the-scenes content.
  • Look for past catalogs or digital archives (if available) to recognize recurring themes or techniques.

Example: In 2021, a student named Alexandra Chen created "Fractured Narratives"—a series of digital collages exploring identity and memory. By researching her work beforehand, attendees could appreciate the deeper meaning behind her use of glitch art and fragmented imagery.

2. Engage with Artists Directly (But Know How)

Why? Artists love sharing their process—but many are nervous about criticism. The key is to ask thoughtful questions rather than just complimenting the work.

How?

  • Start with open-ended questions:
    • "What inspired you to use [specific material/technique]?"
    • "How did this project evolve from your initial idea?"
    • "What’s the biggest challenge you faced while creating this?"
  • Avoid:
    • "This is amazing!" (Too vague—artists want meaningful dialogue.)
    • "Can I have this?" (Unless it’s for sale, this can come across as rude or presumptuous.)
  • Bring a notebook to jot down techniques, artist names, and contact info for future reference.

Example: At the 2020 showcase, a graphic design student named Jamal Rodriguez presented "Neon Nocturnes"—a series of cyberpunk-inspired posters. When asked, "How did you balance bold colors with readability?" he explained his color psychology approach, which many attendees later applied to their own branding projects.

3. Take Notes on Techniques & Trends

Why? The showcase is a living classroom. Many students innovate with tools and methods that aren’t yet mainstream.

How?

  • Create a "trend tracker" in your notes:
    • Digital: Which software (Procreate, Photoshop, Blender) is being used most?
    • Materials: *Are artists experimenting with *recycled plastics, biodegradable inks, or 3D-printed elements?
    • Themes: What social or environmental issues are being addressed?
  • Compare past and present work—many students build on previous projects, so you might see evolution in their style.

Example: In 2019, a digital art student named Ethan Lee used AI-generated textures in his "Algorithmic Landscapes" series. By noting his workflow (using MidJourney for initial concepts, then refining in Photoshop), other artists could adapt his hybrid approach to their own work.

4. Attend Workshops & Networking Events

Why? The real value of the showcase isn’t just the art—it’s the people. Many professionals, alumni, and industry leaders attend to recruit talent or collaborate.

How?

  • Sign up for pre-show workshops (often free for attendees).
  • Bring business cards (even if you’re a student—networking starts early).
  • Join group discussions—many artists collaborate on future projects through these events.
  • Follow up after the event—send a polite LinkedIn message to artists whose work you admired.

Example: At the 2021 showcase, a local advertising agency hosted a "Design Thinking Workshop" where students pitched creative solutions to real business problems. One participant, Sophia Kim, later landed an internship with the agency based on her problem-solving skills demonstrated there.

5. Explore the "Hidden Gems" (Not Just the Biggest Pieces)

Why? The most talked-about installations often get overcrowded, but the smaller, more experimental works can be just as groundbreaking.

How?

  • Look for:
    • Student-curated sections (often in less visible areas).
    • Interactive or immersive pieces (even if they’re not the "main attraction").
    • Works that challenge conventions (e.g., abstract, conceptual, or politically charged art).
  • Ask curators for recommendations—they know what’s underrated but meaningful.

Example: In 2018, a lesser-known student artist, Mira Patel, displayed "Silent Protests"—a series of hand-sewn fabric art addressing social justice. While it didn’t get as much attention as the digital installations, it sparked meaningful conversations among attendees who later supported her crowdfunded solo exhibition.

6. Document Your Experience (But Respect the Artists’ Work)

Why? Sharing your thoughts and discoveries helps spread awareness—but always credit the creators.

How?

  • Take photos (if allowed)—but never post without permission.
  • Write a review or blog post (like this one!) citing specific artists and their techniques.
  • Share on social media with #MoreheadStateArtShowcase to tag the university and artists.
  • Avoid:
    • Stealing images for your portfolio (unless you have explicit permission).
    • Posting without context (always give credit to the artist).

Example: A design blogger named Lena Chen documented her experience at the 2020 showcase, highlighting five emerging artists and their unique approaches to typography. Her post drew attention to their work, leading to collaborations and job opportunities.

7. Participate in the Live Auction & Sales Event

Why? Many students sell their work at the showcase, and auctions benefit scholarships. Bidding (even for small amounts) supports the next generation of artists.

How?

  • Set a budget—even $20–$50 can make a difference.
  • Ask artists about their pricing—some may offer discounts for students or first-time buyers.
  • Consider purchasing a piece if:
    • You love the work and want it for your space.
    • You want to support a student artist directly.
    • You plan to resell it later (if it gains recognition).
  • Avoid:
    • Bidding on everything (stay within your budget).
    • Assuming all work is "investment-grade"—some pieces are experimental and not meant for long-term collection.

Example: In 2019, a digital illustration by Daniel Carter sold for $150 at auction. The artist later used the proceeds to fund a summer workshop in digital painting techniques, which helped other students refine their skills.

8. Reflect & Apply What You Learned

Why? The best way to grow as an artist or designer is to take action after inspiration strikes.

How?

  • Try a new technique from the showcase—even if it’s just a small experiment.
  • Reach out to artists whose work you admire—ask for advice or collaborations.
  • Document your process—start a sketchbook, blog, or social media series inspired by what you saw.
  • Set a goal—whether it’s applying to a portfolio review, starting a side project, or networking with professionals.

Example: After seeing "Biomorphic Sculptures" by Isabella Lopez at the 2022 showcase, a local sculptor named Ryan Thompson incorporated her organic shapes into his own bronze castings. His new collection won a regional award, proving how cross-pollination of ideas can elevate work.

Common Mistakes to Avoid at the Art Showcase (And How to Fix Them)

Even the most enthusiastic attendees can make avoidable mistakes at an art showcase. Here’s what to watch out for—and how to correct them.

1. Not Reading the Artist Statements

Mistake: Walking past exhibit descriptions without understanding the context. Why it’s bad: You miss the deeper meaning behind the work, making your engagement superficial. Fix:

  • Spend 2–3 minutes reading each artist’s statement before viewing their work.
  • Ask the artist to clarify symbolism or techniques if something confuses you.

2. Overwhelming Yourself with Too Many Pieces

Mistake: Trying to see everything at once and not focusing deeply on any single work. Why it’s bad: You don’t absorb the details, and the experience feels rushed. Fix:

  • Prioritize 3–5 pieces that most interest you and study them closely.
  • Use a timer—spend 10–15 minutes per exhibit instead of 5 seconds.

3. Being Too Hesitant to Talk to Artists

Mistake: Not approaching artists because you’re too nervous or unsure what to say. Why it’s bad: You miss networking opportunities and don’t learn from their process. Fix:

  • Practice conversation starters (see Strategy #2 above).
  • Remember: Artists want to share their work—they’re excited to talk about it.

4. Ignoring the Technical Details

Mistake: Focusing only on aesthetics and not asking about the "how." Why it’s bad: You don’t learn practical skills that could improve your own work. Fix:

  • Ask about:
    • Software/tools used (e.g., "What plugins did you use in Photoshop?")
    • Materials (e.g., "Where did you source your unconventional paints?")
    • Process (e.g., "How many drafts did this take?")

5. Not Following Up After the Event

Mistake: Leaving without exchanging contact info or not connecting later. Why it’s bad: You lose out on future collaborations, mentorship, or job opportunities. Fix:

  • Collect business cards (or LinkedIn connections).
  • Send a thank-you message within 48 hours—personalized and brief.
  • Share their work on social media (with credit).

6. Assuming All Work Is for Sale

Mistake: Assuming every piece is available and bidding without checking. Why it’s bad: You waste time and money on unavailable items. Fix:

  • Ask the artist or curator before bidding.
  • Check the auction/sales list (often posted online).

7. Not Taking Breaks to Absorb the Experience

Mistake: Rushing through the entire event without processing what you see. Why it’s bad: You don’t retain key insights and miss emotional connections to the art. Fix:

  • Step away every 30–45 minutes to reflect, sketch, or take notes.
