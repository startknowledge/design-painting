*1. The Vision Behind "Numerous Structures": A Conversation with Jon Merritt

How Did "Numerous Structures" Come to Life?

"Numerous Structures" isn’t just another abstract exhibit—it’s a visual manifesto on balance, contrast, and the interplay between order and chaos. Merritt explains:

"I started by asking myself: What happens when geometry meets spontaneity? Traditional abstract art often leans into either rigid forms or chaotic brushstrokes. My goal was to create something where both coexist—where each structure responds to the others, like a living system."

This exhibit features repeating geometric motifs—triangles, hexagons, and asymmetrical shapes—that shift in scale and density across the canvas. The result? A rhythmic tension that draws the viewer’s eye in unpredictable ways.

The Influence of Nature and Architecture

Merritt draws inspiration from biomorphic forms (think coral reefs, honeycombs) and modernist architecture (like Zaha Hadid’s fluid structures). "I see art as a bridge between the natural world and human-made systems," he says. "The structures in this exhibit mimic how organisms grow—each element has its own life, yet they form a cohesive whole."

Key Takeaway: Abstract art doesn’t have to be just about color—geometry and movement can tell a story too.

2. Eight Actionable Strategies to Integrate Abstract Art into Your Design Projects

Whether you’re a painter, interior designer, or brand strategist, Merritt’s approach offers practical takeaways for using abstract elements effectively.

Strategy 1: Start with a "Skeleton" – Define Your Geometric Framework

Before adding color or texture, sketch a structural grid. Merritt often begins with overlapping lines or implied shapes that guide the composition.

  • Example: In "Numerous Structures", the central canvas features a hexagonal lattice that subtly influences where other elements land.
  • How to Apply: Use graph paper or digital tools (like Adobe Illustrator’s grid system) to map out your design’s "bones" before painting or decorating.

Strategy 2: Play with Scale and Repetition for Depth

Abstract art thrives on contrast in scale. Merritt’s work uses micro and macro structures—small details that become significant when viewed together.

  • Real-World Example: A corporate lobby in Chicago used Merritt’s "Numerous Structures" as a backdrop. The large-scale hexagons anchored the space, while smaller, scattered shapes created visual interest on walls and furniture.
  • Action Step: If decorating, mix large abstract art pieces with smaller complementary elements (e.g., a bold canvas paired with geometric throw pillows).

Strategy 3: Limit Your Palette for Maximum Impact

Merritt’s color choices are deliberately restrained—often two to three dominant hues with subtle variations.

  • Why It Works: A limited palette ensures cohesion while allowing textural contrast (e.g., matte vs. glossy surfaces).
  • How to Apply: Test colors on a small canvas or sample board before committing to a large project.

Strategy 4: Embrace Imperfection – Controlled Chaos

While geometry provides structure, organic brushstrokes or uneven edges add life.

  • Example: In "Numerous Structures", some shapes are perfectly aligned, while others bleed slightly, creating a sense of movement.
  • Tip: Use textured brushes or sponges to introduce subtle irregularities in your designs.

Strategy 5: Use Abstract Art to Define Zones in a Space

Abstract pieces can separate functional areas without walls.

  • Example: A home office in New York used a large abstract mural to divide the workspace from the living area, making the space feel intentional yet open.
  • Action Step: Place a bold abstract piece near a doorway or seating area to guide movement in a room.

Strategy 6: Pair Abstract Art with Functional Textiles

Merritt often collaborates with textile designers to extend his aesthetic into fabrics.

  • Example: A hotel in Miami featured "Numerous Structures"-inspired curtains and upholstery, tying the art to the room’s decor.
  • How to Apply: Choose throw blankets, rugs, or curtains with subtle geometric patterns to complement your abstract art.

Strategy 7: Consider the "Negative Space" as Part of the Design

The empty areas between shapes are just as important as the forms themselves.

  • Merritt’s Approach: He leaves breathing room around key structures, ensuring they don’t feel cluttered.
  • Tip: When framing art, leave 2–4 inches of space around the edges to avoid visual crowding.

Strategy 8: Use Abstract Art for Branding and Wayfinding

Businesses can leverage abstract design for logo creation and interior wayfinding.

  • Example: A tech company in San Francisco used Merritt’s hexagonal motifs in their office signage and meeting room labels, reinforcing brand identity.
  • Action Step: If designing a brand, test abstract shapes in logos or directional signs to see if they communicate clarity and innovation.

3. Common Mistakes in Abstract Art Integration (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most well-intentioned designs can go wrong. Here’s how to steer clear of pitfalls:

Mistake 1: Overcomplicating the Composition

Problem: Too many shapes, colors, or textures can overwhelm the viewer. Solution: Stick to 3–5 key elements (shapes, colors, textures) and edit ruthlessly.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the Room’s Existing Elements

Problem: Abstract art that clashes with furniture or flooring looks jarring. Solution: Test art in the space before purchasing. Ask:

  • Does the color palette complement the room?
  • Does the scale feel proportional?

Mistake 3: Treating Abstract Art as "Just Decoration"

Problem: If abstract pieces are randomly placed, they lose impact. Solution: Intentional placement matters. Use art to anchor a space, guide flow, or highlight a feature.

Mistake 4: Skipping the "Why" Behind the Design

Problem: Abstract art without purpose can feel arbitrary. Solution: Ask:

  • What emotion do I want this art to evoke?
  • How does it serve the space’s function?

Mistake 5: Neglecting Lighting

Problem: Abstract art looks different under natural vs. artificial light. Solution: Test lighting before finalizing a piece. Use warm lighting to enhance depth in geometric designs.

5. FAQ: Answers to Your Biggest Questions About Abstract Art & Design

To help you apply these lessons, here are five schema-optimized FAQs with detailed answers.

Q1: How do I choose the right abstract art for my space?

A: Choosing abstract art depends on three key factors:

  1. The room’s purpose – A minimalist hexagon piece works well in a home office (focused, structured), while a bold, chaotic abstract suits a lounge area (relaxed, expressive).
  2. The existing color palette – Use a color wheel to ensure harmony. For example, cool blues and greens pair well with warm wood tones.
  3. The scale of the space – In a small room, opt for smaller pieces or vertical art to avoid overwhelming the area. In a large gallery, multiple medium-sized canvases create balance.

Pro Tip: Visit galleries or virtual art platforms (like Artsy or Saatchi Art) to filter by mood (e.g., "calm," "energetic," "modern").

Q2: Can abstract art work in a traditional or classic interior?

A: Absolutely—contrasts create interest. For example:

  • A classic white-and-gold room can be elevated with a single abstract piece in deep red or navy.
  • Neoclassical furniture pairs beautifully with modern geometric art for a timeless yet contemporary look.

Example: A Victorian-era home in Boston used a large abstract mural in the dining room, balancing the ornate woodwork with clean lines.

Q3: How do I hang abstract art to maximize impact?

A: Hanging abstract art is an art in itself. Follow these rules:

  • Center it – For large canvases, align the center of the piece with the center of the wall.
  • Use the "eye level" rule – The top of the art should be at eye level when standing (typically 57–60 inches from the floor).
  • Create a focal point – If the room has multiple artworks, arrange them in a triangle or staggered line to guide the viewer’s gaze.
  • Consider the "golden ratio" – For three pieces, place the largest at the bottom, the middle-sized in the center, and the smallest at the top for balance.

Q4: What’s the difference between abstract art and non-representational art?

A: While abstract art is non-literal (it doesn’t depict real objects), non-representational art is a subset that explicitly rejects recognizable forms.

  • Abstract art can still have subtle references (e.g., a painting that hints at a landscape but isn’t one).
  • Non-representational art (like Merritt’s "Numerous Structures") rejects all representation, focusing purely on form, color, and texture.

Think of it this way:

  • Abstract = A blurred photograph (recognizable but altered).
  • Non-representational = A completely new language of shapes.

Q5: How can I create my own abstract designs if I’m not an artist?

A: You don’t need to be a painter to design abstract pieces. Try these beginner-friendly methods:

  1. Start with a grid – Use graph paper or digital tools to map out shapes.
  2. Use stencils – Cut out geometric patterns and paint over them for precise lines.
  3. Experiment with textures – Try sand, fabric, or metallic paints for depth.
  4. Inspire from nature – Study crystals, leaves, or waves for organic abstract forms.
  5. Collaborate with artists – Many abstract artists (like Merritt) offer custom commissions for spaces.

Example: A non-artist in Los Angeles created a wall mural by:

  • Printing geometric patterns on vinyl decals.
  • Arranging them in a random yet balanced layout.
  • Sealing with clear spray paint for durability.
