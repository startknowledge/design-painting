The Story of Angelus Novus: From Klee’s Studio to the Jewish Museum

Who Was Paul Klee? A Brief Biography

Paul Klee (1879–1940) was a Swiss-German painter, draftsman, and printmaker whose work spans expressionism, cubism, and surrealism. Born into a musical family (his father was a musician), Klee initially studied music before shifting to art. His early works were influenced by post-impressionism, but by the 1920s, he had developed a distinctive, almost childlike yet deeply symbolic style.

Klee’s life was marked by trauma and displacement:

  • He was dismissed from his teaching position at the Bauhaus in 1931 due to Nazi political pressure.
  • In 1933, he fled to Switzerland, where he continued creating until his death in 1940 from pulmonary fibrosis.

Despite his struggles, Klee’s work remained profoundly philosophical, often exploring time, memory, and the sublime.

The Angelus Novus Series: A Vision of Chaos and Renewal

Klee’s Angelus Novus (1920) is part of a series of works inspired by Angelus Novus, a poem by Paul Scheerbart (a German writer influenced by Jewish mysticism and futurism). The poem describes an angel who faces the future with a broken sword, symbolizing both destruction and rebirth.

Klee’s interpretation is fragmented, geometric, and dreamlike:

  • The angel is depicted in a state of motion, as if caught in an eternal storm.
  • The broken sword represents the cyclical nature of history—where every end is also a beginning.
  • The color palette (deep blues, blacks, and whites) evokes both despair and transcendence.

This work was highly influential in Walter Benjamin’s Theses on the Philosophy of History (1940), where he describes the angel as:

“A figure that would like to stay with the wreckage of the past. His face is turned toward the past; where a chain of events appears before his eyes, he sees one single catastrophe which keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage and hurls it in front of his feet. The angel would like to stay, awaken the dead, and make whole what has been smashed.”

Why Is This Piece at the Jewish Museum?

The Jewish Museum’s decision to feature Angelus Novus is not just about Klee’s Jewish heritage (though he was raised in a Jewish household and later converted to Christianity). Instead, it’s about how art can serve as a mirror for collective memory.

  • Klee’s Jewish Influences: While Klee was not observant, his work was deeply shaped by Jewish philosophical and mystical traditions, particularly Kabbalah and Hasidism. The Angelus Novus series reflects a preoccupation with time, prophecy, and the apocalyptic.
  • The Museum’s Mission: The Jewish Museum has long been a space for exploring Jewish identity through art, literature, and history. By placing Klee’s work alongside contemporary Jewish artists, they create a dialogue between past and present.
  • A Call to Reinterpretation: The exhibition suggests that even the most "universal" art has cultural roots. Klee’s angel is not just a symbol of modernity—it’s also a Jewish metaphor for survival and renewal.

8 Actionable Strategies for Interpreting Modernist Art Like an Expert

Understanding modernist art—especially works like Angelus Novus—requires more than just visual analysis. It demands historical context, philosophical engagement, and an openness to ambiguity. Here are eight strategies to deepen your appreciation of such masterpieces:

1. Study the Artist’s Life and Influences

Modernist artists were highly influenced by their personal and political environments. For Klee:

  • Research his Jewish background (his father was Jewish, but Klee himself converted).
  • Explore his time at the Bauhaus, where he collaborated with Wassily Kandinsky and Lyonel Feininger.
  • Read his own writings—Klee kept a detailed journal where he reflected on his creative process.

Action Step: Create a timeline of Klee’s life and note how major events (e.g., the rise of Nazism) shaped his work.

2. Analyze the Symbolism in the Work

Angelus Novus is rich with symbols:

  • The angel’s broken sword → the futility of war and the cyclical nature of history.
  • The stormy background → chaos as a force of transformation.
  • The fragmented form → the incompleteness of human perception.

Action Step: Write a symbol-by-symbol breakdown of the painting, referencing Klee’s own notes or Benjamin’s interpretation.

3. Compare It to Other Works in the Series

Klee created multiple versions of Angelus Novus. Comparing them helps reveal:

  • How his style evolved (e.g., earlier versions were more expressionist, later ones more geometric).
  • Different interpretations of the same theme (some angels are more human-like, others more abstract).

Action Step: Visit digital archives (like the Klee Foundation’s collection) to compare three versions and note differences.

4. Connect It to Philosophical and Religious Texts

Klee’s work was deeply influenced by:

  • Jewish mysticism (e.g., Lurianic Kabbalah, which speaks of Tikkun Olam—repairing the world).
  • Walter Benjamin’s Theses on the Philosophy of History, which uses the angel as a metaphor for progress.
  • Friedrich Nietzsche’s ideas on eternal recurrence, where history repeats itself in cycles.

Action Step: Read Benjamin’s theses and Klee’s journal entries, then map their ideas onto the painting.

5. Consider the Historical Context of Its Creation

Angelus Novus was painted in 1920, a time of post-WWI trauma, economic collapse, and political instability in Europe. Klee’s work reflects:

  • The fear of another apocalypse (the angel’s broken sword mirrors the shattered hopes of the post-war era).
  • The rise of fascism, which would later force Klee into exile.

Action Step: Research European art of the 1920s (e.g., Otto Dix’s war paintings) and see how Klee’s work fits into the broader anti-war movement.

6. Explore Contemporary Reinterpretations

Modern artists and thinkers have revisited Angelus Novus in different ways:

  • Jewish artists (e.g., Yael Bartana) use the angel as a symbol of resilience.
  • Postmodern theorists (e.g., Judith Butler) discuss it in terms of grief and collective memory.
  • Digital artists (e.g., AI-generated versions) challenge traditional interpretations.

Action Step: Find one contemporary artist who references Klee and write a short essay on their connection.

7. Visit the Exhibition with a Critical Lens

When viewing Angelus Novus in person (or online), ask:

  • How does the museum’s framing change my understanding?
  • Are there other works in the exhibition that dialogue with Klee?
  • How does the physical space (lighting, placement) affect my perception?

Action Step: Take notes on the exhibition’s layout and how Klee’s work interacts with other pieces.

8. Create Your Own Artistic Response

The best way to fully engage with a masterpiece is to respond to it creatively. Try:

  • Drawing your own version of the angel (using Klee’s geometric style).
  • Writing a poem or short story inspired by the painting.
  • Making a digital collage that combines Angelus Novus with modern imagery.

Action Step: Dedicate one hour to a creative response—whether it’s painting, writing, or even a social media post.

Real-World Examples: How Angelus Novus Has Shaped Culture

Example 1: Walter Benjamin’s Theses on the Philosophy of History (1940)

Benjamin’s famous interpretation of Klee’s angel transformed the painting from a private artistic vision into a philosophical landmark. His idea that the angel faces the wreckage of history became a key metaphor for leftist thought, influencing:

  • Post-war European intellectuals (e.g., Theodor Adorno, Hannah Arendt).
  • Modern discussions on trauma and memory (e.g., how societies process war).

Why It Matters: This shows how art can become a tool for political and philosophical analysis.

Example 2: The Bauhaus and Modern Education

Klee taught at the Bauhaus, where his work was part of a revolution in art education. His approach—combining abstraction with craftsmanship—influenced:

  • Graphic design (e.g., Swiss Style, minimalist typography).
  • Architecture (e.g., Mies van der Rohe’s geometric forms).

Why It Matters: Angelus Novus is not just a painting—it’s a blueprint for modern design.

Why It Matters: Klee’s Angelus Novus is now part of a living conversation about Jewish modernity.

Common Mistakes in Analyzing Modernist Art (And How to Avoid Them)

Analyzing modernist art—especially abstract or symbolic works like Angelus Novus—can be tricky. Here are five common pitfalls and how to steer clear of them:

1. Overlooking the Historical Context

Mistake: Assuming Klee’s angel is just a "pretty" painting without deeper meaning. Solution: Always research the artist’s life, the era, and cultural movements that influenced the work.

Example: If you only see Angelus Novus as a stormy scene, you miss its connection to post-WWI despair.

2. Ignoring the Artist’s Own Words

Mistake: Relying solely on external interpretations (e.g., Benjamin’s theses) without checking Klee’s journals or letters. Solution: Look for primary sources—Klee wrote extensively about his process.

Example: Klee described his angels as "figures of transition," which changes how we read Angelus Novus.

3. Forcing a Single Meaning

Mistake: Treating abstract art as if it has one definitive interpretation. Solution: Embrace ambiguity—modernist art often resists easy answers.

Example: Is the angel hopeful or despairing? The answer depends on who you ask.

4. Neglecting the Physical Experience

Mistake: Analyzing art only through textbooks or digital images without seeing it in person. Solution: If possible, visit the exhibition—lighting, scale, and placement alter the viewing experience.

Example: The size and placement of Angelus Novus in the Jewish Museum may evoke different emotions than a small print.

5. Comparing It Only to Other Modernists

Mistake: Only discussing Klee alongside Picasso or Kandinsky, ignoring earlier influences (e.g., Jewish mysticism, medieval art). Solution: Explore broader cultural and religious traditions that shaped the work.

Example: Klee’s angels resemble medieval depictions of angels, but his fragmented style is uniquely modern.
