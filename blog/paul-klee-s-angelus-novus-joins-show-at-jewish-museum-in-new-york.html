The Genesis of Angelus Novus: A Fragment with a Story

What Was Angelus Novus?

Paul Klee began Angelus Novus in 1920, a year marked by the aftermath of World War I and the rise of political instability in Europe. The work was intended as a triptych, with each panel exploring different facets of an angelic figure—a symbol often associated with annunciation, judgment, and transcendence. However, Klee abandoned the project midway, leaving behind three incomplete panels:

  1. Angelus Novus (1920) – The central figure, a winged angel with a harp, gazing into the distance.
  2. Angelus Novus II (1920) – A more abstract, fragmented version of the same theme.
  3. Angelus Novus III (1920) – A smaller, more symbolic depiction, now part of the Jewish Museum’s collection.

The central panel, the most iconic, was later acquired by the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam in 1946. The Jewish Museum’s fragment—likely Angelus Novus III—represents a lesser-known but equally profound iteration of Klee’s vision.

The Symbolism Behind the Angel

Klee’s angel is not the traditional, serene figure of religious iconography. Instead, it embodies:

  • Displacement: The angel’s gaze is fixed on the horizon, as if watching history unfold. This mirrors Klee’s own experiences as a Swiss artist in a fractured Europe, where war and political upheaval forced many to question their place in the world.
  • Memory and Loss: The fragmented nature of the work reflects the fragmented identities of Jewish artists in the early 20th century, many of whom were later targeted by the Nazis.
  • The Void: Klee’s later works, including Angelus Novus, explore existential emptiness—a theme that resonates deeply with Jewish philosophical traditions, from Martin Buber’s dialogue with the void to the Kabbalistic concept of Tzimtzum (the divine withdrawal).

Why the Jewish Museum?

The Jewish Museum’s acquisition of this fragment is not accidental. Klee’s work has long been studied through a Jewish lens, particularly his engagement with:

  • Jewish mysticism (his fascination with the Sefer Yetzirah, the "Book of Creation")
  • Exile and displacement (his own family’s history of Jewish migration)
  • The Holocaust’s artistic legacy (how his unfinished works became symbols of interrupted narratives)

In 2019, the museum hosted "Paul Klee and the Jewish Imagination," an exhibition that drew over 20,000 visitors and sparked debates about how modernist art intersects with Jewish identity. The current show, "Painting the Void," builds on this by positioning Klee’s fragments as testaments to both artistic innovation and cultural resilience.

8 Actionable Strategies for Artists & Collectors Engaging with Modernist Fragments

Modernist art fragments—like Klee’s Angelus Novus—present unique challenges and opportunities. Whether you’re an artist, collector, or curator, here’s how to engage with them effectively.

1. Research the Fragment’s Provenance with Precision

Problem: Many modernist fragments lack clear documentation, leading to disputes over authenticity. Solution:

  • Consult art historical archives (e.g., the Paul Klee Foundation in Bern, Switzerland).
  • Work with specialized provenance researchers (e.g., Art Loss Register or Artnet’s provenance database).
  • Example: The Angelus Novus fragment at the Jewish Museum was traced through Klee’s personal correspondence, where he mentions sketching the angel during a period of deep introspection.

Action Step: Always demand chain-of-custody records before acquiring a fragment.

2. Understand the Fragment’s Symbolic Weight

Problem: A fragment may seem like a "minor" work, but its contextual meaning can elevate its value. Solution:

  • Study the artist’s broader oeuvre (e.g., Klee’s Child and Bird series, which also explores innocence and loss).
  • Consult philosophical texts that influenced the artist (e.g., Klee’s notes on Wassily Kandinsky’s Point and Line to Plane).
  • Example: Klee’s Angelus Novus was inspired by Angelus Silesius’ 17th-century poem, "The Cherubic Wonder"—a text about divine mystery. This connection adds layers to the fragment’s interpretation.

Action Step: Create a contextual dossier for the fragment, linking it to literary, philosophical, and historical sources.

3. Preserve the Fragment with Expert Conservation

Problem: Modernist works often suffer from poor preservation, leading to degradation. Solution:

  • Partner with specialized conservators (e.g., the Getty Conservation Institute).
  • Use non-invasive techniques like infrared reflectography to study hidden layers.
  • Example: The Angelus Novus fragment at the Jewish Museum underwent UV analysis to reveal Klee’s underdrawings, confirming its authenticity.

Action Step: Invest in climate-controlled storage and regular condition reports.

4. Exhibit the Fragment in a Thematically Cohesive Show

Problem: A fragment can feel isolated if displayed without context. Solution:

  • Pair it with complementary works (e.g., Klee’s Twittering Machine or Castle and Sun).
  • Use curatorial narratives that tie the fragment to broader themes (e.g., exile, memory, or the sublime).
  • Example: The Jewish Museum’s "Painting the Void" exhibition groups Angelus Novus with Jewish mystical texts and surrealist poetry, creating a dialogue between art and thought.

Action Step: Develop a multi-media exhibition plan, including audio guides and scholarly essays.

5. Leverage Digital Technology for Accessibility

Problem: Many fragments are physically inaccessible to the public. Solution:

  • Create high-resolution digital scans (e.g., using 3D modeling).
  • Offer virtual tours with interactive annotations.
  • Example: The Stedelijk Museum’s digital archive allows users to explore Angelus Novus in detail, including layered analysis of Klee’s techniques.

6. Engage with the Fragment’s Cultural Legacy

  • Host public lectures on the fragment’s history (e.g., "Klee and the Jewish Imagination").
  • Collaborate with scholars of Jewish studies, philosophy, and art history.

7. Document the Fragment’s Evolution Over Time

Problem: Without documentation, a fragment’s artistic development is lost. Solution:

  • Track sketchbooks, studies, and finished works related to the fragment.
  • Use artificial intelligence tools (e.g., Google’s Art & Culture AI) to compare variations.
  • Example: The Angelus Novus fragment’s underdrawings reveal Klee’s initial vision of a more angular, almost cubist angel, before he shifted to a softer, more lyrical style.

Common Mistakes When Handling Modernist Fragments (And How to Avoid Them)

1. Ignoring Provenance Without Thorough Research

Mistake: Assuming a fragment’s history is clear without documentary evidence. Solution:

  • Always verify pre-war ownership records.
  • Consult art auction archives (e.g., Sotheby’s, Christie’s).
  • Example: The Kandinsky Composition V case (2013) revealed Nazi-era looting, leading to a restitution claim. Without proper research, collectors risk legal and ethical pitfalls.

How to Avoid: Use provenance databases like LostArt.net or Art Loss Register.

2. Displaying Fragments Without Context

Mistake: Treating a fragment as a standalone object rather than part of a larger narrative. Solution:

  • Group fragments with related works.
  • Provide scholarly annotations in exhibitions.
  • Example: The MoMA’s Klee Retrospective (2017) placed Angelus Novus alongside Klee’s Castle and Sun*, showing how the angel evolved in his later works.

How to Avoid: Develop a curatorial framework before acquisition.

3. Poor Conservation Leading to Degradation

Mistake: Storing fragments in uncontrolled environments, leading to fading or damage. Solution:

  • Use acid-free materials for framing.
  • Maintain stable humidity and temperature.
  • Example: The Metropolitan Museum’s Picasso Fragments suffered from light exposure, prompting stricter conservation protocols.

How to Avoid: Partner with specialized conservators (e.g., The Getty Conservation Institute).

4. Overcommercializing Fragments

  • Focus on educational and public programs.
