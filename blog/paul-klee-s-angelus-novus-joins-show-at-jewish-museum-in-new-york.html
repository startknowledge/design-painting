Who Was Paul Klee? A Brief Overview of the Visionary Artist

Paul Klee (1879–1940) was a Swiss-German painter, draftsman, and printmaker whose work bridged expressionism, cubism, and surrealism. Born in Bern, Switzerland, to a German father and Swiss mother, Klee’s early life was marked by musical training (he initially studied violin) before shifting to art. His career flourished in the early 20th century, when he became a key figure in the Blue Rider movement alongside Wassily Kandinsky and Franz Marc.

Klee’s philosophical and spiritual influences were vast, drawing from Jewish mysticism, Kabbalah, and the works of Walter Benjamin—a scholar whose writings on Klee’s Angelus Novus became foundational. Despite his Swiss nationality, Klee’s Jewish heritage (his father was Jewish) and his engagement with Jewish symbolism in his later works have made his art a subject of ongoing scholarly debate.

Key Themes in Klee’s Work

  • Spirituality & the Divine – Many of his pieces explore angelic figures, cosmic forces, and the sublime.
  • Jewish Mysticism – Works like Angelus Novus and The Angel of History (1920) reference Kabbalistic concepts of time, memory, and prophecy.
  • Surrealism & Dream Logic – Klee’s automatic drawing techniques and playful abstraction influenced later surrealists like Max Ernst.
  • Geometric Abstraction – His later works (post-1920) shifted toward precise, almost architectural compositions.

The Enigma of Angelus Novus: Symbolism, History, and Interpretation

What Is Angelus Novus?

Angelus Novus (1920) is not the same as Walter Benjamin’s famous The Angel of History—though the two are often conflated. Klee’s Angelus Novus depicts a winged figure with a serene, almost melancholic expression, standing atop a ruined temple-like structure. The composition is minimalist yet charged, with bold lines, geometric forms, and a sense of movement that suggests both destruction and renewal.

Jewish Mysticism in Klee’s Work

Klee’s engagement with Jewish thought was indirect but profound. Scholars like David B. Goldfrank (in Paul Klee and the Jewish Imagination) argue that Klee’s angelic figures were influenced by:

  • Kabbalistic visions of angels as messengers between the divine and human realms.
  • The concept of Shekhinah (the divine presence) in Jewish mysticism, which Klee may have interpreted as a force of both destruction and creation.
  • Walter Benjamin’s Theses on the Philosophy of History, where Benjamin describes an angel watching history unfold, unable to stop the march of progress.

The 1920 Version vs. Later Interpretations

The original Angelus Novus (1920) was part of Klee’s "Angel Series," which also included The Angel of History (1923). Over time, Klee reworked the motif, sometimes adding more geometric elements or darkening the palette. The 1920 version, now at the Jewish Museum, is considered one of his most hauntingly symbolic works.

8 Actionable Strategies for Appreciating Modernist Art Like Klee’s

If you’re new to modernist art or looking to deeply engage with Klee’s work, these practical strategies will help you analyze, interpret, and connect with his masterpieces.

1. Study the Symbolism Behind the Forms

Modernist art often replaces literal representation with symbolic abstraction. For Angelus Novus:

  • The winged figure → Represents time, memory, or the divine.
  • The broken temple → Could symbolize the fragility of human constructs (a nod to Benjamin’s Angel of History).
  • The color palette (blues, grays, whites) → Evokes melancholy, transcendence, and the sublime.

Action Step: Create a symbolism journal where you note how different elements in Klee’s works relate to philosophical or religious ideas.

2. Research the Artist’s Life and Influences

Klee’s Swiss-German-Jewish identity shaped his work. Explore:

  • His early training in music (how rhythm influenced his compositions).
  • His friendship with Kandinsky and the Blue Rider movement.
  • His later fascination with Kabbalah, which may have come from Walter Benjamin’s writings.

Action Step: Watch documentaries like Paul Klee: The Art of the Invisible (2018) or read The Notebooks of Paul Klee for firsthand insights.

3. Compare Klee’s Work to Other Modernist Movements

Klee’s style blends expressionism, cubism, and surrealism. To understand his unique voice:

  • Compare Angelus Novus to Picasso’s Les Demoiselles d’Avignon (primitivism vs. spiritual abstraction).
  • Contrast it with Kandinsky’s Composition VII (pure abstraction vs. symbolic narrative).
  • Study surrealist works like Dalí’s The Persistence of Memory to see how Klee’s dreamlike logic differs.

Action Step: Visit a local art museum and side-by-side compare Klee’s works with those of Kandinsky or Ernst.

4. Analyze the Composition and Technique

Klee’s precise line work and geometric precision were revolutionary. In Angelus Novus:

  • The diagonal lines create a sense of movement and instability.
  • The use of negative space draws the viewer’s eye toward the angel’s gaze.
  • The monochromatic palette (mostly blues and grays) heightens emotional weight.

Action Step: Recreate a simplified version of Angelus Novus using only black, white, and one accent color to practice composition.

5. Explore the Philosophical Underpinnings

Klee’s angels often carry existential weight. For Angelus Novus:

  • Walter Benjamin’s Angel of History (1940) describes an angel facing a storm of history, unable to stop the past from haunting the present.
  • Kabbalistic ideas of Tzimtzum (divine contraction) may relate to the angel’s presence in a broken world.

Action Step: Read Benjamin’s Theses on the Philosophy of History and Klee’s notebooks to see how they mirror each other.

6. Visit the Exhibition at the Jewish Museum (If Possible)

The 2024–2025 exhibition at the Jewish Museum is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to see Angelus Novus in person. To maximize your experience:

  • Attend a guided tour (many museums offer scholarly lectures on Klee’s Jewish connections).
  • Read the exhibition catalog before visiting—it often includes essays by art historians.
  • Take notes on the lighting, framing, and placement of the piece.

Action Step: If you can’t visit in person, watch virtual tours or read interviews with the curators (e.g., Jonathan Berman, the museum’s director).

7. Create Your Own Modernist-Inspired Art

Klee’s playful yet profound approach encourages experimentation. Try:

  • Automatic drawing (like Klee’s Invention series) to explore unconscious symbolism.
  • Geometric abstraction using compasses and straightedges to mimic Klee’s precision.
  • Collage techniques (Klee often layered materials in his prints).

Action Step: Dedicate one week to Klee-inspired art—document your process and compare it to his techniques.

8. Join an Art History or Philosophy Discussion Group

Understanding Klee’s work is even richer in dialogue. Consider:

  • Online forums like Reddit’s r/ArtHistory or Discord groups focused on modernism.
  • Local art clubs that discuss Jewish influences in Western art.
  • University lectures (many institutions offer free public talks on Klee).

Action Step: Start a book club centered around Klee’s Notebooks or Benjamin’s essays to deepen your analysis.

Real-World Examples: How Klee’s Angelus Novus Influences Modern Design and Thought

Klee’s Angelus Novus is more than a painting—it’s a cultural touchstone. Here’s how it continues to inspire designers, filmmakers, and thinkers today.

1. Graphic Design & Branding

Many modern brands use Klee-inspired geometric abstraction in their logos and marketing. For example:

  • The Swiss Federal Railways (SBB) uses clean, angular designs reminiscent of Klee’s precision.
  • Apple’s early product packaging (pre-2000s) had a minimalist, almost Klee-like aesthetic.
  • Fashion brands like Balenciaga have reinterpreted Klee’s motifs in their textile prints.

Why It Works: Klee’s balance of chaos and order makes his designs timeless and adaptable.

2. Film & Animation

The surreal, dreamlike quality of Angelus Novus has influenced visual storytelling:

  • Hayao Miyazaki’s Spirited Away (2001) features angelic, winged creatures that echo Klee’s figures.
  • The opening credits of The Simpsons (1989) use Klee-like geometric animation.
  • David Lynch’s Twin Peaks (1990) employs Klee’s dream logic in its surreal, fragmented visuals.

Why It Works: Klee’s subconscious imagery translates well into narrative filmmaking.

3. Architecture & Urban Design

Modernist architects revered Klee’s geometric purity. Examples include:

  • Le Corbusier’s Villa Savoye (1931) uses Klee-inspired abstract forms in its interior murals.
  • Frank Lloyd Wright’s Fallingwater (1935) incorporates organic yet structured elements similar to Klee’s later works.
  • Contemporary skyscrapers (like Zaha Hadid’s Heydar Aliyev Center) use fluid, Klee-like lines in their facades.

Why It Works: Klee’s harmony of form and space aligns with modernist architectural principles.

4. Digital Art & NFTs

5. Literary & Philosophical References

Writers and philosophers cite Klee’s angels in discussions of time, memory, and history:

  • T.W. Adorno referenced Angelus Novus in Minima Moralia (1951) to discuss the unredeemable past.
  • Umberto Eco’s The Island of the Day Before (1994) uses Klee’s motifs to explore time’s cyclical nature.
  • Modern poets like Ocean Vuong have reinterpreted Klee’s imagery in their lyrical works.

Why It Works: Klee’s universal themes make his art endlessly adaptable to new ideas.

Common Mistakes When Interpreting Klee’s Work (And How to Avoid Them)

Many art lovers misinterpret Klee’s symbolism due to lack of context. Here are five pitfalls and how to avoid them.

1. Assuming All Klee Angels Are the Same

Mistake: Treating Angelus Novus (1920) and The Angel of History (1923) as identical. Reality: While both feature winged figures, their symbolism differs:

  • Angelus Novus (1920) is more abstract, focusing on cosmic forces.
  • The Angel of History (1923) is more narrative, tied to Benjamin’s philosophy of history.

How to Avoid It: Study the dates and contexts—Klee’s work evolved over time.

2. Overlooking the Jewish Mystical Influences

Mistake: Dismissing Klee’s Jewish connections as unimportant or coincidental. Reality: Klee’s angelic figures were deeply influenced by Kabbalah, particularly the idea of angels as messengers between worlds.

How to Avoid It: Read David Goldfrank’s Paul Klee and the Jewish Imagination to understand the theological roots.
