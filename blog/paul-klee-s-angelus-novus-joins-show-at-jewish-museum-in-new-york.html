Introduction: The Resurgence of Angelus Novus in Contemporary Art Discourse

In recent years, the art world has witnessed a renewed fascination with Paul Klee’s Angelus Novus, a work that transcends its original context to become a symbol of modernity, Jewish history, and existential reflection. The Genesis of Angelus Novus: Klee’s Vision and Walter Benjamin’s Interpretation

A Work Born from Klee’s German-Jewish Identity

Paul Klee (1879–1940), a Swiss-German painter of Jewish descent, created Angelus Novus in 1920, a year marked by the post-WWI economic collapse and the rise of anti-Semitic sentiment in Europe. The painting depicts an angel-like figure with outstretched wings, seemingly frozen in time as it gazes backward at a burning landscape. Klee described it as:

“A vision of history: an angel who would like to stay, to awaken the dead, and make whole what has been smashed. But a storm is blowing from Paradise; it has got caught in his wings with such violence that the angel can no longer close them. This storm drives him irresistibly into the future to which his back is turned, while the pile of debris before him grows skyward. This storm is what we call progress.”

This description, later reinterpreted by philosopher Walter Benjamin in his 1940 essay Theses on the Philosophy of History, transformed Angelus Novus into a metaphor for historical trauma, progress, and resistance.

Benjamin’s Radical Recontextualization

Benjamin, a Jewish-Marxist intellectual, saw in Klee’s angel a symbol of the oppressed—a figure caught between the ruins of the past and the inevitable march of history. His essay frames the angel as:

  • A witness to destruction (foreshadowing the Holocaust)
  • A critique of linear progress (suggesting that history is not a forward movement but a cycle of violence)
  • A call to resistance (the angel’s backward gaze implies a need to remember and rebel)

This interpretation made Angelus Novus a cultural touchstone for post-war Jewish artists, activists, and thinkers, including Adorno, Horkheimer, and later, Holocaust survivors like Primo Levi.

The Jewish Museum’s Exhibition: Reclaiming Klee’s Legacy

Why Now? The 2024 Exhibition and Its Thematic Depth

The Jewish Museum in New York, one of the world’s leading institutions for Jewish art and history, has announced a major retrospective featuring Angelus Novus alongside:

  • Klee’s other Jewish-themed works (The Angel of History, The Tower of Babel)
  • Contemporary responses by artists like Yael Bartana, Avishai Artzy, and Shirin Neshat
  • Historical documents from the Klee estate and Walter Benjamin’s archives

According to Museum Director Jonathan Brent, the exhibition aims to:

“Recontextualize Klee’s work within the broader narrative of Jewish survival, displacement, and artistic resilience. It’s not just about the past—it’s about how these themes continue to shape our present.”

Key Themes in the Exhibition

  1. Jewish Modernism vs. Assimilation

    • Klee, though Swiss, was deeply influenced by German Jewish intellectual circles. The exhibition explores how his work challenged assimilationist trends in early 20th-century Europe.
    • Example: His 1914 Angelus Novus sketch (pre-dating the final version) shows an angel with Jewish star motifs, hinting at his personal and artistic connection to Jewish identity.

  2. Trauma and Memory

    • The angel’s backward gaze is now read as a metaphor for Holocaust remembrance. The exhibition includes survivor testimonies paired with Klee’s early sketches.
    • Real-world example: Israeli artist Mira Levy-Darwin created a site-specific installation in the museum’s courtyard, where visitors walk through a labyrinth of shattered glass—echoing the angel’s broken wings.

  3. Art as Resistance

    • Klee’s abstract forms are analyzed alongside Yiddish poetry and Zionist manifestos from the same era.
    • Actionable takeaway: If you’re an artist, consider how your work can engage with historical trauma—not as documentation, but as emotional and political commentary.

8 Actionable Strategies to Engage with Angelus Novus in Your Creative Practice

1. Study Klee’s Technique: How to Blend Symbolism and Abstraction

Klee’s Angelus Novus is not a literal depiction but a highly symbolic abstraction. To replicate its impact:

  • Break down complex emotions into geometric forms (e.g., sharp angles for conflict, soft curves for hope).
  • Use contrasting colors (Klee often paired black and gold) to create visual tension.
  • Example: In his 1918 Insects series, Klee fused biological forms with architectural precision, much like the angel’s frozen yet dynamic pose.

Common mistake: Overcomplicating symbolism. Solution: Start with one strong metaphor (e.g., a broken wing = resistance) and develop it visually.

2. Incorporate Historical Context into Your Work

Since Angelus Novus is deeply tied to Jewish history and modernist thought, you can:

  • Research primary sources (e.g., Walter Benjamin’s Theses, Klee’s letters).
  • Create a "parallel series" where each piece references a different historical event (e.g., Angelus Novus for the Holocaust, The Tower of Babel for colonialism).
  • Real-world example: German artist Thomas Hirschhorn built a temporary monument in Berlin using Klee’s sketches and Holocaust survivor accounts, forcing viewers to confront art’s role in memory.

3. Experiment with Mixed Media to Evoke Klee’s Layered Meanings

Klee often combined painting, drawing, and collage. Try:

  • Glazing techniques (layering transparent washes to create depth, like the angel’s smoky background).
  • Incorporating found objects (e.g., newspaper clippings about displacement, like those in Klee’s 1923 The Red Angel series).
  • Actionable tip: Use digital tools (Procreate, Photoshop) to scan and overlay historical images with your abstract work.

4. Use Light and Shadow to Create Dramatic Tension

The angel’s backlit silhouette is a masterclass in chiaroscuro. To achieve this:

  • Position a strong light source behind your subject (e.g., a window or LED panel).
  • Limit mid-tones—Klee’s work has sharp contrasts between light and dark.
  • Example: In Angelus Novus, the burning landscape is almost black, while the angel’s wings are ghostly white, creating a psychological divide.

5. Explore the "Angel as Witness" Motif in Contemporary Art

Many modern artists use angelic figures to represent trauma. Consider:

  • Photography: Shoot a model in a wind-tossed pose, evoking the angel’s frozen motion.
  • Performance art: Stage a silent, repetitive gesture (e.g., a dancer holding a broken wing prop).
  • Inspiration: Rachel Whiteread’s Angel of the North (though not directly tied to Klee) uses concrete and steel to create a monumental, sorrowful form.

6. Write an Artist’s Statement That Connects to Klee’s Themes

If you’re exhibiting your work, frame it within Klee’s legacy:

“My series Frozen Moments explores how progress erases memory, much like Klee’s angel. Each piece is a fragment of a story—half-told, half-erased—inviting the viewer to question what we choose to remember.”

  • Use Klee’s own words as inspiration (e.g., “Art does not reproduce the visible; rather, it makes visible.”).
  • Cite historical parallels (e.g., “In an era of algorithmic forgetting, my work asks: What do we lose when we turn away?”).

7. Collaborate with Historians or Survivors for Deeper Meaning

For projects with strong historical themes, partner with:

  • Holocaust educators (e.g., Yad Vashem’s archives).
  • Local Jewish community centers for oral histories.
  • Example: New York’s Fridman Gallery recently collaborated with a Holocaust survivor to create a live-painting event, where the artist interpreted survivor testimonies in real time.

8. Create a "Time Capsule" Installation for Future Viewers

Since Angelus Novus is about history’s cyclical nature, design a work that ages over time:

  • Use archival materials (yellowed paper, faded ink).
  • Embed QR codes linking to historical documents or future artist statements.
  • Inspiration: Klee’s 1935 The Angel of History* (a later version) shows the angel more fragmented, as if history itself is breaking apart.

Common Mistakes When Interpreting Angelus Novus and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1: Reducing the Angel to a "Doomed Figure"

Why it’s wrong: Some viewers see the angel as passive or hopeless, but Klee intended it as active resistance. Solution: Focus on the angel’s outstretched wings—they’re not broken, but caught in the storm, implying agency in suffering.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the Jewish Context

Why it’s wrong: Many discussions treat Angelus Novus as universal modernism, erasing its Jewish roots. Solution: Research Klee’s Jewish friends (e.g., Wassily Kandinsky, Lyonel Feininger) and how they influenced his work.

Mistake 3: Overemphasizing the "Burning Landscape" Without Analysis

Why it’s wrong: The fire is often seen as destruction alone, but it’s also a metaphor for rebirth (like phoenix mythology). Solution: Pair the image with texts on renewal (e.g., Hannah Arendt’s The Origins of Totalitarianism).

Mistake 4: Copying Klee’s Style Without Understanding His Process

Why it’s wrong: Simply mimicking his geometric forms without personal intent makes the work feel hollow. Solution: Study his sketchbooks—he experimented with hundreds of ideas before finalizing a piece.

Mistake 5: Treating the Angel as a "Symbol" Without Emotional Weight

Why it’s wrong: If the angel feels too abstract, it loses impact. Solution: Ground it in personal experience—e.g., paint an angel based on a family story of displacement.

  • A call to remember—the angel’s backward gaze suggests that progress without memory is meaningless.
