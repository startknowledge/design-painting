8 Actionable Strategies to Run a Successful Public Mural Competition

If you’re inspired by Albany’s approach and want to organize a similar project in your city, here are eight proven strategies to ensure success.

1. Define a Clear Vision & Mission

Before inviting artists or the public, ask yourself:

  • What message do we want the mural to convey?
  • Who does it serve? (Residents? Tourists? Youth?)
  • How will it contribute to the community’s goals?

Example: In Detroit, Michigan, the "Detroit 67" mural (2019) was designed to honor the city’s Black cultural legacy. The project was tied to Detroit’s 67th anniversary of its Black-owned business district, making it both historically significant and timely.

How to Apply This:

  • Host community workshops to gather ideas.
  • Partner with local historians, artists, and activists to refine the theme.

2. Cast a Wide Net for Artists

Don’t limit submissions to established names—emerging artists often bring fresh perspectives. Consider:

  • Open calls on platforms like ArtStation, Behance, or local Facebook groups.
  • Collaborations with art schools (students can submit work).
  • Diversity in representation (gender, ethnicity, disability status).

Real-World Mistake & Fix: In Austin, Texas, a 2022 mural competition unintentionally favored white male artists due to unconscious bias in judging criteria. The city later revised selection parameters to include diversity quotas, ensuring a more representative final pool.

3. Make the Voting Process Accessible & Engaging

Public participation drops if the process is too complex. To boost engagement:

  • Use a simple, mobile-friendly voting platform (e.g., Doodle, SurveyMonkey, or a custom website).
  • Offer multiple ways to vote (online, in-person kiosks, social media polls).
  • Provide clear instructions (e.g., "Vote once per day to maximize impact").

Example: The ***"Wall of Respect" mural in Chicago (1967)** was a grassroots effort where Black artists and activists painted directly on a brick wall after police violence. While not a formal competition, it empowered the community to take ownership—a lesson in organic engagement.

4. Educate Voters on the Designs

Many people vote based on aesthetics alone, missing the story behind the art. To deepen understanding:

  • Include artist statements explaining the design’s meaning.
  • Host virtual or in-person tours where artists discuss their work.
  • Use social media to share behind-the-scenes content (sketches, inspirations).

Pro Tip: Albany could create a "Mural Storytime" series on Instagram Stories, where artists break down their designs in 60-second clips—making voting feel more personal and informed.

5. Leverage Local Influencers & Media

Local celebrities, business owners, and media outlets can amplify your reach. Strategies:

  • Partner with influencers who align with your theme (e.g., a local historian for a historical mural).
  • Pitch stories to newspapers, radio, and TV (e.g., "How Albany’s Mural Will Redefine Downtown").
  • Run social media campaigns with user-generated content (e.g., "Tag a friend who’d love this mural!").

Example: When Seattle’s "Floating Mural" project (2021) launched, the city worked with local podcasts to interview artists and featured the mural in a viral TikTok trend, driving thousands of votes.

6. Ensure Transparency & Fair Judging

To avoid controversy or favoritism, make your selection process clear and fair:

  • Publicly announce judges (who they are, their expertise).
  • Share judging criteria (e.g., "Originality: 30%, Community Relevance: 40%, Technical Feasibility: 30%").
  • Allow artists to appeal if they feel a decision was unfair.

Common Mistake: In Baltimore’s 2020 mural competition, political ties were suspected in the final selection, leading to public backlash. The city later reformed its judging panel to include more independent voices.

7. Plan for Long-Term Impact

A mural isn’t just paint on a wall—it’s an investment in community pride. To maximize its lasting effect:

  • Schedule maintenance to prevent vandalism or weather damage.
  • Create an "Adopt-a-Mural" program where businesses sponsor sections.
  • Use the mural as an educational tool (e.g., school field trips, guided tours).

Example: The "Hope Diamond" mural in Denver (2018) became a symbol of resilience after a mass shooting. The city turned it into a memorial space, hosting annual vigils and art workshops tied to its themes.

8. Celebrate the Process & Results

The grand opening is just the beginning. Keep the momentum going with:

  • A public unveiling event (live music, speeches, artist Q&A).
  • A "Mural Walk" tour where visitors learn about the design’s meaning.
  • Social media takeovers where artists or voters share their favorite moments.

Real-World Success: When Los Angeles’ "The Great Wall of Los Angeles" (1982-88) was restored in 2020, the city hosted a 10-day festival with live murals, poetry readings, and historical talks, turning a single project into a cultural event.

Common Mistakes in Public Mural Projects (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned projects can fall flat if key pitfalls aren’t addressed. Here’s what to watch out for:

1. Lack of Clear Goals

Mistake: Starting without a defined purpose (e.g., "We just want a pretty mural"). Solution:

  • Ask: "What problem does this mural solve?" (e.g., revitalizing a blighted area, educating youth, celebrating heritage).
  • Example: Instead of a generic "Albany Pride" mural, the city could tie it to a specific anniversary (e.g., "150 Years of Albany’s Chinese Community").

2. Excluding Diverse Voices

Mistake: Unintentionally favoring mainstream artists over underrepresented groups. Solution:

  • Set diversity quotas in artist submissions.
  • Partner with community organizations (e.g., women’s collectives, LGBTQ+ groups, immigrant associations).
  • Example: San Francisco’s "Oakland Mural Project" (2021) included a Latino artist collective to ensure Chicano cultural representation was central.

3. Poor Communication

Mistake: Vague updates or no transparency in the process. Solution:

  • Use a project management tool (e.g., Trello, Asana) to track progress.
  • Send regular newsletters with behind-the-scenes content.
  • Example: Portland’s "Mural Map" project kept voters updated via weekly Instagram Stories, showing artist sketches, voting stats, and timelines.

4. Underestimating Maintenance Costs

Mistake: Assuming the mural will last forever without planning for upkeep. Solution:

  • Budget for repainting every 5-10 years.
  • Install protective coatings to prevent weather damage.
  • Example: New York’s "The Gates" (2005) required a $1.5M restoration in 2019—showing that even iconic art needs long-term care.

5. Ignoring Local Feedback

Mistake: Rushing the process without listening to community concerns. Solution:

  • Host pop-up voting stations in high-traffic areas.
  • Conduct focus groups with youth, elders, and business owners.
  • Example: Chicago’s "Black Wall Mural" (2020) faced backlash when some residents felt it erased other cultural narratives. The city later added a "Community Advisory Board" to balance perspectives.

6. Overlooking Legal & Permit Hurdles

Mistake: Assuming any wall is fair game without checking ownership or zoning laws. Solution:

  • Work with city planners to identify legal mural sites.
  • Get permits for scaffolding, paint, and public access.
  • Example: A 2019 mural in Miami was demolished after 3 months because the building owner hadn’t given permission—leading to $50K in legal fees.
