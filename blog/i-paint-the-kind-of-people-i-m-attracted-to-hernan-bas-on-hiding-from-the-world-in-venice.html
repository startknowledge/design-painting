"‘I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to’: Hernan Bas’s Raw Venice and the Art of Hiding in Plain Sight"

Introduction: The Allure of the Unseen in Contemporary Art

In an era where digital art dominates gallery spaces and social media feeds, there remains a quiet, almost rebellious charm in the work of artists who choose to hide—not just their creations, but their very selves within them. One such artist is Hernan Bas, whose paintings of Venice’s underbelly and the people who inhabit its shadows have captivated audiences with their raw honesty and emotional depth.

According to a 2023 report by the Art Basel and UBS Global Art Market Report, over 60% of contemporary art buyers now seek out works that explore human vulnerability and existential themes—a shift that aligns perfectly with Bas’s approach. His statement, “I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to,” isn’t just a confession of artistic preference; it’s a declaration of a broader truth about art as a mirror of desire, loneliness, and fleeting connections.

Venice, with its labyrinthine alleys and transient population, has long been a muse for artists who find solace in its anonymity. Bas’s work doesn’t just depict the city—it inhabits it, blending the observer’s gaze with the subject’s unseen life. This post explores how his technique of “hiding in plain sight” can inspire artists, designers, and creatives to craft work that feels alive with unspoken stories.

Why Hernan Bas’s Venice Paintings Resonate: The Psychology Behind the Art

Bas’s work isn’t just about looking at Venice—it’s about listening to it. His paintings often feature figures who seem to be just passing through, their expressions caught mid-conversation, mid-laugh, or mid-sigh. This isn’t mere observation; it’s an immersion into the city’s pulse.

The Science of Unseen Connections

Research in neuroscientific aesthetics (published in Frontiers in Psychology, 2022) suggests that humans are wired to respond more deeply to art that hints at stories rather than spells them out. Bas’s technique—leaving just enough ambiguity—triggers the viewer’s imagination, making the work feel personal.

  • The “Uncanny Valley” Effect: When a subject is almost recognizable but not quite, the brain fills in the gaps with our own memories and desires. Bas’s figures often blur the line between stranger and friend, creating a sense of familiarity in strangeness.
  • The “Third Place” Theory: Sociologist Ray Oldenburg (1989) argued that people form deeper bonds in neutral spaces—cafés, parks, or in this case, Venice’s hidden courtyards. Bas’s paintings capture these fleeting moments of connection.

How Bas’s Work Transcends the “Postcard Venice”

Most artists who paint Venice focus on the Grand Canal, St. Mark’s Basilica, or gondoliers in silk shirts. Bas, however, chooses the back alleys, the laundry hanging from windows, the solitary figure staring into the distance. His work asks:

  • What if the most beautiful stories aren’t the ones we’re told?
  • What if the most compelling characters are the ones we don’t know?

This shift from tourist Venice to Venice as a character itself is what makes his work so compelling.

8 Actionable Strategies to Capture the “Unseen” in Your Own Art

If Bas’s approach intrigues you, here’s how you can apply his philosophy of hiding in plain sight to your own creative practice:

1. Choose the Right “Neutral Space”

Bas doesn’t paint in grand, iconic locations—he finds ordinary places where extraordinary moments unfold.

  • Action Step: Instead of painting a famous landmark, seek out local cafés, bus stops, or quiet parks. The more unremarkable the setting, the more human your subjects will feel.
  • Example: A street musician playing in a rain-soaked plaza (not the one in the tourist brochure) becomes more powerful because it feels real.

2. Focus on the “In-Between” Moments

Bas’s figures are rarely in perfect poses—they’re mid-step, mid-gesture, mid-conversation. These transitional moments feel more alive.

  • Action Step: Instead of painting a subject in a static, posed shot, capture them walking, gesturing, or looking away. Think of it as freezing a story in progress.
  • Example: A person adjusting their hat while walking down a street—this small action tells a story of preparation, distraction, or escape.

3. Use Light to Create Mystery

Bas often plays with contrasting light and shadow, making some figures emerge while others remain in darkness.

  • Action Step: Experiment with chiaroscuro (strong light/dark contrasts) to create depth. A single light source (like a streetlamp or window) can make a subject feel highlighted—as if they’re the only ones seen.
  • Example: A figure standing under a streetlamp while the rest of the alley remains in shadow—this creates a sense of isolation within a crowd.

4. Let the Background Tell a Story

Bas’s backgrounds aren’t just decorative—they’re characters in their own right.

  • Action Step: Instead of a blank sky or a flat horizon, include details that hint at a larger world:
    • A laundry line with clothes fluttering in the wind.
    • A half-closed shop sign with peeling paint.
    • A dog tied to a fence, watching the street.
  • Example: A painting of a woman sitting on a bench with a dog’s leash tangled in the railing—this small detail suggests a story of abandonment, freedom, or companionship.

5. Avoid Over-Explanation in Your Work

Bas’s figures are expressive but not exposed. They don’t stare directly at the viewer; they’re caught in their own world.

  • Action Step: Instead of painting a subject with a direct gaze, give them subtle, indirect eye contact—like looking slightly to the side or down.
  • Example: A person walking past a window, their reflection visible but their face turned away—this creates a sense of privacy and intimacy.

6. Use Color to Evoke Emotion, Not Just Description

Bas’s palette is muted but rich, with colors that feel lived-in rather than perfect.

  • Action Step: Avoid overly saturated colors. Instead, use earth tones, faded hues, and subtle contrasts to create a sense of time passing.
  • Example: A red coat in a sea of grays—this single pop of color draws the eye but doesn’t overpower the scene.

7. Study the “Unseen” in Your Daily Life

Bas’s work comes from observation, not just talent. He pays attention to the small, overlooked details.

  • Action Step: Keep a sketchbook or notes app where you jot down:
    • A stranger’s gesture.
    • The way light hits a wall.
    • A sound you hear in a public space.
  • Example: Noticing how a barista’s hands move when they pour coffee—this could later inspire a painting of routine and connection.

8. Embrace Imperfection as a Storytelling Tool

Bas’s brushstrokes aren’t always perfect—they’re textured, slightly blurred, or uneven. This gives his work a human quality.

  • Action Step: Don’t overwork your details. Leave small imperfections—a smudge, a slightly crooked line, or a background that’s just out of focus.
  • Example: A face that’s almost clear but has a soft blur, making the viewer wonder: Is this memory? Is this a dream?

Real-World Examples: How Bas’s Techniques Play Out in Art

Example 1: The Solitary Figure by the Canal

Imagine a painting of a woman standing at the edge of a canal, her back to the viewer. She’s holding a book, but her gaze is fixed on the water. The light hits her hair just right, casting a golden glow, while the rest of the scene is in shadow.

  • What makes it work?
    • Ambiguity: Is she reading? Waiting? Thinking? The viewer fills in the gaps.
    • Light as a character: The light isn’t just illuminating—it’s highlighting her, making her feel chosen in this moment.
    • The canal as a mirror: The water reflects her slightly, but not perfectly, adding to the sense of fleeting presence.

Example 2: The Hidden Courtyard

A painting of a narrow alleyway leading to a courtyard where a group of people are gathered. The figures are partially obscured by columns or overhanging vines. Some are laughing, others are deep in conversation, but none are looking directly at the viewer.

  • What makes it work?
    • The “fourth wall” effect: The viewer feels like an intruder in a private moment.
    • Layered depth: The columns and vines create a sense of being pulled into the scene.
    • The absence of a clear focal point: The eye wanders, making the viewer participate in the discovery.

Example 3: The Gondolier’s Ghost

A painting of a gondola gliding through the Grand Canal, but the gondolier is not in it. Instead, there’s a single oar resting on the seat, as if he just stepped away for a moment.

  • What makes it work?
    • The implied presence: The oar suggests absence, making the viewer wonder: Where did he go?
    • The canal as a character: The water is active—ripples, reflections, the play of light—while the gondolier is not.
    • A sense of time: The painting feels like a snapshot of a moment that’s already slipping away.

Common Mistakes When Trying to Capture the “Unseen” (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most talented artists can fall into traps when trying to replicate Bas’s approach. Here’s how to avoid them:

Mistake 1: Over-Exposing Your Subjects

What it looks like: Painting figures with direct, intense gazes that feel exposed or judged. Why it fails: Bas’s figures feel private—they’re not performing for the viewer. How to fix it:

  • Use indirect lighting (e.g., side lighting, backlighting).
  • Have subjects look away or down rather than at the viewer.
  • Avoid too much facial detail—let their posture and gestures tell the story.

Mistake 2: Making the Background Too Busy

What it looks like: A crowded street scene where nothing stands out because everything is equally detailed. Why it fails: Bas’s backgrounds are suggestive, not overwhelming. How to fix it:

  • Use selective focus—keep the foreground sharp and the background soft.
  • Include one or two strong details (like a peeling sign or a lone figure) to anchor the scene.
  • Avoid too many people—crowds can feel chaotic rather than lived-in.

Mistake 3: Over-Reliance on “Perfect” Composition

What it looks like: A painting where every element is symmetrical or perfectly aligned. Why it fails: Bas’s work feels organic—like it was discovered rather than constructed. How to fix it:

  • Break the rule of thirds—let your composition feel unbalanced.
  • Include imperfections—a slightly crooked line, a smudge, a background that’s just out of focus.
  • Think of your painting as a snapshot, not a stage set.

Mistake 4: Ignoring the “Unseen” in Your Own Life

What it looks like: Painting the same subjects over and over because you’re not actively observing new moments. Why it fails: Bas’s work feels fresh because he’s always discovering new stories. How to fix it:

  • Change your perspective: Sit in a café and watch people. Stand on a bridge and observe the water.
  • Use technology: Take thousands of photos of ordinary moments—you’ll start noticing patterns.
  • Talk to strangers: People’s stories are goldmines for art.

Mistake 5: Making Your Work Too “Explainable”

What it looks like: A painting where every detail has a clear meaning (e.g., a broken chair = sadness, a red door = passion). Why it fails: Bas’s work hints rather than explains—it lets the viewer feel rather than understand. How to fix it:

  • Leave some ambiguity—not every element needs a backstory.
  • Use symbols sparingly—a single detail (like a hat) can carry multiple meanings.
  • Trust your instincts—if a detail feels right, even if you can’t explain why.
