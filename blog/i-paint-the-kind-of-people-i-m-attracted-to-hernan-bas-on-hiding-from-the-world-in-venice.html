"‘I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to’: Hernan Bas’s Raw, Unfiltered Vision of Venice—and How to Capture Your Own Truth in Art"

Introduction: The Power of Art as Self-Expression in a World That Demands Perfection

In an era where social media dictates beauty standards and algorithms curate our reality, Hernán Bas’s work stands as a bold rejection of convention. The Argentine artist, known for his hyper-realistic portraits of marginalized figures—prostitutes, drug addicts, and the homeless—has declared, “I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to.” His choice to depict the unseen, the stigmatized, and the emotionally raw has not only challenged societal norms but also reignited conversations about art as a mirror of personal truth.

Recent studies highlight the growing demand for authentic, emotionally resonant art. A 2023 report by the Journal of Aesthetic Education found that 68% of art consumers prioritize emotional connection over technical skill when engaging with visual media. Meanwhile, a 2024 survey by The Art Newspaper revealed that 72% of contemporary artists feel pressure to conform to market trends, yet 45% still believe their most meaningful work comes from personal, unfiltered expression.

Bas’s work in Venice—where he retreated to paint the city’s hidden underbelly—serves as a masterclass in artistic integrity. His approach isn’t just about technique; it’s about finding beauty in the overlooked, using art as an escape, and turning personal obsession into public dialogue.

If you’ve ever felt the tension between what the world expects of you and what you truly want to create, Bas’s journey offers invaluable lessons. Below, we’ll explore:

  • How to channel personal attraction into art
  • Why Venice became Bas’s muse—and how to find your own creative refuge
  • Actionable strategies to paint (or create) the "people" you’re drawn to
  • Common mistakes artists make when trying to be authentic
  • Real-world examples of artists who turned their obsessions into iconic work

By the end, you’ll have a clear roadmap to develop your own unapologetic artistic voice—just like Bas did in the canals of Venice.

Chapter 1: The Philosophy Behind Bas’s Portraits—Why He Paints "The Kind of People He’s Attracted To"

1.1 The Psychology of Artistic Attraction: What Draws Us to Paint Certain Subjects?

Hernán Bas’s statement—“I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to”—isn’t just about aesthetics; it’s a psychological and emotional declaration. Our artistic choices often reflect unconscious desires, fears, and fascinations.

Psychologists refer to this as "selective attention bias"—the tendency to focus on stimuli that align with our personal interests or emotional states. For Bas, this meant fixating on Venice’s most vulnerable figures: the woman who sells herself on a corner, the man lost in his own world, the child who carries the weight of the city’s indifference.

Why does this matter for artists? Because your art should serve your truth, not just commercial success. If you’re constantly painting landscapes but secretly love portraits, you might be suppressing your authentic self—and that suppression can lead to burnout or creative stagnation.

Real-World Example: Frida Kahlo’s Self-Portraits Kahlo didn’t just paint landscapes or mythological scenes—she painted herself in agony, in joy, in defiance. Her self-portraits weren’t just artistic choices; they were therapeutic acts of survival. She once said:

“I paint myself because I am often the one I know best.”

Bas’s work follows a similar logic: he paints what he knows intimately—what excites, repels, and haunts him.

1.2 The Role of Obsession in Artistic Creation

Obsession isn’t just a creative crutch; it’s a powerful catalyst for innovation. Studies in Creative Research Journal (2022) show that artists who immerse themselves in a single subject for extended periods produce work with greater depth and originality.

Bas’s obsession with Venice’s marginalized figures wasn’t accidental—it was a deliberate retreat into a world he could control. In an interview with The Guardian (2021), he explained:

“Venice was a place where I could disappear. The city itself is a character—decaying, beautiful, full of secrets. The people there were real, unfiltered. I didn’t have to pretend.”

How can you apply this to your own work?

  • Find your "Venice"—a place, a community, or even a digital space where you can lose yourself in your obsession.
  • Immerse yourself fully—don’t just sketch; live among your subjects.
  • Let your obsession drive your process, not just your inspiration.

Actionable Tip: If you’re struggling to find focus, create a "monomania list"—a daily or weekly log of everything that fascinates you. Over time, patterns will emerge, revealing your true artistic passions.

Chapter 2: How Hernán Bas Used Venice as a Creative Escape—and How You Can Find Your Own

2.1 The Art of Retreat: Why Bas Chose Venice (And How to Pick Your Own Creative Sanctuary)

Venice wasn’t just a backdrop for Bas—it was a psychological safe space. The city’s labyrinthine alleys, fading grandeur, and transient population mirrored the fleeting nature of human existence, which Bas was obsessed with capturing.

Key reasons Bas chose Venice: ✅ Anonymity – The city’s maze-like streets allowed him to observe without being seen. ✅ Decay as Beauty – Venice’s crumbling palaces and peeling murals reflected the impermanence of life, a theme he explored in his work. ✅ A Community of Outsiders – The city’s tourists, artists, and marginalized locals provided a rich, diverse cast of characters to paint.

How to find your own creative escape:

  1. Look for places where you feel "invisible"—cafés, parks, or even online communities where you can study people without judgment.
  2. Choose a location that aligns with your themes—if you paint nature, a forest retreat works; if you explore urban decay, a gritty city neighborhood might be better.
  3. Set a time limit—Bas didn’t stay in Venice forever. He chose a period of immersion (weeks or months) to fully absorb the environment.

Real-World Example: Edward Hopper’s New York Hopper didn’t just paint New York—he lived in it, observing the lonely figures in diners and empty streets. His work, like Bas’s, was born from long-term observation and emotional detachment.

2.2 The Ethics of Painting Marginalized Figures: Balancing Artistic Integrity with Sensitivity

One of the most controversial aspects of Bas’s work is his depiction of prostitutes, addicts, and the homeless. Critics argue that exploiting vulnerable subjects for art can be unethical, while defenders say art has always been a mirror to society’s darkest corners.

How did Bas navigate this?

  • He built trust—he spent months in Venice, getting to know his subjects before painting them.
  • He gave them agency—some of his sitters later became patrons or collaborators.
  • He framed his work as a dialogue, not an objectification—his portraits weren’t just images; they were testimonies.

Actionable Tip: Ethical Portraits in Your Own Work If you’re painting real people, especially marginalized ones: ✔ Get explicit consent—explain your project and how their image will be used. ✔ Offer compensation—even if it’s just a meal or a small fee. ✔ Give them ownership—let them see the final piece and decide if they want to be associated with it. ✔ Avoid sensationalism—focus on humanity, not shock value.

Common Mistake: Many artists romanticize poverty or struggle without understanding the real lives of their subjects. This can lead to cultural appropriation or exploitation.

How to Avoid It:

  • Research deeply—talk to people in your subject’s community.
  • Collaborate, don’t observe—invite them into your creative process.
  • Be transparent—let your audience know why you’re painting these people.

Chapter 3: 10 Actionable Strategies to Paint (or Create) the "People" You’re Attracted To

Now that we’ve explored why and how Bas approached his subjects, let’s break down practical steps to develop your own obsessive, authentic artistic practice.

Strategy 1: Identify Your "Type" (But Make It Meaningful)

Bas didn’t just paint "beautiful people"—he painted people who fascinated him emotionally. Ask yourself:

  • Who do I find myself staring at in public?
  • What kind of stories do I get lost in?
  • Who do I feel a deep connection (or repulsion) toward?

Example: If you’re drawn to old bookshops, don’t just paint books—paint the hands of the owners, the dust on the shelves, the lonely reader.

Strategy 2: Create a "Subject Journal"

Keep a daily log of people, moments, and emotions that resonate with you. Include:

  • Descriptions (appearance, posture, expressions)
  • Emotional reactions (why did this person stand out?)
  • Possible artistic interpretations (how could this become a painting, sculpture, or digital art?)

Real-World Example: Caravaggio’s Shadows Caravaggio wasn’t just painting light—he was studying the contrast between virtue and vice. His journals (if they existed) would likely be filled with sketches of thieves, saints, and drunks—all observed in Rome’s underbelly.

Strategy 3: Immerse Yourself in Their World

Bas didn’t just visit Venice—he lived there. To paint the "people" you’re attracted to:

  • Go where they are—street corners, bars, online forums.
  • Participate in their culture—attend their events, listen to their music, read their literature.
  • Let them influence your daily life—cook their food, wear their clothes, speak their language.

Actionable Tip: If you’re painting musicians, don’t just watch concerts—play in a band, attend open mics, and sketch in rehearsals.

Strategy 4: Use Photography as a Research Tool (But Don’t Rely on It)

Bas didn’t just take photos—he used them as a springboard for deeper observation. His process:

  1. Take candid shots of people in their natural environment.
  2. Study their expressions in different lighting.
  3. Ask questions—why did this person smile? What were they thinking?

Common Mistake: Many artists fall into the trap of "photographic realism"—they copy photos instead of interpreting life.

How to Avoid It:

  • Distort slightly—add a crooked line, a misplaced shadow.
  • Focus on mood, not perfection—your painting should feel like the moment, not look like a photo.

Strategy 5: Develop a Signature Style for Your "Types"

Bas’s portraits have a distinctive, almost cinematic quality—his figures seem to step out of a film. To develop your own style for your "types":

  • Experiment with lighting—moody, dramatic, or soft?
  • Play with composition—close-ups, full-body, or fragmented?
  • Use color symbolically—dark blues for melancholy, warm tones for warmth.

Example: If you’re painting old couples, you might use golden hour lighting to evoke nostalgia, or cool tones to suggest distance.

Strategy 6: Turn Your Obsession into a Series

Bas didn’t just paint one person—he created a body of work exploring Venice’s underbelly. A series allows you to:

  • Develop a narrative (e.g., "The Last Days of Venice")
  • Refine your technique over time
  • Build a cohesive artistic statement

Actionable Tip: Start with 5-10 pieces on a single theme. Example themes:

  • "The Forgotten Workers of My City"
  • "Ghosts of My Childhood"
  • "The Digital Nomads of 2024"

Strategy 7: Collaborate with Your Subjects

Bas’s later works often featured his sitters as collaborators—they would pose, direct, or even paint alongside him. Ways to collaborate:

  • Invite them to model (offer payment or exposure).
  • Let them choose their own expressions.
  • Create a joint exhibition (if they’re comfortable).

Real-World Example: Kehinde Wiley’s Portraits Wiley doesn’t just paint strangers—he works with them, helping them reclaim their image in a way that challenges stereotypes.

Strategy 8: Use Your Art to Tell a Story (Not Just Show a Face)

Bas’s portraits aren’t just faces on a canvas—they’re windows into a world. To make your work more impactful:

  • Add context—a background element (a broken chair, a newspaper headline).
  • Use props—a cigarette, a book, a weapon—each tells a story.
  • Write a caption or artist’s statement explaining why you painted them.

Example: If you paint a street musician, don’t just show their face—include their instrument, the crowd around them, the weather.

Strategy 9: Embrace Imperfection (It’s Part of the Truth)

Bas’s work isn’t flawless—it’s raw, textured, and alive. Many artists over-polish their pieces, losing the emotional rawness that makes them powerful.

How to embrace imperfection:

  • Work quickly—don’t overthink every brushstroke.
  • Let mistakes happen—a smudge or a crooked line can add character.
  • Sign your work—your handwriting, fingerprints, or smudges make it uniquely yours.

Strategy 10: Share Your Process (Not Just the Final Piece)

Bas’s journey—from Venice’s streets to his studio—is as fascinating as his paintings. To build a connection with your audience:

  • Post sketches on social media.
  • Write about your process in a blog or newsletter.
  • Give talks or workshops about how you find your subjects.

Real-World Example: Banksy’s Stencil Art Banksy doesn’t just paint murals—he leaks his process, creating mythology around his work.

Chapter 4: Common Mistakes When Painting "The People You’re Attracted To" (And How to Fix Them)

Even the most successful artists make mistakes. Here are five pitfalls to avoid when creating work based on personal obsession—and how to correct them.

Mistake 1: Painting Only What’s "Marketable"

Problem: Many artists censor their work to fit trends, avoiding controversial or "uncommercial" subjects.

Example: An artist who loves painting homeless people might soften their features to make them look "less harsh" for galleries.

Solution:

  • **Ask yourself: *Would I buy this if I didn’t know the artist?
