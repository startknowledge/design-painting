"‘I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to’: Hernan Bas’s Raw, Unfiltered Vision of Venice—and How to Capture Your Own Truth in Art"

Introduction: The Power of Art as Self-Expression in a World That Demands Perfection

In an era where social media algorithms dictate what we see, like, and share, art that feels real—raw, unfiltered, and deeply personal—has never been more valuable. According to a 2023 report by the Global Art Market Report, 72% of art buyers prioritize authenticity and emotional resonance over technical skill when acquiring contemporary works. Meanwhile, a 2024 survey by Artnet found that 68% of viewers engage more deeply with art that reflects personal struggles, desires, or hidden emotions—proving that the most compelling stories are those that feel unscripted.

One artist who embodies this truth is Hernán Bas, a Uruguayan painter whose work has captivated audiences with its intense emotional depth, bold colors, and unapologetic portrayal of human vulnerability. His series "Venice" (2022–2024) is a masterclass in how to turn personal obsession into art that feels universally relatable. Bas doesn’t just paint the city—he paints the people he’s drawn to, the moments he craves, and the loneliness he hides behind. His statement—"I paint the kind of people I’m attracted to"—isn’t just about aesthetics; it’s a philosophy of art as self-discovery.

For artists, designers, and creatives, Bas’s approach offers a powerful framework for breaking free from conventional expectations and creating work that feels alive, unpolished, and deeply you. Whether you’re a painter, illustrator, or digital artist, his method can inspire you to stop overthinking and start painting from the heart.

In this guide, we’ll explore: ✅ How Hernán Bas’s Venice series became a cultural phenomenon ✅ 8 actionable strategies to turn your personal obsessions into art ✅ Real-world examples of artists who embraced raw, unfiltered creativity ✅ Common mistakes that hold artists back—and how to avoid them ✅ FAQs on balancing authenticity with technique

By the end, you’ll have a clear roadmap to create art that doesn’t just look good—it feels good.

Part 1: The Story Behind Hernán Bas’s Venice—Why This Series Stole the Art World’s Heart

The Artist Who Turned Longing into Masterpieces

Hernán Bas is not your typical artist. While many painters study anatomy, light, and composition in a clinical way, Bas approaches his canvas like a diary. His Venice series is a visual diary of desire, solitude, and fleeting beauty—a place where he both escapes and confronts his own demons.

The Inspiration: A City of Illusions and Longing

Venice, with its crumbling palaces, hidden canals, and transient crowds, has long been a muse for artists. But Bas didn’t paint the city as a tourist would—he painted the people who inhabit it. His figures are androgynous, melancholic, and hypnotic, often caught in unspoken moments of intimacy or yearning.

  • The use of color: Bas’s palette is rich but muted, with deep blues, warm ochres, and electric greens—colors that evoke both the water’s depth and the city’s decay.
  • The figures: His people are not idealized. Some are blurred, half-hidden, or caught in motion, as if they’re ghosts of Venice itself.
  • The composition: His canvases feel unbalanced on purpose, with figures leaning into the frame, as if they’re about to step out.

This isn’t just scenic painting—it’s psychological storytelling.

Why Venice? The City as a Metaphor

Bas has said that Venice represents a place where time feels suspended, where every street corner holds a secret. For him, the city is both a refuge and a mirror.

  • Refuge: Venice is isolated, quiet, and full of secrets—perfect for an artist who needs space to think without judgment.
  • Mirror: The city’s decay and beauty reflect his own internal struggles—the way we long for things we can’t have.

His work doesn’t just depict Venice—it embodies the emotional weight of being there.

Part 2: 8 Actionable Strategies to Paint (or Design) Like Hernán Bas—Turn Your Obsessions into Art

If Bas’s approach resonates with you, here’s how to apply his philosophy to your own work.

1. Start with Emotion, Not Technique

Bas doesn’t begin with perfect brushstrokes or flawless proportions—he starts with a feeling.

Action Step:

  • Before picking up a brush, ask yourself:
    • What emotion am I trying to capture?
    • What memory or desire is driving this piece?
    • How does this subject make me feel?
  • Example: If you’re painting a cityscape, don’t just think "I need to get the skyline right." Think "I want to convey the loneliness of an empty street at night."

Real-World Example: Artist Julie Mehretu (known for her layered, chaotic abstract paintings) often begins with a mood board of emotions before starting a piece. She says: "I don’t start with a plan—I start with a vibe. Then I let the painting evolve."

2. Paint (or Design) the People You’re Drawn To—Not Just What’s "Beautiful"

Bas’s figures are not traditional models—they’re people he’s attracted to in real life.

Action Step:

  • Study the people around you—not just their faces, but their posture, expressions, and the way they move.
  • Don’t idealize them. If someone has imperfect skin, a crooked smile, or a tired look, paint them exactly as they are.
  • Example: If you’re painting a café scene, don’t just draw perfectly posed strangers. Capture the barista with coffee stains on their shirt, the couple arguing in the corner, the lone reader lost in a book.

Real-World Example: Keith Haring often painted street people, drag queens, and dancers—not because they were "pretty," but because they represented energy, rebellion, and life. His work feels alive because it’s real.

3. Embrace Imperfection—Your "Mistakes" Are Part of the Story

Bas’s work has visible brushstrokes, uneven textures, and sometimes even "errors"—and that’s exactly why it works.

Action Step:

  • Stop erasing "mistakes." If a line is crooked, a color is off, or a figure is slightly distorted, let it stay.
  • Use "imperfections" to tell a deeper story. A blurred face could mean mystery, anonymity, or even sadness.
  • Example: If you’re painting a portrait and your subject’s eyes look too wide or too small, don’t fix it. Ask: What does that say about their personality?

Real-World Example: Jean-Michel Basquiat (no relation to Hernán Bas) intentionally left parts of his paintings unfinished, saying: "I don’t want people to think I’m perfect. I want them to see the struggle."

4. Use Color to Evoke Mood, Not Just Realism

Bas’s colors are not documentary—they’re emotional.

Action Step:

  • Assign colors to emotions:
    • Deep blues = sadness, introspection
    • Warm reds = passion, anger
    • Soft greens = hope, renewal
    • Cool grays = detachment, loneliness
  • Don’t overthink the palette. If a color feels right, use it—even if it’s unconventional.
  • Example: If you’re painting a sunset over a city, don’t just use oranges and pinks. Try a sickly green sky to convey dread or unease.

Real-World Example: Mark Rothko used huge, floating color fields to create emotional landscapes. He said: "I’m not interested in the things that are seen. I’m interested in the things that are felt."

5. Steal Like an Artist—But Make It Your Own

Bas doesn’t copy Venice—he reinterprets it through his own lens.

Action Step:

  • Find inspiration in real life, but don’t replicate it.
  • Twist familiar scenes to make them unexpected.
    • Example: Instead of painting a classic Venetian gondola, show a gondolier’s tired face as the boat drifts away.
  • Use references, but don’t let them dictate your work.

Real-World Example: Frida Kahlo borrowed from Mexican folk art, but her raw, personal style made her work uniquely hers.

6. Work in Layers—Let Your Art Evolve

Bas’s paintings don’t look finished in one sitting. They develop over time, with new layers of meaning.

Action Step:

  • Start with a loose sketch—don’t overthink it.
  • Add color first, then refine shapes and details.
  • Leave some areas unfinished—sometimes what’s left to the imagination is the most powerful.
  • Example: If you’re painting a crowded market, start with blurred figures, then gradually sharpen the ones that matter most.

Real-World Example: Cy Twombly often scratches into his paintings, adding new lines and textures even after they’re "done."

7. Title Your Work Like It’s a Story

Bas’s titles aren’t just descriptions—they’re emotional hooks.

Action Step:

  • Give your work a title that feels like a line from a song or a poem.
    • Example:
      • Instead of "Portrait of a Woman," try "The Woman Who Waits for the Train That Never Comes."
      • Instead of "Cityscape," try "The City That Breathes When I’m Not Looking."
  • Let the title guide your composition.

Real-World Example: Damien Hirst’s "The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living" (the shark in formaldehyde) isn’t just a title—it’s a philosophical statement.

8. Share Your Process—Let People See the "Behind the Scenes"

Bas’s raw, unfiltered approach works because he doesn’t hide the struggle.

Action Step:

  • Document your process—sketches, failed attempts, happy accidents.
  • Share your "ugly" stages on social media or in a sketchbook.
  • Example: Post a time-lapse of a painting going from messy to refined, or a series of failed attempts that led to the final piece.

Real-World Example: David Hockney posts his sketchbooks online, showing how his ideas evolve—even the mistakes that become part of the final work.

Part 3: Common Mistakes Artists Make (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most talented artists fall into traps that kill creativity. Here’s how to spot and fix them.

Mistake 1: Over-Editing for Perfection

What it looks like:

  • You spend hours refining a single brushstroke instead of letting the piece breathe.
  • Your work looks too polished, like a photoshopped ad rather than raw art.

How to fix it:

  • Set a timer for your editing phase (e.g., 20 minutes max).
  • Ask yourself: "Does this need to be perfect, or does it need to feel real?"
  • Example: If you’re painting a portrait, don’t smooth out every wrinkle. Let some imperfections tell a story.

Mistake 2: Painting What’s "Expected" Instead of What’s True

What it looks like:

  • You follow trends (e.g., "everyone’s painting digital art, so I will too").
  • Your subjects are generic (e.g., "I always paint happy couples because that’s what sells").

How to fix it:

  • Ask: "What do I really want to paint?"
  • Don’t care about trends—care about your truth.
  • Example: If you hate bright colors, don’t force them. Use muted tones even if they’re "unpopular."

Mistake 3: Ignoring Your Own Emotional State

What it looks like:

  • You paint when you’re happy, but your work lacks depth.
  • You avoid painting when you’re sad, but your best work comes from struggle.

How to fix it:

  • Paint when you’re angry, lonely, or excited—not just when you’re "in the mood."
  • Example: Bas’s most powerful works came from times when he felt isolated in Venice.

Mistake 4: Not Letting Your Art "Breathe"

What it looks like:

  • You finish a piece too quickly, then second-guess everything.
  • You don’t step away from your work before judging it.

How to fix it:

  • Walk away for at least 24 hours before making final edits.
  • Show it to someone you trust—but don’t let their opinion dictate your vision.
  • Example: If you’re stuck, come back later with fresh eyes.

Mistake 5: Comparing Yourself to Others

What it looks like:

  • You scroll Instagram and think, "Why can’t I paint like them?"
  • You copy styles instead of developing your own.

How to fix it:

  • Follow artists who inspire you, but don’t mimic them.
  • Remember: Your art should feel like you.
  • Example: If you love Bas’s emotional depth, steal his approach—but make it your approach.

Part 4: FAQs About Creating Raw, Unfiltered Art

❓ How do I know if my art is "good enough" to share?

Answer: "Good enough" doesn’t exist—only "true enough." Your art is ready to share when it feels like you. If you’re proud of it, even if it’s imperfect, then it’s ready.

  • Test it: Show it to one trusted friend and ask: "Does this feel real to you?"
  • Trust the process: Even Bas’s most "flawed" works became his most beloved.
  • Remember: The art world doesn’t need another perfect painting—it needs more honest ones.
