Drawings Dominate at a Chicago de Kooning Exhibit: 10 Key Takeaways for Artists & Collectors

By Design-Painting | Last Updated: May 20, 2024 | 35 Minutes Read

Why de Kooning's Drawings Are Redefining Modern Art in Chicago

In the heart of Chicago, where the skyline meets the artistic soul of the Midwest, the Art Institute of Chicago hosted a landmark exhibit in 2023: *Willem de Kooning: Drawings*. This wasn’t just another retrospective—it was a revelation. For the first time in decades, the public could witness the raw, unfiltered creative process of one of the 20th century’s most influential abstract expressionists. With over **120,000 visitors** (per Art Institute records) and critical acclaim, the exhibit proved that de Kooning’s drawings were just as revolutionary as his paintings.

But why does this matter to you? Whether you're an artist, collector, or simply a lover of modern art, de Kooning’s work offers **timeless lessons** on creativity, technique, and the power of imperfection. According to a 2022 survey by the College Art Association, **78% of contemporary artists** cite abstract expressionism as a major influence on their practice. Yet, few explore the drawings that fueled de Kooning’s genius—works that were often discarded or overlooked in favor of his larger canvases.

This post dives deep into the Chicago exhibit, breaking down **10 actionable strategies** inspired by de Kooning’s drawings. We’ll explore how his methods can transform your own artistic approach, from layering techniques to embracing 'ugly' phases of your work. By the end, you’ll understand not just what made the exhibit a sensation, but how to apply its lessons to your creative practice.

The Chicago Exhibit: A Closer Look at de Kooning’s Hidden Mastery

The *Willem de Kooning: Drawings* exhibit at the Art Institute of Chicago featured **150 works**, spanning from the 1930s to the 1980s. Unlike his paintings—often bold, aggressive, and large-scale—his drawings were **intimate, layered, and deeply personal**. The exhibit included:

  • Sketchbooks and preparatory studies for his iconic *Woman* series, revealing how his ideas evolved from chaotic scribbles to finished canvases.
  • Charcoal and ink works that pushed the boundaries of line and texture, often blending abstraction with figuration.
  • Collage elements incorporated into some drawings, showing his experimental spirit.
  • Rare early works from the 1930s and 1940s, demonstrating his shift from realism to abstraction.

One standout piece was *Study for 'Woman—O' (1952), a charcoal and pastel drawing that served as the foundation for his 1952–53 painting of the same name. The exhibit highlighted how de Kooning’s drawings were **not just preliminary sketches** but complete works in their own right. His process was iterative—he would return to the same subject repeatedly, refining and reimagining it.

According to Dr. Sarah Hamill, curator of the exhibit and the Art Institute’s curator of modern art, "De Kooning’s drawings challenge the idea that abstract expressionism was purely about spontaneity. They show a deep engagement with structure, even as he was breaking rules."

10 Actionable Strategies from de Kooning’s Drawings

De Kooning’s drawings offer a masterclass in artistic freedom and discipline. Here are **10 strategies** you can apply to your own work:

1. Embrace 'Controlled Spontaneity'

De Kooning’s drawings often began with **loose, gestural marks** that later became structured compositions. His method was to let go first, then refine. Try this:

  • Start with a fast, uninhibited sketch—don’t worry about accuracy.
  • Step back and observe where your marks naturally lead.
  • Use a second layer (e.g., ink or charcoal) to define shapes and relationships.

Example: In *Study for 'Woman—Green' (1955)*, de Kooning’s initial lines were jagged and uneven, but the final drawing’s composition emerged from those organic forms.

2. Layer Like a Pro

De Kooning rarely worked in a single pass. His drawings often featured **multiple layers of charcoal, ink, and even collage**. This created depth and complexity.

  • Begin with a light underdrawing (e.g., pencil or pastel).
  • Add darker layers (charcoal or ink) to build contrast.
  • Experiment with erasing and reworking—de Kooning often erased lines to reveal hidden forms.

Example: *Untitled (1948)* combines scraped charcoal with ink washes, creating a textured surface that feels almost three-dimensional.

3. Don’t Fear the 'Ugly' Phase

De Kooning’s sketchbooks are filled with messy, unfinished works that never made it to the final canvas. His philosophy? Every mark matters.

  • Allow yourself to make intentionally imperfect marks.
  • Use these phases to explore alternative ideas.
  • Remember: What looks like a mistake today might become a key element tomorrow.

Example: In his 1950 sketchbook, de Kooning scribbled a series of distorted faces that later evolved into the *Woman* series. The "failed" drawings were just as important as the successful ones.

4. Study the Figure, But Break the Rules

While de Kooning’s drawings often depicted figures, they were never realistic. His figures were exaggerated, distorted, and emotionally charged.

  • Start with a basic figure study (e.g., a quick stick figure).
  • Distort proportions—elongate limbs, flatten faces.
  • Use expressive lines to convey emotion rather than accuracy.

Example: *Study for 'Woman—Red' (1953)* features a face with a gaping mouth and exaggerated eyes, yet it feels intimate rather than grotesque.

5. Use Negative Space Strategically

De Kooning’s drawings often relied on negative space to create tension. He would leave areas blank or use subtle contrasts to guide the viewer’s eye.

  • Identify key focal points in your composition.
  • Use negative space to draw attention away from distractions.
  • Experiment with asymmetrical balance—de Kooning’s drawings rarely felt symmetrical.

Example: In *Untitled (1949)*, the empty spaces between the figures create a sense of movement and energy.

6. Incorporate Collage Elements

De Kooning occasionally used collage in his drawings, adding texture and depth. This was rare in his time but became a hallmark of his later work.

  • Tear or cut paper scraps to add texture.
  • Use glue or paste to embed found materials (e.g., fabric, newspaper).
  • Combine with traditional media (e.g., charcoal + collage).

Example: *Study for 'Woman—Pink' (1954)* includes a collaged element—a piece of torn paper that adds an unexpected texture to the composition.

7. Work in Series

De Kooning’s most famous works—like the *Woman* series—were developed through repetition and variation. He would return to the same subject over months or years, refining it.

  • Choose a single subject (e.g., a face, landscape, or abstract form).
  • Create multiple versions, changing one element at a time.
  • Study the evolution of your ideas—what works? What doesn’t?

Example: The *Woman* series spans over a decade, with each version offering a new perspective on the same theme.
