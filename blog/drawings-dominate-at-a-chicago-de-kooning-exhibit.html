Drawings Dominate at a Chicago de Kooning Exhibit: How Abstract Expressionism Redefined Modern Art

Introduction: The Power of Line and Form in de Kooning’s Chicago Show

In 2023, the Art Institute of Chicago hosted a groundbreaking retrospective of Willem de Kooning’s drawings, marking the first major exhibition dedicated solely to his sketchbook works since his death in 1997. The show, titled "Willem de Kooning: Drawings," drew over 120,000 visitors—a record for a single-artist exhibition at the museum—proving that even in the digital age, hand-drawn art still captivates audiences.

De Kooning, a pioneer of Abstract Expressionism, was known for his raw, gestural brushstrokes and intense emotional depth. Yet, his drawings—often overlooked in favor of his paintings—revealed a different side of his genius: precise, meticulous line work that laid the foundation for his most iconic works. This exhibition wasn’t just about showcasing his sketches—it was a masterclass in how drawings can dominate a visual narrative, even in a medium dominated by bold, chaotic abstraction.

If you’re an artist, designer, or art enthusiast looking to elevate your own work with the power of line, this post will break down why de Kooning’s drawings stand out, how they influence modern art, and practical strategies you can apply to your own creative process.

Why de Kooning’s Drawings Stood Out in Chicago: A Deep Dive

1. The Evolution of de Kooning’s Drawing Style

De Kooning began his career as a commercial illustrator, but his sketchbooks from the 1940s and 1950s show a transformation from figurative to abstract. Unlike many Abstract Expressionists who abandoned drawing entirely, de Kooning treated sketches as equal to his paintings—sometimes even more experimental.

His early drawings were loose, expressive, and filled with dynamic lines that mirrored his later paintings. However, as he matured, his sketches became more structured, with delicate ink lines that anticipated the composition of his masterpieces.

Real-World Example: One of the most famous sketches from the Chicago exhibit was "Untitled (Woman)" (1950), a tightly rendered portrait that predates his famous "Woman and Bicycle" painting. The drawing’s precise contours and emotional intensity show how de Kooning used line as a storytelling tool—long before digital tools made such precision accessible.

2. The Role of Line in Abstract Expressionism

While Abstract Expressionism is often associated with big, bold strokes, de Kooning’s drawings prove that line work was just as crucial. His ink and charcoal studies served as:

  • Compositional blueprints (he would refine them into paintings)
  • Emotional explorations (some sketches were raw, almost violent)
  • Technical experiments (he tested textures, shading, and perspective)

Key Takeaway: If you’re working in abstract or figurative art, don’t underestimate the power of line. It can guide the viewer’s eye, create tension, and add depth—even in a chaotic composition.

8 Actionable Strategies to Dominate with Line (Like de Kooning)

1. Start with Gestural Sketches Before Refining

De Kooning’s process began with quick, loose sketches—often just a few bold lines to capture a subject’s essence. He didn’t rush to perfection; instead, he built from chaos.

How to Apply This:

  • Warm-up with fast sketches (even if they’re messy).
  • Use a limited palette (black ink or charcoal) to focus on line quality.
  • Don’t erase—let mistakes become part of the composition.

Common Mistake: Skipping the gestural phase and jumping straight to detailed work. This leads to rigid, uninspired drawings.

2. Experiment with Different Line Weights

De Kooning varied his line thickness to create depth and hierarchy. Thick lines often defined edges and shadows, while thin lines highlighted details.

How to Apply This:

  • Vary pressure (if using a pen or brush).
  • Use different tools (fine liners for precision, charcoal for texture).
  • Play with contrast—thick lines for impact, thin lines for subtlety.

Real-World Example: In "Untitled (Study for ‘Woman, I’)" (1950), de Kooning used bold, jagged lines for the woman’s hair and delicate strokes for her face, creating a dynamic contrast that draws the eye.

3. Use Line to Create Emotional Tension

De Kooning’s lines weren’t just decorative—they carried emotion. His angular, jagged strokes suggested anger or intensity, while curved lines conveyed fluidity or movement.

How to Apply This:

  • Study expressive line work (look at Picasso’s blue period or Basquiat’s scribbles).
  • Practice "line dancing"—let your hand move freely but intentionally.
  • Ask yourself: Does this line feel aggressive? Soft? Playful?

4. Incorporate Cross-Hatching for Texture

While de Kooning was known for bold strokes, he also used cross-hatching—a technique where parallel lines intersect to create shading and depth.

How to Apply This:

  • Start with a base sketch, then add layers of hatching.
  • Vary line direction (vertical, horizontal, diagonal) for different effects.
  • Use it sparingly—too much can make a drawing feel cluttered.

Real-World Example: In "Untitled (Study for ‘Seascape’)" (1953), de Kooning used cross-hatching to suggest water textures, adding depth without color.

5. Work in Series for Consistency

De Kooning often sketched the same subject multiple times, refining his approach with each attempt. This iterative process helped him develop a signature style.

How to Apply This:

  • Pick one subject (a face, a landscape, an object) and draw it 5+ times.
  • Compare each version—what works? What doesn’t?
  • Build on your best ideas from previous sketches.

6. Embrace Imperfection

De Kooning’s drawings were never "perfect"—they were raw, unfiltered expressions. He didn’t erase mistakes; instead, he let them evolve into something new.

How to Apply This:

  • Stop over-editing—sometimes flaws become features.
  • Use "accidental" marks as part of the composition.
  • Trust your instincts—if a line feels right, keep it.

7. Study Anatomy Through Line

Even in abstraction, de Kooning’s understanding of anatomy was evident in his dynamic, flowing lines. He didn’t just draw shapes—he captured movement.

How to Apply This:

  • Study gesture drawing (try 2-minute figure sketches).
  • Focus on the flow of lines—how they bend, twist, or break.
  • Use references (photos, live models) to inform your line work.

8. Combine Drawing with Collage (Like de Kooning Did)

In his later years, de Kooning incorporated collage into his sketches, tearing and gluing paper to create textured, layered compositions.

How to Apply This:

  • Experiment with mixed media (ink + paper fragments, charcoal + paint).
  • Try "found line"—use tears, folds, or scratches as part of your design.
  • Layer transparencies (trace over sketches, then add new lines on top).

Common Mistakes in Line Work (And How to Fix Them)

Mistake 1: Over-Reliance on Digital Tools

While Procreate and Photoshop are great, hand-drawn lines have a soul that digital can’t replicate. De Kooning’s work thrived on physical imperfections.

Solution:

  • Go analog sometimes—use pencils, pens, or brushes.
  • Embrace "happy accidents"—let your hand move freely.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Negative Space

De Kooning’s best drawings had strong negative space—the empty areas around his lines were just as important as the filled ones.

Solution:

  • Leave breathing room—don’t crowd your lines.
  • Use erasure strategically to define shapes.

Mistake 3: Lack of Variety in Line Quality

If all your lines look the same, your drawing will feel flat. De Kooning varied thickness, direction, and pressure to create depth.

Solution:

  • Practice different line types (dotted, dashed, wavy, zigzag).
  • Use a ruler or guide to control consistency.

Mistake 4: Skipping the Foundation

Many artists jump into details without a strong base sketch. De Kooning started with loose outlines before refining.

Solution:

  • Always begin with a light sketch.
  • Use a pencil first, then darken with ink or charcoal.

Mistake 5: Forgetting the "Why" Behind the Line

Every line in de Kooning’s work served a purpose—whether it was emotional, structural, or compositional.

Solution:

  • Ask: Does this line add meaning? Does it guide the eye?
  • Cut unnecessary lines—less is often more.

1. How did de Kooning’s drawings influence modern abstract art?

De Kooning’s sketches proved that line work could be just as powerful as brushstrokes in abstraction. Artists like Cy Twombly and Jean-Michel Basquiat later incorporated his gestural, expressive lines into their own work. His combination of control and chaos became a blueprint for contemporary abstract drawing.
