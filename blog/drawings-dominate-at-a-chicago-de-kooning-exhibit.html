Drawings Dominate at a Chicago de Kooning Exhibit: How Abstract Expressionism Redefined Modern Art

Introduction: The Resurgence of de Kooning’s Drawings in Chicago

The art world has long celebrated Willem de Kooning as one of the most influential figures of Abstract Expressionism, a movement that shook the foundations of modern art in the mid-20th century. While his bold, gestural paintings—like Woman (1950–52) and Excavation (1950)—are iconic, his drawings have recently taken center stage in a groundbreaking exhibit at the Art Institute of Chicago. Titled "Willem de Kooning: Drawings," this retrospective (2023–2024) has drawn record-breaking attendance, with over 120,000 visitors in its first six months—a 30% increase compared to the institute’s average annual turnout (Art Institute of Chicago Annual Report, 2023).

This exhibit isn’t just a celebration of de Kooning’s technical mastery; it’s a revelation of how his sketches, studies, and preparatory works evolved alongside his paintings, offering a raw, unfiltered glimpse into his creative process. For artists, designers, and art enthusiasts, this exhibition serves as a masterclass in abstraction, spontaneity, and the power of line.

If you’ve ever wondered how to harness spontaneity in your own work, why gestural drawing matters in modern art, or how to balance control and chaos in your compositions, de Kooning’s drawings provide unparalleled insights. Below, we’ll break down the key takeaways from this exhibit, explore actionable strategies inspired by his techniques, and discuss common mistakes to avoid when experimenting with abstract expressionist drawing.

Why de Kooning’s Drawings Are a Game-Changer for Artists

Before diving into strategies, it’s essential to understand why these drawings are making such an impact.

1. The Shift from Painting to Drawing: A New Perspective

De Kooning was primarily known as a painter, but his drawings—often dismissed as "preliminary work"—were far more than just studies. Many were finished pieces in their own right, exploring themes of female figures, landscapes, and urban decay with the same intensity as his canvases.

  • Example: "Woman—O" (1952), a charcoal and pastel drawing, mirrors the raw energy of his Woman paintings but in a more immediate, less polished form. The loose, jagged lines and layered textures show how de Kooning built his compositions through erasure and reworking—a process that feels alive and dynamic.

2. The Role of Spontaneity in Abstract Expressionism

Unlike the controlled precision of Renaissance drawing or the structured grids of Cubism, de Kooning’s drawings thrive on controlled chaos. His work suggests that great art isn’t about perfection—it’s about risk.

  • Key Insight: "The best drawings are those where you can see the hand of the artist struggling with the subject," says Dr. Sarah Bernstein, curator of the Chicago exhibit. "De Kooning didn’t erase mistakes; he turned them into part of the work."

3. How Drawings Bridge the Gap Between Idea and Execution

Many artists treat drawings as blueprints—a way to plan before painting. But de Kooning’s drawings blurred that line. Some were direct translations of his paintings, while others were completely independent, exploring new ideas.

  • Example: "Untitled (Seascapes)" (1953) is a series of rapid, gestural sketches that feel like improvisational jazz—each line reacting to the last, with no clear beginning or end. This non-linear approach is a lesson in embracing the unknown in your own work.

10 Actionable Strategies Inspired by de Kooning’s Drawings

Now that we’ve established the philosophical and technical brilliance of de Kooning’s drawings, let’s apply these lessons to your own artistic practice.

1. Embrace the "Wet Edge" Technique for Fluidity

De Kooning often worked with ink and watercolor, allowing the medium to bleed and merge naturally. This technique creates organic, unpredictable textures that resist rigid structure.

How to Apply It:

  • Use water-soluble markers or ink pens on rough paper (like Strathmore 400 Series).
  • Don’t overthink—let the medium do the work. Dab water onto the page to blur lines intentionally, creating soft transitions between shapes.
  • Example: If you’re sketching a landscape, instead of outlining trees, drip ink to suggest foliage, then scratch into the wet ink to add depth.

2. Work in Layers—Like a Palimpsest

De Kooning’s drawings often show multiple layers, where erased lines and repainted areas create a textured, historical effect. Think of them as artistic palimpsests—where each new mark reacts to the old.

How to Apply It:

  • Start with a light underdrawing (charcoal or pencil).
  • Work over it with ink or watercolor, then scratch into the wet medium to reveal previous layers.
  • Don’t fear destruction—if a line doesn’t work, cover it with paint or tear the paper to start fresh.

3. Use Gestural Lines to Define Form

De Kooning’s figures are never static. His swirling, uneven lines give his drawings a sense of movement, as if the subject is breathing.

How to Apply It:

  • Instead of precise outlines, use loose, expressive strokes to suggest form.
  • Example: If drawing a portrait, don’t define the nose with clean lines—scribble around it, then darken certain areas to imply its shape.
  • Practice: Do a 1-minute gesture drawing of a hand or face. Don’t lift your pen—let the line flow naturally.

4. Explore Contrast Through Thickness and Thinness

De Kooning’s lines vary dramatically in thickness—some are thin and delicate, others bold and aggressive. This visual tension keeps the drawing dynamic.

How to Apply It:

  • Use different pen nibs (fine liner for details, broad brush pen for thick strokes).
  • Vary pressure—press hard for bold lines, lift slightly for faint suggestions.
  • Example: In a cityscape, use thin lines for distant buildings and thick, jagged strokes for foreground structures.

5. Incorporate Collage and Mixed Media

Many of de Kooning’s drawings include tears, smudges, and collaged elements, turning the page into a living, evolving surface.

How to Apply It:

  • Tear paper and glue fragments onto your drawing.
  • Smear paint with your fingers or a brush to disturb the surface.
  • Example: Create a surreal landscape by gluing torn fabric onto a charcoal sketch, then painting over it with watercolor.

6. Study the Power of Negative Space

De Kooning’s drawings often rely on negative space—the empty areas between forms—to define shape and mood.

How to Apply It:

  • Leave gaps in your drawing—don’t fill every inch.
  • Example: If sketching a tree, don’t outline its branches—leave white space to suggest light filtering through.
  • Exercise: Do a black-and-white composition where only 30% of the page is inked.

7. Use Repetition for Rhythm and Energy

De Kooning’s repetitive motifs (like swirling patterns or repeated figures) create a hypnotic rhythm that draws the viewer in.

How to Apply It:

  • Stencil or trace the same shape multiple times, slightly varying each instance.
  • Example: Create a patterned background by repeating a simple mark (like a zigzag) in different sizes.
  • Tool Tip: Use a stencil brush or masking fluid to control repetition while keeping it organic.

8. Work Fast—Resist Over-Editing

De Kooning’s drawings often look unfinished on purpose. His raw, unpolished approach invites the viewer to fill in the gaps with their imagination.

How to Apply It:

  • Set a timer for 10–15 minutes and draw without stopping.
  • Don’t erase—let mistakes become part of the work.
  • Example: Instead of perfecting a landscape, scribble over areas you dislike, then build new forms on top.

9. Experiment with Unconventional Tools

De Kooning didn’t just use pencils and ink—he repurposed everyday objects as tools.

How to Apply It:

  • Use a fork or comb to drag paint across the page.
  • Dip a sponge in ink and stamp abstract marks.
  • Example: Create a textured background by dripping oil paint onto paper, then scratching into it with a knife.

10. Let the Drawing "Breathe"

De Kooning’s work feels alive because it resists completion. The incomplete edges, unfinished areas, and layered textures make the drawing feel like a living organism.

How to Apply It:

  • Don’t overwork—know when to stop.
  • Leave some areas blank to balance the composition.
  • Example: If drawing a portrait, leave the background undrawn to focus attention on the face.

Real-World Examples: How Artists Are Applying de Kooning’s Techniques

While the Chicago exhibit is the most comprehensive look at de Kooning’s drawings, other artists have embraced his approach in their own work.

1. The Gestural Portraits of Julie Mehretu

Mehretu, a contemporary artist known for her layered, abstract maps, often starts with rapid, gestural sketches that resemble de Kooning’s expressive lines.

  • How It Connects: Mehretu’s quick, confident strokes in her preparatory drawings mirror de Kooning’s spontaneous mark-making. Both artists use gesture to build complex compositions before refining them.

2. The Mixed-Media Works of Kara Walker

Walker’s silhouette-based drawings often incorporate tears, collage, and erasure, much like de Kooning’s destructive yet creative approach.

  • How It Connects: Walker embrace imperfection—her unfinished edges and layered textures create a dialogue between past and present, much like de Kooning’s palimpsestic drawings.

3. The Abstract Landscapes of Mark Bradford

Bradford’s collage-based paintings use layered paper and paint to create textured, narrative-driven works. His process echoes de Kooning’s experimentation with mixed media.

  • How It Connects: Both artists build depth through destruction—Bradford tears and repurposes paper, while de Kooning erases and repaints his sketches.

4. The Street Art of Os Gêmeos (Brazil)

The twin artists Os Gêmeos use gestural, graffiti-inspired lines in their murals, much like de Kooning’s urban-infused drawings.

  • How It Connects: Their loose, expressive strokes capture the energy of city life, just as de Kooning’s New York-themed drawings do.

5. The Digital Drawings of Refik Anadol

Anadol, a data artist, uses AI-generated gestures to create abstract, algorithmic drawings. His work reinterprets de Kooning’s spontaneity in a digital format.

  • How It Connects: While Anadol uses technology, his fluid, evolving lines reflect de Kooning’s organic, non-linear approach.

Common Mistakes When Drawing Like de Kooning—and How to Avoid Them

While de Kooning’s techniques are liberating, they can also lead to pitfalls if not approached carefully.

1. Mistake: Over-Reliance on "Messy" for Messy’ Sake

Problem: Some artists think chaos = good art, leading to unintentional disorganization.

Solution:

  • Plan your chaos—have a rough concept before diving in.
  • Example: If you’re drawing a cityscape, sketch a loose grid first, then break the lines with gestural marks.

2. Mistake: Ignoring Composition

Problem: De Kooning’s drawings feel alive because they balance chaos and structure, but some artists forget balance entirely.

Solution:

  • Use the rule of thirds as a starting point, then disturb it.
  • Example: Divide your paper into thirds, then place key elements along those lines before adding spontaneous marks.

3. Mistake: Not Experimenting with Tools

Problem: Sticking to pencils and erasers limits your textural possibilities.

Solution:

  • Test different mediums—ink, pastel, collage, digital.
  • Example: Try dipping a brush in coffee and dabbing it onto paper for organic textures.

4. Mistake: Fear of Erasure

Problem: Some artists avoid erasing because they think it’s "cheating," but de Kooning used erasure as a tool.

Solution:

  • Embrace destruction—tear paper, scratch into wet paint, or cover mistakes with new layers.
  • Example: If a line doesn’t work, paint over it with a different color and build from there.

5. Mistake: Rushing Without Preparation

Problem: While de Kooning worked fast, some artists skip planning entirely, leading to unfocused work.

Solution:

  • Do a quick thumbnail sketch before committing to a large drawing.
  • Example: If painting a portrait, doodle 5–10 small versions first to find the right composition.

6. Mistake: Not Letting the Drawing "Breathe"

Problem: Some artists overwork their drawings, erasing all imperfections until they lose their raw energy.

Solution:

  • Set a stopping point—know when to walk away.
  • Example: If a landscape feels "done," leave some areas blank to keep it dynamic.
