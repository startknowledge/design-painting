Yet, in a recent 2023 Chicago exhibition at the Art Institute of Chicago, it was not his canvases that stole the show—it was his drawings. These delicate, raw, and often unfinished sketches revealed a side of de Kooning rarely seen in his more polished paintings, proving that drawings were the foundation of his genius.

The success of this show underscores a broader trend: drawings are not just preliminary studies—they are powerful artworks in their own right.

Why de Kooning’s Drawings Were Revolutionary

De Kooning’s drawings are not mere preparatory studies—they are bold, chaotic, and emotionally charged, often rivaling his paintings in impact. Unlike traditional sketching, which is typically seen as a blueprint for final works, de Kooning’s drawings were freestanding artworks that explored themes of femininity, violence, and existential dread—just like his paintings.

1. The Raw Emotional Power of Line

De Kooning’s sketches are unfiltered expressions of his subconscious. In works like "Untitled (Woman)" (1950), he used tight, jagged lines to convey aggression and vulnerability, mirroring the same intensity found in his Woman series. This approach blurred the line between abstraction and figuration, making his drawings visually striking without relying on traditional composition.

Key Takeaway: Your sketches don’t have to be "perfect"—they should carry emotional weight.

2. The Role of Erasure and Layering

Many of de Kooning’s drawings feature erased lines, smudges, and overlapping marks, creating a sense of impermanence. This technique was not just a stylistic choice—it reflected his philosophy of art as an ongoing dialogue with the canvas. He often worked on the same sheet for days, adding and subtracting marks until the paper was visually exhausted.

Real-World Example: "Study for Excavation" (1950) begins with loose, gestural marks that gradually evolve into a chaotic, textured surface. The erasures reveal hidden layers, making the drawing feel like a dig into the subconscious. This method influenced later artists like Jean-Michel Basquiat, who also used layering and erasure in his sketches.

3. The Shift from Painting to Drawing as Primary Medium

While de Kooning is best known for his oil paintings, his drawings were just as essential to his practice. In interviews, he once said: "A drawing is a painting that has been killed by the artist." This statement highlights his respect for the sketch as a standalone work, not just a stepping stone.

Why This Matters for Artists Today: Many contemporary artists (including Julie Mehretu and Kehinde Wiley) use drawings as their primary medium, proving that sketches can be just as powerful as finished works.

8 Actionable Strategies to Master the Art of Sketching Like de Kooning

If you want to incorporate de Kooning’s approach into your own work, here are eight practical strategies to elevate your sketches:

1. Embrace Imperfection—Let the Drawing "Breathe"

De Kooning’s sketches are never polished. They often look messy, unfinished, or even "wrong"—but that’s the point. His intention was to capture the energy of the moment, not a flawless representation.

How to Apply This:

  • Work quickly—don’t overthink each mark.
  • Use loose, confident lines rather than tight, controlled strokes.
  • Leave some areas blank—sometimes, negative space is the strongest element.

Example: In "Untitled (Figure)" (1951), de Kooning scratched into the paper with a knife, creating raw, textured lines that feel almost violent. This technique can be replicated with charcoal or pastel, where accidental smudges become part of the design.

2. Use Layering to Build Complexity

De Kooning’s drawings often accumulate marks over time, creating depth and texture. Instead of starting with a clean sheet, work in stages, adding and subtracting lines until the paper is visually saturated.

How to Apply This:

  • Start with light, exploratory marks (pencil or charcoal).
  • Build up darker layers (ink, pastel, or marker) to create contrast.
  • Erase selectively to reveal hidden details beneath.

Example: "Study for Pink Angels" (1950) begins with soft, organic shapes that later harden into angular forms. The contrasts between light and dark make the drawing feel dynamic and alive.

3. Explore Abstraction Through Figuration

De Kooning’s drawings often straddle abstraction and representation. He didn’t just draw faces or bodies—he deconstructed them, using distorted lines and exaggerated features to evoke emotion.

How to Apply This:

  • Start with a recognizable subject (a face, a hand, a landscape).
  • Distort it intentionally—break proportions, add unexpected textures.
  • Focus on mood over realism.

Example: "Untitled (Head)" (1952) features a facial structure that’s almost unrecognizable, yet the energy of the lines conveys rage, sorrow, and desire. This approach can be applied to portraits, still lifes, or even abstract compositions.

4. Experiment with Mixed Media

De Kooning didn’t limit himself to one material. He used pencil, charcoal, ink, pastel, and even collage to create textured, multi-layered drawings.

How to Apply This:

  • Combine dry and wet media (e.g., charcoal + watercolor, ink + gouache).
  • Add collage elements (tears of paper, sand, or fabric) for extra dimension.
  • Try unconventional tools (palette knives, sponges, or even his fingers).

Example: "Untitled (Collage)" (1953) incorporates torn newspaper fragments into a charcoal drawing, creating a textural contrast that feels both raw and refined.

5. Work Directly from Observation (But Don’t Fear Abstraction)

While de Kooning’s drawings often refer to real subjects, they are not photorealistic. He observed the world but translated it through his own lens, making his sketches uniquely expressive.

How to Apply This:

  • Sketch from life (a person, a landscape, an object).
  • Let your hand move freely—don’t force accuracy.
  • Focus on capturing the "essence" rather than details.

Example: "Study for The Seascape" (1953) begins with loose, wavy lines that suggest water and sky, but the distorted forms make it feel more like a dream than a realistic scene.

6. Use Contrast to Create Drama

De Kooning’s drawings thrive on contrast—between light and dark, rough and smooth, controlled and chaotic. This visual tension makes his work visually gripping.

How to Apply This:

  • Vary your pressure (hard vs. soft strokes).
  • Use complementary colors (if working in color).
  • Play with texture (scratching, rubbing, or blending).

Example: "Untitled (Black and White)" (1951) uses sharp charcoal lines against a white background, creating a stark, almost aggressive contrast that draws the eye.

7. Let the Paper Tell Its Own Story

De Kooning often worked on the same sheet for hours, allowing the paper’s texture and wear to become part of the artwork. This organic approach makes his drawings feel alive and evolving.

How to Apply This:

  • Choose textured paper (watercolor, newsprint, or even cardboard).
  • Work on the same sheet for multiple sessions.
  • Embrace "accidents"—tears, smudges, and marks become intentional elements.

Example: "Untitled (Worn Paper)" (1952) shows visible creases and tears, making the drawing feel like a relic of the creative process rather than a polished piece.

8. Study the Evolution of a Single Drawing

De Kooning’s sketches often develop over time, with new layers added in different sessions. Instead of finishing a drawing in one sitting, he let it grow organically.

How to Apply This:

  • Work on a drawing for days (or weeks).
  • Add new elements without erasing everything.
  • Document the process (take photos of each stage).

Example: "Study for The Women" (1950) starts as a few loose shapes, then evolves into a complex network of lines and textures as de Kooning refines and reworks the composition.

Real-World Examples: How de Kooning’s Sketches Influenced His Masterpieces

De Kooning’s drawings were not just side projects—they were the foundation of his greatest paintings. Here’s how his sketches directly inspired his most famous works:

1. Woman I (1950–1952) – Born from a Single Sketch

The iconic Woman I began as a small charcoal drawing titled "Untitled (Woman Study)" (1950). The distorted face, exaggerated features, and chaotic energy in the sketch directly translated into the painting.

Key Parallels:

  • The jagged, almost "screaming" mouth in the drawing appears in the final painting.
  • The layered, textured surface of the sketch is mirrored in the oil’s impasto technique.
  • The use of erasure in the drawing (where he scratched away parts of the face) influenced the areas of the painting where the canvas shows through.

Lesson for Artists: Your sketches can be direct blueprints for larger works—don’t discard them as "failed attempts."

2. Excavation (1950) – A Drawing Unfolding into a Painting

"Study for Excavation" (1950) is a small, chaotic charcoal piece that prefigures the painting’s digging, layered composition. The scratches, smudges, and overlapping marks in the sketch became the "excavated" textures in the final work.

Key Parallels:

  • The angular, almost "broken" forms in the drawing appear as shattered surfaces in the painting.
  • The use of white gouache in the sketch (to highlight certain areas) is replicated in the painting’s white underpainting.
  • The raw, unfinished look of the drawing influenced the painting’s rough, tactile quality.

Lesson for Artists: Sometimes, the most "messy" sketches become the most powerful paintings.

3. Pink Angels (1950) – A Drawing That Defined a Series

The Pink Angels paintings are some of de Kooning’s most surreal works, featuring floating, elongated figures in a pink-hued landscape. The original sketch, "Untitled (Angel Study)" (1950), shows how the composition evolved:

  • The **original sketch had a more grounded, almost "earthy" feel, with heavy, dark lines.
  • In the final painting, these lines became softer, more ethereal, while the pink background (originally a muted tone) glowed with intensity.

Key Parallels:

  • The distorted, elongated bodies in the sketch became the signature "angel" forms.
  • The use of pink in the drawing deepened in the painting, creating a more dreamlike atmosphere.

Lesson for Artists: Your early sketches can set the tone for an entire series—don’t rush them.

Common Mistakes in Drawing-Based Art (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the greatest artists make mistakes. Here are five common pitfalls when working with sketches, along with de Kooning-inspired solutions:

1. Mistake: Treating Sketches as "Practice" (Not Art)

Problem: Many artists see sketches as just warm-ups before the "real" work begins. This mindset undervalues the sketch’s potential.

De Kooning’s Approach: He never separated sketches from finished works. His drawings were artworks in their own right.

How to Fix It:

  • Treat every sketch as a standalone piece.
  • Experiment with different materials (even if you’re not "finished").
  • Display your sketches—they deserve to be seen.

2. Mistake: Overworking the Drawing

Problem: Some artists spend too much time refining, turning a spontaneous sketch into a rigid composition.

De Kooning’s Approach: He worked quickly, allowing imperfections to remain.

How to Fix It:

  • Set a time limit (e.g., "I’ll work on this for 20 minutes").
  • Resist the urge to erase—let mistakes become part of the design.
  • Step away and return later—fresh eyes see new possibilities.

3. Mistake: Ignoring Texture and Layering

Problem: Many sketches rely only on line work, missing out on depth and complexity.

De Kooning’s Approach: He layered marks, used mixed media, and embraced texture.
