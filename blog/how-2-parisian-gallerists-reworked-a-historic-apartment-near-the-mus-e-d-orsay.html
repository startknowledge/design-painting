Chapter 1: The Challenge – Preserving History While Embracing the Future

Why This Apartment Was a Perfect (and Difficult) Project

The apartment in question was a three-bedroom, 120m² Haussmannian property built in 1885, featuring:

  • Original wrought-iron balconies with intricate scrollwork
  • Beamed ceilings with exposed woodwork
  • Marble fireplaces in two of the three living spaces
  • High ceilings (3.2m) with ornate cornices
  • Original stained-glass windows in the dining area

However, the space had several structural and aesthetic limitations:

  1. Outdated wiring and plumbing (from the 1950s)
  2. Peeling wallpaper in the bedrooms (a 1920s Art Deco remnant)
  3. Uneven floors due to settling over the decades
  4. A lack of natural light in the rear rooms
  5. Original partitions that divided the space inefficiently

Élodie Moreau, a Paris-based gallerist specializing in contemporary French art, and Thibault Lefèvre, an expert in historic preservation, faced a delicate balancing act:

  • Could they modernize without erasing the past?
  • How would they make the space feel both "timeless" and "cutting-edge"?
  • Would the transformation appeal to both art collectors and casual visitors?

Their solution? A hybrid approach—respecting the bones of the building while reinventing its soul.

Chapter 2: The 8 Key Strategies for a Historic Artistic Renovation

1. Conduct a Thorough Historic Assessment Before Making Any Changes

Why it matters: Before lifting a single nail, Moreau and Lefèvre hired a certified architect specializing in 19th-century Parisian homes. They documented:

  • Original structural elements (beams, load-bearing walls)
  • Authentic materials (type of plaster, wood species)
  • Any protected features (e.g., the stained-glass windows were listed as Monument Historique)

Actionable tip:

  • Hire a structural engineer to assess stability before demolition.
  • Consult local heritage offices (in Paris, this is the Direction des Affaires Culturelles).
  • Take 3D scans of the space to document its original state.

Real-world example: The team discovered that one of the bedroom walls was not load-bearing, allowing them to remove a partition to create a larger master suite. However, they replaced the wall with a modern glass-and-steel structure to maintain the room’s historic feel while improving functionality.

2. Preserve Original Architectural Details While Updating Their Function

The golden rule: "If it’s beautiful, keep it. If it’s broken, fix it—don’t replace it."

What they did:

  • Restored the wrought-iron balconies by sandblasting and reapplying patina-resistant paint.
  • Reinstalled original marble fireplace surrounds (some had been covered in 1970s linoleum).
  • Reused the beamed ceilings but painted them a deep charcoal to contrast with modern lighting.
  • Kept the stained-glass windows but installed LED lighting behind them to enhance their glow.

Actionable tip:

  • Prioritize restoration over replacement—many historic elements can be cleaned, repaired, or repainted rather than discarded.
  • Use modern materials that mimic old ones (e.g., reclaimed wood veneers instead of new wood).
  • Highlight original features with strategic lighting (e.g., spotlights on cornices).

Common mistake to avoid: ❌ Over-restoring—some patina (like on metal) adds character. Don’t go overboard with polishing. ❌ Ignoring structural integrity—if a beam is compromised, reinforce it, don’t just cover it up.

3. Redesign Layouts for Modern Living Without Losing Character

The problem: The original layout was functional but inefficient—small closets, a cramped kitchen, and no open-concept living area.

Their solution:

  • Removed a non-load-bearing wall between the living room and dining area to create a grand, light-filled space.
  • Added a floating staircase (in blackened steel) to maintain the vertical lines of the Haussmannian design while improving accessibility.
  • Expanded the kitchen by extending into a former hallway, keeping the original oak flooring but updating the cabinetry to matte black.

Actionable tip:

  • Work with an architect to reconfigure spaces while keeping at least one original feature (e.g., a fireplace, a door).
  • Use modular furniture that can be moved if future renovations are needed.
  • Consider open shelving to display artwork and historic artifacts together.

Real-world example: The original dining room had a low ceiling, so they installed a skylight to bring in natural light while keeping the stained-glass window as the centerpiece. The result? A moody yet luminous space that feels both timeless and modern.

4. Choose a Cohesive Color Palette That Honors the Past While Feeling Fresh

The dilemma: Should they stick to neutral tones (like many historic restorations) or introduce bold colors to make the space feel alive?

Their approach:

  • Base walls in a warm, muted beige (similar to original plaster tones).
  • Accent walls in deep navy (to contrast with the marble fireplaces).
  • **Floors in reclaimed terracotta tiles (a nod to 19th-century Parisian floors).
  • **Textiles in black, white, and gold (to echo Art Deco influences).

Actionable tip:

  • Test paint samples on small sections before committing.
  • Use color to define zones (e.g., a bold wall in the living room, neutral in bedrooms).
  • Balance warm and cool tones—historic spaces often have warm wood and cool stone; modern additions can bridge the gap.

Common mistake to avoid: ❌ Overusing white—while clean, it can make historic spaces feel sterile. Add textured fabrics or dark accents. ❌ Ignoring natural light—if a room is dark, lighter walls can help, but dark accents add depth.

5. Integrate Art and Design as Functional Elements

Since Moreau and Lefèvre are gallerists, they wanted the space to display art naturally—not as an afterthought.

How they did it:

  • Custom-built floating shelves (in blackened steel) to showcase modern art pieces alongside antique mirrors.
  • A gallery wall in the hallway featuring original sketches by the apartment’s first owner (discovered in the attic) alongside contemporary prints.
  • A hidden art storage system behind a false wall in the master bedroom.

Actionable tip:

  • Mix old and new art—historic spaces often have hidden treasures; pair them with modern pieces.
  • Use art to guide the eye—place large statement pieces near focal points (fireplaces, windows).
  • Consider interactive elements (e.g., a touchscreen display in the living room that cycles through artwork from the gallery).

Real-world example: The staircase landing now features a rotating display of small sculptures, with LED lighting that changes color based on the season or gallery exhibitions.

6. Upgrade Utilities Without Compromising Aesthetics

The challenge: The apartment had outdated electrical and plumbing, but exposed pipes and wiring would ruin the historic look.

Their solution:

  • Installed recessed lighting (instead of surface-mounted fixtures).
  • Used black metal conduit for wiring to match the industrial aesthetic of the kitchen.
  • Hidden plumbing behind custom cabinetry in the bathroom.

Actionable tip:

  • Work with an electrician who specializes in historic homes—they know how to disguise modern tech.
  • Use smart home systems (e.g., hidden speakers, automated lighting) for a luxurious, seamless feel.
  • Choose finishes that blend—e.g., brass fixtures in a gold-toned bathroom to echo Art Deco influences.

Common mistake to avoid: ❌ Using cheap, shiny materials—they clash with historic charm. Opt for matte black, brass, or aged bronze. ❌ Overloading outlets—historic wiring can’t handle modern devices. Distribute outlets strategically.

7. Create Zones That Feel Intentionally Designed (Not Just Functional)

The goal: Make the space feel like a curated gallery, not just a home.

How they achieved this:

  • The living room became a "gallery space" with soft seating, a fireplace, and a sound system for private viewings.
  • The kitchen was designed like a modernist studio, with open shelving for cookbooks and art.
  • The master bedroom had a walk-in closet that doubles as an art storage room.

Actionable tip:

  • Define each area with a signature element (e.g., a specific color, material, or lighting fixture).
  • Use furniture as room dividers (e.g., a large console table separating the living room from the dining area).
  • Add sensory details—scent diffusers, textured fabrics, or even a small indoor fountain to enhance the experience.

Real-world example: The dining room now has a long, narrow table (originally a 19th-century sideboard) as its centerpiece, with chairs in a mix of vintage and modern styles to encourage conversation and art appreciation.

8. Make the Space Sustainable Without Sacrificing Luxury

The eco-conscious approach:

  • Reclaimed wood for flooring and cabinetry.
  • Low-VOC paints to maintain air quality.
  • Energy-efficient windows (with historic-style frames).
  • Rainwater harvesting system for the garden.

Actionable tip:

  • Opt for FSC-certified wood and recycled materials where possible.
  • Install smart thermostats to regulate temperature efficiently.
  • Use natural light maximization to reduce electricity use.

Common mistake to avoid: ❌ Sacrificing comfort for sustainability—green materials should still feel luxurious. ❌ Ignoring local regulations—some historic areas have strict eco-building codes.

Chapter 3: Common Mistakes in Historic Renovations (And How to Avoid Them)

Renovating a historic space is not for the faint of heart. Here are five critical errors Moreau and Lefèvre encountered—and how they fixed them:

1. Skipping the Historic Survey

Mistake: Some homeowners assume their space is "just old" and skip professional assessments. Solution: Always document and consult experts before making changes.

2. Over-Restoring Instead of Repairing

Mistake: Trying to make everything look brand new (e.g., sanding down 100-year-old wood until it’s smooth). Solution: Embrace patina—some wear is part of the charm.

3. Poor Lighting Choices

Mistake: Using bright, modern fixtures that clash with dark, ornate moldings. Solution: Layer lighting—warm ambient, task lighting, and accent lights to highlight features.

4. Ignoring Acoustics

Mistake: Thin walls and high ceilings can make a space echoey. Solution: Add soft furnishings (rugs, curtains, upholstery) to absorb sound.

5. Choosing the Wrong Materials

Mistake: Using cheap laminates where real stone or wood would have worked. Solution: Invest in high-quality, durable materials that age well.
