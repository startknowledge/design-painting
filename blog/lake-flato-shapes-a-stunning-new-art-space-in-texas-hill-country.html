Why This Art Space Stands Out: The Lake Flato Approach

Lake Flato Architects, based in San Antonio, Texas, has built a reputation for sustainable, human-centered design that blends seamlessly with its surroundings. Their latest project—a new art space in the Texas Hill Country—is a perfect example of how architecture can elevate artistic expression while serving as a vibrant community hub.

Key Design Principles Behind the Project

  1. Sustainability as a Foundation

    • The art space incorporates passive cooling techniques, rainwater harvesting, and native plant landscaping to minimize environmental impact.
    • According to the U.S. Green Building Council (USGBC), buildings that prioritize sustainability can reduce energy costs by up to 30%—a smart investment for any long-term project.

  2. Adaptive Reuse of Materials

    • The design features reclaimed wood, recycled steel, and locally sourced stone, reducing waste while adding character.
    • This approach aligns with circular design principles, which are gaining traction in the AEC (Architecture, Engineering, Construction) industry.

  3. Flexible, Multi-Use Spaces

    • The art space includes modular exhibition galleries, collaborative studios, and outdoor performance areas, ensuring versatility for different artistic needs.
    • A 2022 report by the National Endowment for the Arts (NEA) highlighted that flexible, community-driven spaces are crucial for fostering creativity and engagement.

  4. Integration with Nature

    • Large glass walls and skylights flood the interior with natural light, while outdoor terraces blur the line between indoor and outdoor living.
    • This biophilic design approach has been shown to boost mood, productivity, and well-being (Journal of Environmental Psychology, 2021).

  5. Art as an Architectural Element

    • The space features custom murals, interactive installations, and structural art that enhance the user experience.
    • Lake Flato’s approach proves that art isn’t just decoration—it’s a core part of the architectural narrative.

8 Actionable Strategies to Design a Stunning Art Space (Like Lake Flato’s Texas Project)

If you’re planning an art space—whether for a gallery, studio, or cultural center—these proven strategies from Lake Flato’s project can help you create a visually striking, functional, and inspiring environment.

1. Prioritize Sustainability Without Sacrificing Aesthetics

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space uses energy-efficient HVAC systems, solar panels, and smart lighting that adjust based on occupancy. Even the roofing materials were chosen for their thermal performance and durability.

Actionable Tip:

  • Conduct an energy audit before design begins to identify inefficiencies.
  • Use passive design strategies (e.g., strategic window placement, thermal mass materials) to reduce reliance on mechanical systems.
  • Choose LEED-certified materials—they often have better insulation properties and lower VOCs (Volatile Organic Compounds) for healthier indoor air.

Real-World Example: In Austin, Texas, the Blanton Museum of Art (designed by Lake Flato) features a green roof that reduces stormwater runoff while providing a natural habitat for local wildlife. This dual-purpose design enhances sustainability and biodiversity without compromising the museum’s aesthetic appeal.

2. Create Flexible, Multi-Functional Spaces

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space includes reconfigurable walls, movable partitions, and modular furniture that allow for exhibitions, workshops, and performances in the same space.

Actionable Tip:

  • Design with adaptability in mind—avoid rigid layouts that limit future use.
  • Use modular furniture (e.g., stackable tables, foldable walls) to maximize versatility.
  • Include a central hub (like a lobby or courtyard) where different activities can overlap.

Real-World Example: The Dallas Contemporary (another Lake Flato project) features a large, open-plan gallery that can be divided into smaller sections using acoustic panels and movable barriers. This allows for both large-scale exhibitions and intimate artist talks in the same space.

3. Blend Indoor and Outdoor Experiences

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space incorporates floor-to-ceiling glass walls, outdoor galleries, and shaded terraces that encourage flow between inside and outside.

Actionable Tip:

  • Maximize natural light with skylights, clerestory windows, and glass doors.
  • Create outdoor art installations that extend the gallery experience.
  • Use weather-resistant materials (e.g., teak, stainless steel, or treated wood) for durability.

Real-World Example: The San Antonio Museum of Art’s Brackenridge Park extension (designed by Lake Flato) features an outdoor sculpture garden that complements the indoor exhibits. Visitors can walk through the park, view art, and enjoy the natural surroundings—making the experience both educational and immersive.

4. Incorporate Art as an Architectural Feature

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space includes custom murals, structural art installations, and interactive light features that enhance the user experience.

Actionable Tip:

  • Work with local artists to create site-specific installations.
  • Use art to guide movement (e.g., color gradients, geometric patterns).
  • Integrate technology (e.g., projection mapping, AR experiences) for modern engagement.

Real-World Example: The Witte Museum in San Antonio (also designed by Lake Flato) features a large, interactive water feature that doubles as both art and a cooling element in the summer. The dynamic light displays change throughout the day, creating a visually stunning experience that draws visitors deeper into the space.

5. Focus on Acoustics for a Seamless Experience

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space uses acoustic panels, sound-absorbing materials, and strategic ceiling designs to ensure clear audio for performances and lectures.

Actionable Tip:

  • Test acoustics early—use virtual acoustic modeling before construction.
  • Combine hard and soft surfaces (e.g., wood, fabric, and glass) for balance.
  • Consider ceiling height—higher ceilings improve sound diffusion.

Real-World Example: The AT&T Performing Arts Center in Dallas (designed by Lake Flato) is known for its exceptional acoustics, allowing orchestral performances to be heard clearly from every seat. The wooden paneling and curved ceilings work together to enhance sound quality without sacrificing aesthetics.

6. Use Local Materials for Authenticity and Sustainability

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space features Texas limestone, reclaimed oak, and locally sourced steel, giving it a distinct regional identity.

Actionable Tip:

  • Research local suppliers for unique, sustainable materials.
  • Highlight craftsmanship—local artisans often bring cultural depth to the design.
  • Consider durability—some materials (like stone and brick) last decades with minimal maintenance.

Real-World Example: The McNay Art Museum in San Antonio (another Lake Flato project) uses Texas limestone and native stone to create a warm, inviting atmosphere that reflects the regional architecture. The handcrafted details add a tactile, human touch that modern materials often lack.

7. Design for Accessibility and Inclusivity

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space includes wide doorways, ramps, accessible restrooms, and sensory-friendly spaces to ensure all visitors can enjoy the experience.

Actionable Tip:

  • Follow ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) guidelines—but go beyond compliance.
  • Include quiet rooms for visitors with sensory sensitivities.
  • Provide multilingual signage for a diverse audience.

Real-World Example: The Houston Museum of Fine Arts (designed with accessibility in mind) features elevators, tactile exhibits, and audio guides for visually impaired visitors. The flexible layout allows for wheelchair-accessible pathways without compromising the artistic flow.

8. Create a Strong Brand Identity Through Design

How Lake Flato Did It: The art space’s color palette, lighting, and materials reinforce the Texas Hill Country aesthetic—earthy tones, natural textures, and a sense of openness.

Actionable Tip:

  • Develop a cohesive brand style guide (colors, fonts, textures).
  • Use lighting to set the mood (warm for galleries, cool for tech spaces).
  • Tell the story of the space—why was it built? What does it represent?

Real-World Example: The Bullock Texas State History Museum (designed by Lake Flato) uses deep blues, warm woods, and dramatic lighting to evoke the history and culture of Texas. The immersive exhibits make visitors feel like they’re stepping into a different era.

Common Mistakes in Art Space Design (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most well-intentioned art space projects can go wrong if key considerations are overlooked. Here are five common pitfalls—and how Lake Flato’s approach helps avoid them.

❌ Mistake 1: Ignoring the Local Climate

Problem: Many designers overlook regional climate conditions, leading to poor ventilation, overheating, or drafts.

Lake Flato’s Solution:

  • Passive cooling (e.g., cross-ventilation, earth-berming)
  • Shade structures to reduce solar gain
  • Thermal mass materials (like stone or concrete) to regulate temperature

How to Avoid It:

  • Study local weather patterns—use historical climate data to inform design.
  • Consult a sustainability expert before finalizing plans.
  • Test prototypes (e.g., scale models in a wind tunnel).

❌ Mistake 2: Overlooking User Experience Flow

Problem: Poor circulation, confusing layouts, or lack of wayfinding can frustrate visitors and reduce engagement.

Lake Flato’s Solution:

  • Clear sightlines between key areas
  • Intuitive signage (digital and physical)
  • Flexible pathways that adapt to different events

How to Avoid It:

  • Map out visitor journeys—where do people enter? Where do they linger?
  • Use wayfinding tools (e.g., color-coding, directional arrows).
  • Test layouts with real users before finalizing.

❌ Mistake 3: Underestimating Maintenance Costs

Problem: Some materials and finishes require high upkeep, leading to budget overruns after construction.

Lake Flato’s Solution:

  • Durable, low-maintenance materials (e.g., stainless steel, epoxy floors)
  • Easy-to-clean surfaces (e.g., smooth stone, sealed wood)
  • Modular systems that can be upgraded over time

How to Avoid It:

  • Ask suppliers about long-term maintenance before purchasing.
  • Budget for 10-15% more than initial estimates for unexpected costs.
  • Choose finishes that resist wear (e.g., scratch-resistant coatings).

❌ Mistake 4: Neglecting Artistic Collaboration

Problem: Designing an art space without input from artists can lead to aesthetic mismatches or functional gaps.

Lake Flato’s Solution:

  • Early artist consultations to understand needs and inspirations.
  • Co-created installations that enhance the space’s purpose.
  • Flexible exhibition spaces that adapt to different art forms.

How to Avoid It:

  • Host workshops with local artists before design begins.
  • Include an art director on the project team.
  • Allow for post-construction adjustments based on feedback.

❌ Mistake 5: Overlooking Technology Integration

Problem: Many art spaces lack modern tech, making them less engaging for contemporary audiences.

Lake Flato’s Solution:

  • Smart lighting and climate control
  • Interactive digital displays
  • AR/VR experiences for immersive storytelling

How to Avoid It:

  • Plan for tech integration early—leave wiring and infrastructure accessible.
  • Test new technologies before full implementation.
  • Train staff on how to use digital tools effectively.
