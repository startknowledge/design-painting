8 Actionable Strategies for Designing a Stunning Art Space

If you’re planning a similar project—whether for a gallery, studio, or public art installation—these proven strategies from Lake Flato’s design approach will help you achieve functional beauty and cultural impact.

1. Embrace the Natural Landscape as a Design Inspiration

Lake Flato’s art space doesn’t just coexist with its surroundings—it celebrates them. The architects studied the topography, sunlight patterns, and native materials of the Hill Country to create a building that feels like an extension of nature.

How to apply this:

  • Site analysis: Before breaking ground, conduct a detailed environmental study—note wind patterns, seasonal light changes, and how visitors move through the space.
  • Material selection: Use locally sourced stone, wood, and metal to maintain authenticity while reducing environmental impact.
  • Open-air integration: Incorporate outdoor galleries, terraces, or viewing platforms to blur the line between indoor and outdoor experiences.

Real-world example: In a similar project in Austin, Texas, the architects designed a rooftop garden that doubles as an art installation, using native wildflowers and drought-resistant plants to attract pollinators while providing shade and aesthetic appeal.

2. Prioritize Flexibility for Artistic Expression

Art spaces need to adapt—whether for exhibitions, workshops, or community events. Lake Flato’s design includes modular, reconfigurable spaces that can transform based on the needs of the moment.

How to apply this:

  • Modular walls and partitions: Use tracked systems that allow walls to slide open or close, creating larger or smaller exhibition areas.
  • Multi-functional rooms: Design spaces that can serve as galleries by day and performance venues by night.
  • Adjustable lighting: Install smart lighting controls to highlight different artworks without permanent fixtures.

Real-world example: The Menil Collection in Houston (though not designed by Lake Flato) is a masterclass in flexibility. Its open, airy galleries can be rearranged to accommodate rotating exhibitions while maintaining a cohesive visitor experience.

3. Create a Sense of Flow with Thoughtful Wayfinding

A great art space isn’t just about the destination—it’s about the journey. Lake Flato’s design ensures that visitors discover the space naturally, with intuitive pathways that guide them through different exhibits.

How to apply this:

  • Natural lighting: Use skylights, clerestories, and large windows to create a light-filled, welcoming atmosphere.
  • Sequential storytelling: Arrange artworks in a logical progression—start with large, immersive pieces, then narrow into intimate displays.
  • Subtle signage: Avoid overwhelming visitors with signs. Instead, use minimalist wayfinding with landmark features (e.g., a striking staircase or a unique material choice).

Real-world example: The Dallas Contemporary features a spiral staircase that becomes a central navigational tool, drawing visitors deeper into the gallery while offering panoramic views of the surrounding city.

4. Incorporate Sustainable Practices Without Sacrificing Aesthetics

Modern art spaces must lead by example in sustainability. Lake Flato’s design includes green building certifications (such as LEED) while maintaining a luxurious, timeless look.

How to apply this:

  • Passive cooling: Use natural ventilation, cross-breezes, and thermal mass materials to reduce energy use.
  • Rainwater harvesting: Install permeable pavers and rain gardens to manage stormwater sustainably.
  • Energy-efficient systems: Opt for LED lighting, solar panels, and high-efficiency HVAC without compromising design.

Real-world example: The San Antonio Museum of Art’s expansion includes a green roof that not only reduces heat island effects but also provides a unique viewing platform for visitors.

5. Design for Accessibility and Inclusivity

A truly great art space welcomes everyone. Lake Flato’s design ensures universal accessibility, from wheelchair-friendly ramps to sensory-friendly spaces for visitors with disabilities.

How to apply this:

  • ADA compliance: Follow ADA guidelines for ramps, elevators, and restroom accessibility.
  • Sensory considerations: Provide quiet zones, adjustable lighting, and tactile exhibits for visitors with sensory sensitivities.
  • Multilingual signage: Include interpretive materials in multiple languages to enhance accessibility for diverse audiences.

Real-world example: The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, has a dedicated accessibility team that ensures all exhibits are navigable and engaging for visitors of all abilities.

6. Blend Art and Architecture Through Integrated Installations

The best art spaces don’t just display art—they become part of it. Lake Flato’s design includes architectural elements that interact with the art, creating a unified experience.

How to apply this:

  • Structural art: Use exposed beams, unique roof lines, or geometric patterns as part of the exhibition design.
  • Interactive installations: Incorporate touchscreens, augmented reality, or soundscapes that enhance the visitor experience.
  • Site-specific commissions: Commission local artists to create works that respond to the building’s architecture.

Real-world example: The High Museum of Art in Atlanta features a rotunda with a dramatic skylight that acts as both a structural marvel and a focal point for light-based installations.

7. Foster Community Engagement Through Public Programs

An art space isn’t just a building—it’s a hub for creativity. Lake Flato’s design includes spaces for workshops, performances, and community events, ensuring the space remains vibrant and relevant.

How to apply this:

  • Public art programs: Host artist residencies, open studios, and collaborative projects.
  • Educational spaces: Include classrooms, lecture halls, or maker labs for workshops and school groups.
  • Seasonal events: Plan festivals, film screenings, and live music to keep the space dynamic.

Real-world example: The Blanton Museum of Art at UT Austin runs free family days, artist talks, and hands-on workshops, making art accessible to all ages.

8. Invest in Long-Term Maintenance and Adaptability

A great design isn’t just about the opening day—it’s about lasting impact. Lake Flato’s art space is built with durability and adaptability in mind, ensuring it evolves with the community’s needs.

How to apply this:

  • High-quality materials: Use low-maintenance, durable finishes that stand the test of time.
  • Modular expansions: Design with future growth in mind—can the space easily be expanded?
  • Regular updates: Plan for periodic renovations to keep the space fresh and relevant.

Real-world example: The Walker Art Center in Minneapolis has undergone multiple expansions and renovations since its founding, always preserving its core identity while adapting to new trends.

Common Mistakes in Art Space Design—and How to Avoid Them

Even the most well-intentioned projects can fall short if key considerations are overlooked. Here are five common pitfalls in art space design—and how Lake Flato’s approach avoids them.

❌ Mistake 1: Ignoring the Local Context

Problem: Some designers create generic, one-size-fits-all spaces that don’t reflect the culture, climate, or history of the location. Solution: Study the region deeply—its artistic traditions, environmental challenges, and community needs—before designing.

Example: A gallery in New Orleans that ignored the city’s Creole architecture and flood risks would miss the opportunity to integrate water-resistant materials and cultural motifs into the design.

❌ Mistake 2: Overlooking Visitor Experience Flow

Problem: Poor wayfinding, lighting, or spatial organization can make an art space feel cluttered or confusing. Solution: Map out visitor journeys—how will people move through the space? What emotional impact should each area have?

Example: A museum in Chicago that placed high-traffic areas near restrooms created unnecessary congestion, disrupting the flow of visitors.

❌ Mistake 3: Sacrificing Sustainability for Aesthetics

Problem: Some designers prioritize looks over eco-friendly practices, leading to higher operating costs and environmental harm. Solution: Incorporate green building principles seamlessly—solar panels, natural ventilation, and recycled materials can enhance, not detract from, design.

Example: A luxury gallery in Los Angeles that used non-recycled marble and energy-guzzling HVAC ended up with higher maintenance costs and lesser environmental impact than a LEED-certified alternative.

❌ Mistake 4: Underestimating Flexibility Needs

Problem: Rigid, permanent layouts limit how the space can be used for different events. Solution: Design with adaptability in mind—modular walls, movable furniture, and reconfigurable lighting.

Example: A permanent exhibition hall in Boston that couldn’t be rearranged for pop-up markets or concerts quickly became underutilized.

❌ Mistake 5: Forgetting Accessibility

Problem: Inaccessible spaces exclude visitors with disabilities, reducing the social impact of the project. Solution: Follow ADA guidelines and think beyond compliance—consider sensory needs, multilingual support, and inclusive programming.

Example: A high-end gallery in Seattle that lacked braille signage or wheelchair-accessible restrooms received negative feedback from accessibility advocates.

❓ 2. What are the best materials for a sustainable art space in the Texas Hill Country?

Answer: Since the Texas Hill Country experiences hot summers, mild winters, and occasional droughts, the best materials for a sustainable art space include:

  • Stone & Brick: Local limestone or sandstone provides thermal mass, helping regulate indoor temperatures.
  • Wood: Sustainably sourced cedar or oak for durability and natural insulation.
  • Metal: Recycled steel or aluminum for structural integrity and low maintenance.
  • Glass: Double-pane, low-E glass to reduce heat gain while allowing natural light.
  • Permeable Pavers: For outdoor areas to manage stormwater runoff.

Avoid: ❌ Non-recycled concrete (high carbon footprint). ❌ Plastics or synthetic materials (poor long-term sustainability).

Example: Lake Flato’s project likely combines these materials in a way that minimizes environmental impact while enhancing aesthetic appeal.
