8 Actionable Strategies for Designing an Inspiring Art Space (Inspired by Lake Flato’s Approach)

If you’re planning an art space—whether for a private collection, a public gallery, or a creative studio—Lake Flato’s project offers valuable lessons. Here are eight proven strategies to apply in your own design:

1. Prioritize Sustainability Without Sacrificing Aesthetics

Lake Flato’s Approach: The firm used passive design strategies, such as cross-ventilation, deep overhangs, and thermal mass materials, to regulate temperature naturally. They also incorporated solar panels and rainwater harvesting systems to reduce utility costs.

How to Apply This:

  • Use locally sourced, recycled, or FSC-certified materials to reduce carbon footprint.
  • Maximize natural light with strategically placed skylights and large windows to cut energy use.
  • Incorporate green roofs or living walls to improve air quality and insulation.

Real-World Example: A contemporary art museum in Portland, Oregon, designed by Leddy Maytum Stacy Architects, achieved LEED Platinum certification by integrating geothermal heating, rainwater filtration, and a solar array—all while maintaining a sleek, modern aesthetic.

2. Adaptive Reuse: Breathing New Life into Old Structures

Lake Flato’s Approach: Instead of demolishing existing buildings, the firm repurposed historic barns and warehouses into galleries, studios, and event spaces, preserving the site’s character while adding modern functionality.

How to Apply This:

  • Preserve architectural details like exposed beams, brickwork, or original windows to add historical depth.
  • Modify spaces incrementally—convert attics into lofts, basements into galleries, or warehouses into studios.
  • Work with local historians or preservationists to ensure authenticity.

Real-World Example: The High Line in New York City, designed by Diller Scofidio + Renfro, transformed an abandoned elevated railway into a public park and art corridor, proving that adaptive reuse can create cultural landmarks.

3. Design for Flexibility: A Space That Evolves with Art

Lake Flato’s Approach: The art space features modular walls, retractable partitions, and adjustable lighting to accommodate rotating exhibitions, performances, and community events.

How to Apply This:

  • Use movable furniture and modular displays to reconfigure spaces quickly.
  • Install dimmable LED lighting to enhance different artworks.
  • Create multi-level platforms for installations that require height variation.

Real-World Example: The Broad Museum in Los Angeles, designed by Diller Scofidio + Renfro, features a vast, open-plan gallery with retractable walls and adjustable ceilings, allowing for endless exhibition possibilities.

4. Blend Indoor and Outdoor Experiences

Lake Flato’s Approach: The design eliminates rigid boundaries between inside and outside, with floor-to-ceiling glass walls, outdoor terraces, and integrated landscaping that invites visitors to engage with art in multiple ways.

How to Apply This:

  • Use large glass panels or sliding doors to create seamless transitions.
  • Incorporate outdoor sculptures or installations that complement indoor exhibits.
  • Design shaded outdoor lounges where visitors can reflect on art in a natural setting.

Real-World Example: The Serpentine Gallery Pavilion in London (designed by Frida Escobedo) features a shaded courtyard that extends the gallery experience outdoors, blending architecture, nature, and art.

5. Choose Materials That Tell a Story

Lake Flato’s Approach: The firm selected exposed stone, reclaimed wood, and handcrafted metalwork to reflect the Texas Hill Country’s rugged beauty, ensuring the materials feel authentic and timeless.

How to Apply This:

  • Use natural textures like stone, brick, or wood to ground the space.
  • Highlight craftsmanship—whether through hand-forged ironwork or custom joinery.
  • Consider patina effects—oxidized metals or weathered wood add character over time.

Real-World Example: The Kimbell Art Museum in Fort Worth, Texas (designed by Louis Kahn) uses exposed limestone and minimalist detailing to create a serene, contemplative environment that enhances the art within.

6. Lighting as an Art Form

Lake Flato’s Approach: The lighting design dynamically shifts—soft, warm tones for intimate exhibitions, dramatic backlighting for immersive displays, and natural daylight control to prevent glare.

How to Apply This:

  • Layer lighting—combine ambient, task, and accent lighting for depth.
  • Use adjustable track lighting to highlight specific artworks.
  • Incorporate smart lighting controls for energy efficiency.

Real-World Example: The Tate Modern’s Switch House extension (designed by Herzog & de Meuron) features custom-designed lighting systems that enhance the contrast and texture of modern art.

7. Create a Sense of Community and Accessibility

Lake Flato’s Approach: The space includes public plazas, interactive workshops, and flexible event spaces to ensure it’s not just for art lovers but for the entire community.

How to Apply This:

  • Host free or low-cost events like artist talks, workshops, or family days.
  • Design inclusive spaces—wide aisles, accessible restrooms, and seating areas.
  • Partner with local schools and nonprofits to expand outreach.

Real-World Example: The Museum of Contemporary Art (MOCA) in Los Angeles (designed by Frank Gehry) hosts free community programs, including youth art classes and public performances, making art accessible to all.

8. Focus on the "Human Scale"

Lake Flato’s Approach: The design avoids overwhelming visitors—spaces are intentionally scaled for comfort, with soft seating, quiet corners, and unobstructed views of the surrounding landscape.

How to Apply This:

  • Keep walkways wide enough for easy movement (at least 6 feet).
  • Provide seating niches where visitors can sit and contemplate art.
  • Avoid excessive clutter—clean lines and open spaces create a calm, contemplative atmosphere.

Real-World Example: The Louvre’s Denon Wing (designed by Pierre Visconti) maintains a grand yet human-scale experience, with ample space between exhibits to prevent crowding.

Common Mistakes in Art Space Design (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most well-intentioned art space projects can go wrong. Here are five pitfalls to avoid, based on lessons from Lake Flato’s success:

1. Overlooking the Site’s Natural Beauty

Mistake: Ignoring the landscape and climate of the location can lead to uncomfortable or impractical designs. Solution: Conduct a site analysis—consider sun exposure, wind patterns, and natural landmarks—and design around them.

Example: A gallery in a desert climate without proper shading or ventilation would be unusable in summer.

2. Lack of Flexibility in Layout

Mistake: Designing rigid, permanent installations that can’t adapt to different exhibitions. Solution: Use modular furniture, movable walls, and adjustable lighting to ensure the space remains versatile.

Example: A fixed, museum-style gallery may struggle to host live performances or interactive installations.

3. Neglecting Sustainability Early in the Process

Mistake: Adding eco-friendly features as an afterthought rather than integrating them from the start. Solution: Consult sustainability experts early and prioritize green materials and energy-efficient systems.

Example: A retrofit project that only adds solar panels later may lack proper insulation, reducing efficiency.

4. Underestimating the Importance of Lighting

Mistake: Using cheap, harsh lighting that destroys artwork or creates an unpleasant viewing experience. Solution: Invest in high-quality, adjustable lighting that enhances (not competes with) the art.

Example: Fluorescent lights can cause color distortion, while poorly placed LEDs may create unflattering shadows.

5. Forgetting the Human Experience

Mistake: Designing for aesthetics alone, without considering visitor comfort, accessibility, or engagement. Solution: Test the space with real people—observe how they move, interact, and experience the art.

Example: A gallery with no seating may discourage visitors from staying longer and appreciating the full experience.

3. How can I make an art space more sustainable without breaking the bank?

Answer: Sustainability doesn’t have to mean high costs—many low-cost, high-impact strategies exist:

✅ Passive Design Strategies (Free/Low Cost):

  • Cross-ventilation (design windows to catch breezes).
  • Shade structures (permanent or retractable awnings).
  • Thermal mass (using stone or concrete floors to regulate temperature).

✅ Energy-Efficient Upgrades:

  • LED lighting (uses 75% less energy than incandescent).
  • Smart thermostats to optimize heating/cooling.
  • Rainwater harvesting for irrigation.

✅ Material Choices:

  • Recycled or reclaimed materials (wood, metal, glass).
  • Low-VOC paints and adhesives for better air quality.
  • Native landscaping (reduces water and maintenance needs).

Example: A small gallery in Austin, Texas, reduced its energy bill by 40% by installing solar panels and using passive cooling—with a payback period of just 5 years.

4. What are the key considerations when designing an art space for accessibility?

Answer: An accessible art space ensures that everyone—regardless of ability—can enjoy the experience. Key considerations include:

🔹 Physical Accessibility:

  • Wider doorways (at least 32 inches) for wheelchairs.
  • Ramps or elevators for multi-level spaces.
  • Grab bars and tactile pathways for visually impaired visitors.

🔹 Sensory Considerations:

  • Quiet zones for visitors with sensory sensitivities.
  • Adjustable lighting to reduce glare or flick
