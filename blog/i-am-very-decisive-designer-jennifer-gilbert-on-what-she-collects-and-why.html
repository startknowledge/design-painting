"‘I Am Very Decisive’: Designer Jennifer Gilbert on What She Collects and Why"

A Deep Dive into the Mind of a Visionary Collector and How Her Approach Can Transform Your Own Design Aesthetic

Introduction: The Rise of Intentional Collecting in Design (2023–2024 Trends)

In an era where 78% of millennials and Gen Z consumers prioritize personalization in their living spaces (per a 2023 Homes & Gardens report), collecting has evolved from a hobby into a strategic expression of identity. Designers like Jennifer Gilbert, whose work blends minimalist functionality with bold, curated statements, prove that what you collect isn’t just about aesthetics—it’s about telling a story.

Gilbert, a London-based designer known for her work with brands like Selfridges and her own eponymous label, has built a reputation on decision-making with purpose. Her collections—whether it’s vintage Japanese ceramics, mid-century modern furniture, or contemporary art pieces—are never random. They’re thoughtfully chosen to evoke emotion, challenge norms, and create spaces that feel alive.

By the end, you’ll understand not just what to collect, but why—and how to do it with the same clarity and conviction as Jennifer Gilbert.

Part 1: The Philosophy Behind Jennifer Gilbert’s Collections

1.1 The Power of a Single, Defining Statement

Gilbert’s design philosophy revolves around the "one thing" rule—the idea that one standout piece can anchor a space and dictate its mood. She once described her approach as:

"If I can’t explain why a piece belongs in a room in one sentence, it doesn’t belong at all."

This principle is backed by design psychology: Focal points draw the eye, create rhythm, and prevent visual clutter. A 2024 study in Journal of Environmental Psychology found that rooms with a clear centerpiece (like a bold sculpture or a vintage clock) were perceived as 30% more harmonious than those with scattered decor.

Real-World Example: The Vintage Japanese Tsubo Jar In Gilbert’s London townhouse, a handcrafted 1950s tsubo jar (a traditional Japanese storage vessel) sits on a minimalist white shelf. It’s not just decor—it’s a cultural anchor. The matte black glaze, asymmetrical shape, and subtle crackle contrast with the sleek, modern furniture around it. Gilbert explains:

"It’s not about matching. It’s about creating a dialogue between old and new. That jar tells a story of craftsmanship, imperfection, and timelessness—qualities I want my spaces to reflect."

Key Takeaway: Your collection should speak to your values. If sustainability matters to you, prioritize upcycled or handmade pieces. If you love history, seek antique objects with provenance.

1.2 The Role of "Emotional Resonance" in Collecting

Gilbert doesn’t just collect objects—she collects memories, emotions, and experiences. She once said:

"A great collection isn’t just about what you own; it’s about what it makes you feel when you look at it."

This aligns with neuroscientific research on aesthetic pleasure: The brain releases dopamine when we encounter objects that evoke nostalgia, curiosity, or awe (Harvard Study, 2023). Gilbert’s approach ensures that every item in her home triggers a positive emotional response.

Real-World Example: The Mid-Century Modern Chair In her Bermuda studio, Gilbert has a 1960s Danish chair by Hans J. Wegner. It’s not the most expensive piece in the room, but it’s the one that makes guests pause. The warm walnut wood, organic curves, and hand-carved details remind her of afternoons spent in her grandmother’s kitchen, where similar chairs were used. She explains:

"That chair isn’t just furniture. It’s a bridge between past and present. It makes me feel grounded."

Actionable Tip:

  • Ask yourself: "Does this object make me happy, inspired, or curious?"
  • Keep a "feeling journal"—note how each potential purchase makes you feel before buying.

1.3 The Art of "Controlled Chaos"

While Gilbert’s spaces are visually clean, they’re not sterile. She embraces controlled imperfection—think a slightly off-center vase, a collection of mismatched books, or a gallery wall with varying frame sizes.

This reflects modern design trends, where 72% of interior designers (per Architectural Digest, 2024) now favor "curated clutter" over rigid symmetry. Gilbert’s rule:

"If a room feels like a museum, it’s dead. If it feels like a lived-in home, it’s alive."

Real-World Example: The Gallery Wall of Textures In her Brighton apartment, Gilbert’s gallery wall isn’t symmetrical. Instead, it’s a mosaic of textures:

  • A 19th-century Japanese woodblock print (framed in black)
  • A modern abstract painting (with a raw, unvarnished edge)
  • A vintage travel poster (torn at the edges)
  • A handwritten letter from a friend, preserved under glass

The asymmetry and mix of old/new create a dynamic visual rhythm that feels intentional, not accidental.

Why It Works:

  • Breaks monotony without looking haphazard.
  • Encourages conversation—people are drawn to explore the details.
  • Reflects real life, where things aren’t always perfect.

Common Mistake to Avoid: ❌ Overcrowding—too many items can make a space feel loud and overwhelming. ✅ Solution: Use the "rule of three"—group items in odd numbers (3, 5, or 7) for balance.

Part 2: 8 Actionable Strategies to Build Your Own Curated Collection (Like Jennifer Gilbert)

Strategy 1: Define Your "Why" Before You Buy

Gilbert’s first rule: Know your purpose. Before collecting, ask:

  • What story do I want my space to tell?
  • What emotions do I want to evoke?
  • What values does this object represent?

Example: If you love sustainability, focus on reclaimed wood, vintage textiles, or upcycled ceramics.

Action Step:

  1. Write down 3 core values (e.g., simplicity, history, warmth).
  2. Only consider items that align with at least two of them.

Strategy 2: The "One In, One Out" Rule

Gilbert maintains her collections by keeping her space fresh. She follows:

"For every new piece, I remove one that no longer serves its purpose."

This prevents clutter and ensures every item has meaning.

Action Step:

  • Before adding a new item, remove something similar (e.g., if you buy a new vase, donate an old one).
  • Rotate collections seasonally (e.g., swap summer beach art for winter botanical prints).

Strategy 3: Hunt for "Character Objects"

Gilbert avoids perfect, mass-produced decor. Instead, she seeks "character objects"—items with history, flaws, or unique stories.

Examples:

  • A vintage typewriter with a chipped paint job.
  • A hand-painted ceramic plate with a visible crack.
  • A secondhand leather jacket with patches and stains.

Why It Works:

  • Adds personality to a space.
  • Proves you’re not afraid of imperfection—a trait modern design loves.

Where to Find Them:

  • Flea markets (for hidden gems)
  • Estate sales (for one-of-a-kind pieces)
  • Local artisans (for handmade uniqueness)

Strategy 4: Use Color as a Unifying Thread

Gilbert’s collections often share a subtle color palette—whether it’s deep blues, warm terracottas, or muted greens. This creates visual harmony without being obvious.

Example: In her Parisian apartment, she pairs:

  • A 1970s blue ceramic vase (from Morocco)
  • A terracotta pottery piece (from Italy)
  • A muted green abstract painting (by a contemporary artist)

Action Step:

  1. Pick a dominant color (e.g., sage green).
  2. Introduce variations (e.g., dusty green, olive, mint).
  3. Balance with neutrals (white, black, wood) to avoid overwhelming the space.

Strategy 5: Incorporate "Silent Storytellers"

Gilbert believes objects should tell a story without needing explanation. These are items that evoke curiosity—like:

  • A vintage globe with a missing piece.
  • A collection of old postcards from different eras.
  • A family heirloom with a mysterious past.

Example: In her country home, she displays a 19th-century pocket watch that doesn’t work. When asked about it, she smiles and says:

"It’s not about function. It’s about the time it’s seen—all the hands that held it before me."

Action Step:

  • Ask yourself: "Would someone look at this and wonder about its story?"
  • Display items at eye level so they’re hard to ignore.

Strategy 6: Balance Bold and Subtle

Gilbert’s spaces never rely on one extreme. Instead, she contrasts bold statements with quiet elements to create dynamic balance.

Example: In her London studio, she has:

  • A large, abstract sculpture (as the focal point).
  • Three small, delicate vases (to soften the impact).
  • A single line of minimalist books (to add texture without clutter).

Action Step:

  • For every statement piece, add two neutral elements.
  • Use negative space—don’t fill every corner.

Strategy 7: Collect with a "Future Self" in Mind

Gilbert thinks long-term. She asks:

"Will I still love this in 5 years? Will it still make sense in my life?"

This prevents trend-chasing and ensures your collection evolves with you.

Example: She passed on a trendy neon sculpture in 2020 because:

"I don’t want my home to feel like a museum of fleeting trends. I want it to feel like me."

Action Step:

  • Wait 24 hours before buying—this reduces impulse purchases.
  • Ask a trusted friend: "Does this feel like you, or just a trend?"

Strategy 8: Make Collecting a Ritual

Gilbert treats collecting like a meditative practice. She visits antique shops on Sundays, attends auctions with a notebook, and takes photos of objects that inspire her—even if she doesn’t buy them.

Why It Works:

  • Turns collecting into a joyful habit, not a chore.
  • Helps you recognize quality over time.

Action Step:

  1. Dedicate one day a month to exploring (thrift stores, markets, galleries).
  2. Keep a "wish list" of objects that resonate with you.
  3. Visit the same places regularly—you’ll notice new pieces emerge.

Part 3: Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Mistake 1: Collecting Without a Theme

Problem: Buying items randomly leads to a cluttered, disjointed space.

Gilbert’s Fix:

"If you can’t explain the ‘why’ behind each piece, you’re just accumulating stuff."

Solution:

  • Pick a theme (e.g., travel, nature, industrial, vintage).
  • Stick to it—or mix themes intentionally (e.g., travel + nature).

Mistake 2: Overvaluing Trends Over Timelessness

Problem: Chasing fashionable decor (like maximalist wallpaper or bold colors) can make a space feel dated quickly.

Gilbert’s Fix:

"Trends come and go, but you stay. Your home should reflect that."

Solution:

  • Prioritize neutral bases (white walls, wood floors).
  • Use trends sparingly (e.g., one bold throw pillow, not an entire room).

Mistake 3: Ignoring Scale and Proportion

Problem: A tiny vase on a massive table or a huge sculpture in a small room can disrupt the flow.

Gilbert’s Fix:

"Every piece should feel like it belongs—like it’s been there for years."

Solution:

  • Measure your space before buying.
  • Use the "hand rule": If an object is smaller than your hand, it may be too delicate for a high-traffic area.

Mistake 4: Collecting Only What’s "Safe"

Problem: Sticking to neutral, boring items (like plain white vases or generic frames) makes a space lifeless.

Gilbert’s Fix:

"Boredom is the enemy of beauty."

Solution:

  • Introduce one "wildcard" piece per room.
  • Mix textures (smooth vs. rough, matte vs. glossy).

Mistake 5: Not Letting Go

Problem: Hoarding unused or unloved items turns a home into a storage unit.

Gilbert’s Fix:

"A home is a living thing—it should grow and change with you."

Solution:

  • Do a quarterly "edit"—remove items that no longer spark joy.
  • Donate or sell what you don’t need (even if it’s "just in case").
