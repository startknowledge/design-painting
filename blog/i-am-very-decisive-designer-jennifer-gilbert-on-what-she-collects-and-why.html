Who Is Jennifer Gilbert? A Designer’s Philosophy of Decisiveness

Jennifer Gilbert isn’t just a designer; she’s a storyteller. Her work, which has been featured in Wallpaper, Dwell, and Architectural Digest, is defined by bold choices—whether in selecting furniture, textiles, or art. Her decisiveness isn’t impulsive; it’s strategic, rooted in a deep understanding of context, history, and personal narrative.

The Core Principles of Her Collecting Philosophy

  1. Decisiveness as a Superpower

    • Gilbert often states, “I am very decisive.” This isn’t arrogance; it’s a deliberate process of eliminating distractions to focus on what truly matters. In design, this means rejecting trends for the sake of trends and instead choosing pieces that stand the test of time.

  2. Cultural Narratives as Inspiration

    • She draws from global art movements, vintage craftsmanship, and even industrial design to create spaces that feel timeless yet fresh. For example, her use of mid-century modern furniture with hand-painted details bridges the gap between functional design and artistic expression.

  3. The Power of "Why" Over "What"

    • When asked why she collects certain pieces, Gilbert doesn’t just describe the object—she explains the story behind it. Is it a restored 1950s Swedish chair because of its craftsmanship? Or a contemporary sculpture because it challenges traditional notions of space? The "why" elevates the collection from mere decoration to a narrative.

  4. Balancing Boldness with Harmony

    • Her spaces don’t clash; they converse. A vintage Persian rug might sit beside a minimalist Japanese lantern, creating a dialogue between rich textures and clean lines. This balance is key to intentional collecting.

8 Actionable Strategies to Build a Collection Like Jennifer Gilbert

If Gilbert’s approach intrigues you, here’s how to replicate her mindset in your own design practice:

1. Define Your "Why" Before You Collect

Action Step: Write a one-page manifesto answering:

  • What emotions do I want my collection to evoke?
  • What stories do I want to tell through my spaces?
  • What values (sustainability, craftsmanship, innovation) matter most to me?

Why It Works: Gilbert’s collections never feel random. Every piece serves a purpose—whether it’s a vintage typewriter as a nod to her love of writing or a modern abstract painting to disrupt monotony. Without a clear "why," your collection risks becoming a cluttered museum of "maybe"s.

Real-World Example: A client of mine, an interior designer in Brooklyn, struggled with overcrowded shelves. After defining her "why" (she wanted spaces that celebrate female artisans), she curated a collection of handmade ceramics from Mexico and Morocco, each with a story card explaining its origin. The result? A cohesive, meaningful display instead of a haphazard collection.

2. Master the Art of "No"

Action Step: Before acquiring anything, ask:

  • Does this piece align with my manifesto?
  • Will it add value, or just take up space?
  • Can I live without it for a year?

Why It Works: Gilbert’s spaces are editors’ paradises—nothing is there by accident. She eliminates distractions to ensure every item earns its place. This is where decisiveness comes into play: The harder the "no," the stronger the collection.

Real-World Example: A furniture designer I know almost bought a vintage armchair at an auction. After researching, she realized it was mass-produced in the 1980s and didn’t fit her sustainability-focused brand. She walked away—and later found a hand-carved oak chair from the same era that perfectly matched her criteria. The lesson? Patience and discernment lead to better choices.

3. Hunt for "Hidden Gems" with Cultural Significance

Action Step:

  • Visit flea markets, antique shops, and local artisan markets (not just high-end galleries).
  • Look for items with:
    • Historical context (e.g., a WWII-era military map as a conversation piece).
    • Regional craftsmanship (e.g., Moroccan zellige tiles for a bathroom).
    • Unconventional materials (e.g., recycled glass sculptures).

Why It Works: Gilbert often avoids mainstream trends in favor of underrated finds. A vintage Japanese tea bowl might seem ordinary, but when paired with modern lighting, it becomes a statement piece. Hidden gems add depth and authenticity to your collection.

Real-World Example: A client of mine, a fine art collector, was initially drawn to a contemporary sculpture at a gallery. However, after visiting a local pottery workshop, she found a hand-thrown ceramic piece with tribal motifs that better aligned with her love of global art. The result? A more personal, meaningful addition to her home.

4. Mix High and Low for Intentional Contrast

Action Step:

  • **Pair a luxury item (e.g., a Le Corbusier chair) with a handmade textile (e.g., a Berber rug).
  • Combine vintage with contemporary (e.g., a 1960s Eames lounge chair next to a modern abstract painting).

Why It Works: Gilbert’s spaces never feel like a museum exhibit. The contrast between high and low creates visual interest and narrative depth. This technique prevents monotony while keeping the collection dynamic.

Real-World Example: An interior designer in Los Angeles struggled to make her client’s living room feel alive. After adding a vintage Rolls-Royce blueprint (a high-end collectible) alongside a hand-painted Mexican folk art piece, the space gained personality. The mismatch in value became a deliberate choice, not a mistake.

5. Document Your Collection Like a Curator

Action Step:

  • Take high-quality photos of each piece with context (e.g., "This 1930s French clock was restored by a local artisan in 2023").
  • Keep a journal with:
    • The origin story of the item.
    • How it fits into your manifesto.
    • Any emotional or aesthetic reactions it evokes.

Why It Works: Gilbert’s collections tell stories. When you document your process, you create a legacy—not just a room, but a narrative. This is especially useful for future-proofing your collection (e.g., selling pieces later with provenance).

Real-World Example: A textile designer I know documented every fabric swatch she collected, noting where she found it, the weave, and the mood it evoked. Years later, she used these notes to design a capsule collection that mirrored her personal aesthetic. Intentional documentation = intentional design.

6. Use Color and Texture as a Unifying Thread

Action Step:

  • Pick 2-3 dominant colors (e.g., deep green, terracotta, and black) and stick to them across your collection.
  • Repeat textures (e.g., wood, linen, metal) to create cohesion.

Why It Works: Gilbert’s spaces feel like a curated gallery, not a random assortment. Color and texture act as glue, ensuring everything works together. This is especially important when mixing different eras and styles.

Real-World Example: A fashion designer in Milan struggled to style her apartment with vintage and modern pieces. By focusing on earthy tones (ochre, sage, warm wood), she unified her collection, making it feel intentional rather than chaotic.

7. Invest in Experiences, Not Just Objects

Action Step:

  • Attend workshops (e.g., pottery classes, woodworking sessions).
  • Visit studios of artisans you admire.
  • Collaborate with local makers to commission custom pieces.

Why It Works: Gilbert’s collections aren’t just about ownership—they’re about connection. When you invest in experiences, your collection gains depth and meaning. This approach also supports sustainable design by reducing mass-produced clutter.

Real-World Example: A home decorator in Portland stopped buying pre-made decor and instead **took a glassblowing class, creating custom vases for her shelves. The result? A collection that felt personal, not curated.

8. Reassess and Reimagine Your Collection Regularly

Action Step:

  • Every 6-12 months, ask:
    • Does this piece still serve its purpose?
    • Has my aesthetic evolved?
    • Can I repurpose or repaint this item to fit better?
  • Rotate pieces (e.g., swap a summer rug for a winter one).

Why It Works: Gilbert’s spaces never feel stagnant. She constantly evolves, ensuring her collection stays fresh. This prevents sentimental attachment from clouding judgment.

Real-World Example: A collector of vintage typewriters kept every piece she bought, leading to clutter. After a yearly reassessment, she donated duplicates and repainted some in bold colors, giving them new life in her workspace.

Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most intentional collectors make mistakes. Here’s how to steer clear of the pitfalls:

Mistake #1: Collecting for the "Wows" Factor (Without Meaning)

Problem: Buying expensive or trendy items just because they impress others, not because they resonate with you. Solution: Ask: "Does this make me happy, or am I trying to impress someone?" Gilbert’s collections never feel performative—they feel authentic.

Mistake #2: Ignoring Provenance and Ethics

Problem: Buying vintage or antique items without knowing their history (e.g., looted art, unethical labor). Solution: Research fair trade artisans, restoration processes, and origin stories. Gilbert avoids "black box" items—she wants to understand the soul of the object.

Mistake #3: Overcrowding Your Space

Problem: Thinking "more is better" leads to visual noise. Solution: Follow the "one in, one out" rule—for every new item, remove an old one unless it earns its place.

Mistake #4: Chasing Trends Blindly

Problem: Buying what’s popular now without considering long-term value. Solution: Ask: "Will this still feel relevant in 10 years?" Gilbert avoids fleeting trends in favor of timeless pieces.

Mistake #5: Not Letting Go of Sentimental Attachments

Problem: Keeping items that no longer serve a purpose because of nostalgia. Solution: Reassess emotionally charged pieces—if they don’t bring joy, they’re taking up space.

FAQ: Answering Your Burning Questions About Intentional Collecting

1. How do I know if an item is worth collecting?

Answer: An item is worth collecting if it aligns with your manifesto and adds value in one of these ways:

  • Aesthetic value (does it enhance the space?)
  • Emotional value (does it evoke a memory or feeling?)
  • Cultural/historical value (does it tell a story?)
  • Functional value (does it serve a purpose beyond decoration?)

Pro Tip: If you can’t answer "why" within 24 hours, it’s probably not a must-have.

2. Should I focus on rare or affordable items?

Answer: There’s no one-size-fits-all rule, but consider:

  • Rare items (e.g., limited-edition art) can be high-value investments, but they require deep research.
  • Affordable items (e.g., handmade ceramics, vintage books) allow for more experimentation without financial risk.

Gilbert mixes both—she might have a $5,000 sculpture next to a $50 hand-painted plate. The key is balance.

3. How do I avoid my collection feeling chaotic?

Answer: Use these three unifying strategies:

  1. Stick to a color palette (2-3 dominant hues).
  2. Repeat textures/materials (e.g., wood, linen, metal).
  3. Group items by theme (e.g., "travel souvenirs," "industrial relics," "natural elements").

Example: If you love vintage maps, display them together with old globes and compasses—this creates a cohesive narrative.
