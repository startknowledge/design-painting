Who Is Jennifer Gilbert? A Designer’s Perspective on Collecting

Jennifer Gilbert is not just a designer—she is a storyteller through objects. Her work spans interior design, fashion, and decorative arts, but her true passion lies in curating collections that tell a narrative. Unlike many collectors who chase trends or status symbols, Gilbert’s approach is rooted in authenticity and emotional connection.

Key Traits That Define Her Collecting Philosophy

  1. Decisiveness Without Hesitation

    • Gilbert has stated in interviews that she rarely overthinks purchases. Instead, she trusts her instincts, which is why her collections feel cohesive and intentional rather than scattered.
    • "I don’t collect things I might like someday. I collect things I love now." — Jennifer Gilbert

  2. A Love for the Unconventional

    • She favors vintage, handcrafted, and culturally significant pieces over mass-produced items. Her collections often include mid-century modern furniture, antique textiles, and artisanal ceramics—each with a story.

  3. Functionality Meets Aesthetics

    • Unlike some collectors who prioritize rarity over usability, Gilbert ensures that every piece she acquires serves a purpose—whether in her home, studio, or as a gift.

  4. Cultural and Historical Context

    • She seeks objects that reflect a moment in time, whether it’s a 1960s Scandinavian lamp or a 19th-century Japanese woodblock print. This adds depth to her collections.

  5. Emotional Resonance Over Market Value

    • While she appreciates investment potential, Gilbert’s primary driver is how a piece makes her feel. A hand-painted Mexican talavera tile might be priceless to her not because of its auction value, but because of its craftsmanship and cultural heritage.

8 Actionable Strategies to Collect Like Jennifer Gilbert

Inspired by Gilbert’s methods, here are eight strategies to build a collection that is meaningful, intentional, and uniquely yours.

1. Define Your "Why" Before You Start Collecting

Gilbert’s collections begin with a clear purpose. Ask yourself:

  • What does this collection represent? (e.g., a love for mid-century design, a fascination with global textiles)
  • How will this collection evolve over time? (Will it grow with you, or will it feel stagnant?)
  • What emotions do I want it to evoke? (Warmth? Nostalgia? Sophistication?)

Example: If you’re collecting vintage typewriters, your "why" might be a nostalgia for analog communication or an appreciation for mechanical craftsmanship.

Action Step:

  • Write a mission statement for your collection (even if it’s just a few sentences).
  • Stick to it—if a piece doesn’t align, don’t force it.

2. Prioritize Stories Over Status

Gilbert’s collections are not about bragging rights—they’re about narratives. She seeks objects with:

  • Provenance (e.g., a piece used by a famous designer).
  • Cultural significance (e.g., a textile from a specific region).
  • Personal connection (e.g., a gift from a loved one).

Real-World Example: Imagine a 1950s French chair that was once owned by a Parisian café owner. The story of its daily use—of people sitting in it, laughing, sipping espresso—adds layers of meaning far beyond its material value.

Action Step:

  • When researching a potential acquisition, dig into its history. Ask sellers about:
    • Who made it?
    • Where was it used?
    • What was its original purpose?

3. Trust Your Instincts (But Verify First)

Gilbert’s decisiveness comes from years of experience, but she still researches before committing. Her rule:

  • If it sparks joy and aligns with your collection’s theme, move forward.
  • If it’s just "nice," walk away.

Common Mistake: Buying something because it’s trendy or on sale, only to realize later it doesn’t fit your aesthetic.

Action Step:

  • Create a "yes/no/maybe" checklist for potential acquisitions.
    • Yes: Fits your collection’s theme, has a compelling story, feels right.
    • No: Doesn’t align, lacks provenance, feels forced.
    • Maybe: Needs more research—set a deadline to decide.

4. Mix High and Low (But Keep It Cohesive)

Gilbert’s collections balance luxury and affordability. She believes:

  • A single high-end piece can elevate a room (e.g., a custom rug).
  • Affordable, well-chosen items can fill gaps without overwhelming (e.g., vintage books, handmade ceramics).

Real-World Example: A 1970s Danish Eames chair (high-end) paired with mid-century glass vases (lower-cost finds) creates a harmonious, curated look rather than a cluttered display.

Action Step:

  • Allocate 70% of your budget to core pieces (the "high" items).
  • Use the remaining 30% for complementary finds (the "low" items).
  • Test arrangements in your space before finalizing purchases.

5. Seek Out the "In-Between" (Not Just New or Vintage)

Gilbert avoids extremes—she doesn’t just collect brand-new designer pieces or antique rarities. Instead, she looks for:

  • Restored vintage items (e.g., a 1980s lamp with modern refinishing).
  • Limited-edition contemporary works (e.g., a single artist’s ceramic piece).
  • Handmade modern pieces (e.g., a sustainably sourced wooden sculpture).

Why? Because these items offer a balance of history and freshness, avoiding the sterility of new or the wear-and-tear of old.

Action Step:

  • Expand your search to auction houses, local artisans, and pop-up shops—not just high-end galleries.
  • Ask sellers about restoration history—was it professionally done, or DIY?

6. Collect in Layers (Not All at Once)

Gilbert builds her collections gradually, ensuring each piece adds value rather than creating chaos. Her approach:

  • Start with 3-5 core pieces that define your aesthetic.
  • Add 1-2 new items per season to maintain growth without overwhelm.
  • Rotate pieces to keep your space feeling fresh.

Real-World Example: A modernist bookshelf (core) might be paired with:

  • A vintage typewriter (first addition).
  • A hand-painted ceramic plate (second addition).
  • A small sculpture (third addition).

This layered approach prevents collection fatigue—a common issue when people buy too much too soon.

Action Step:

  • Set a purchase limit (e.g., no more than one major item per month).
  • Before buying, ask: "Does this enhance what I already have, or does it compete?"

7. Consider the "Soul" of the Object

Gilbert doesn’t just collect beautiful things—she collects things with soul. She looks for:

  • Handmade imperfections (e.g., a hand-thrown pottery piece with a slight crack).
  • Patina and wear (e.g., a leather-bound book with age marks).
  • Ethical sourcing (e.g., fair-trade textiles, recycled materials).

Why? Because these flaws and stories make objects more human and relatable.

Action Step:

  • When evaluating a piece, touch it, hold it, examine its texture.
  • If it feels cold or mass-produced, reconsider.
  • If it has character, it’s likely a better fit.

8. Document and Share Your Collection’s Journey

Gilbert keeps detailed records of her acquisitions—where she found them, why she chose them, and how they fit into her space. She believes:

  • Documentation adds value (for future reference or potential resale).
  • Sharing your story (through blogs, social media, or journals) deepens your connection to the collection.

Real-World Example: She might write a short essay about her 1960s Italian sideboard, detailing:

  • How she found it in a small-town antique shop.
  • Why its walnut finish and brass hardware remind her of her grandmother’s home.
  • How it anchors her dining room’s aesthetic.

Action Step:

  • Start a collection journal (digital or physical) with:
    • Purchase dates.
    • Provenance notes.
    • Photos (if allowed).
    • Personal reflections.
  • Share one piece per month on social media with a story behind it.

Common Mistakes Collectors Make (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most passionate collectors fall into traps. Here’s how to steer clear of the pitfalls that derail intentional collecting.

Mistake #1: Chasing Trends Without Soul

Problem: Buying because something is "in" rather than because it resonates with you. Example: Purchasing a trendy plant stand just because it’s popular on Instagram, only to realize it doesn’t fit your space or style in six months.

Solution:

  • Ask: "Will I still love this in five years?"
  • Limit trend-driven purchases to no more than 10% of your collection.

Mistake #2: Overlooking Provenance

Problem: Assuming a piece is valuable just because it’s old or rare, without knowing its history. Example: Buying a vintage chair from an unknown seller without asking who made it, where it was used, or how it was cared for.

Solution:

  • Always ask for documentation (receipts, appraisals, photos from previous owners).
  • If provenance is unclear, pass on it—unless you’re okay with a wild guess.

Mistake #3: Collecting for Resale, Not Joy

Problem: Treating your collection like an investment portfolio rather than a passion project. Example: Buying vintage watches because you think they’ll appreciate, but never wearing them because they’re just "assets."

Solution:

  • Set a 50/50 rule: 50% of your collection should be for joy, 50% for potential resale value.
  • If you’re not using or loving a piece, donate or sell it.

Mistake #4: Neglecting the Space It Lives In

Problem: Buying pieces that don’t fit your home’s layout or style. Example: Purchasing a large, ornate mirror for a small apartment, only to realize it makes the room feel cramped.

Solution:

  • Measure your space before buying.
  • Test pieces in your home (even if it’s just a virtual layout).
  • Start small—fill gaps before adding statement pieces.

Mistake #5: Ignoring Maintenance and Care

Problem: Buying delicate or high-maintenance items without planning for their upkeep. Example: Investing in a handwoven Persian rug but not knowing how to clean or store it properly, leading to damage over time.

Solution:

  • Research care instructions before buying.
  • Budget for maintenance (e.g., professional cleaning, refinishing).
  • Store pieces properly (e.g., humidity-controlled environments for wood).

Mistake #6: Collecting in a Vacuum

Problem: Not connecting with other collectors, designers, or experts to refine your taste. Example: Buying vintage furniture without consulting a designer on how to style it, leading to a clashing, unbalanced look.

Solution:

  • Join collecting communities (online forums, local clubs, social media groups).
  • Attend auctions, pop-up shops, and design events.
  • Seek feedback from trusted sources before major purchases.
