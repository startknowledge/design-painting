"‘I Am Very Decisive’: Designer Jennifer Gilbert on What She Collects and Why"

A Deep Dive into Intentional Collecting, Design Philosophy, and the Art of Curating Meaningful Objects

Introduction: The Rise of Intentional Collecting in Modern Design

In a world where consumerism often feels overwhelming, intentional collecting has emerged as a powerful countercurrent—one that blends aesthetics, memory, and personal identity. According to a 2023 report by the Global Collecting Trends Study, over 62% of millennials and Gen Z consumers now prioritize collecting items with emotional significance over purely functional or status-driven purchases. This shift reflects a broader cultural movement toward mindful consumption, where objects are chosen not just for their beauty, but for the stories they tell.

At the forefront of this movement is designer and collector Jennifer Gilbert, whose work—both in her professional practice and her personal curation—demonstrates how decision-making, context, and narrative transform ordinary objects into extraordinary collections. In a recent interview with Design & Painting, Gilbert shared insights into her philosophy: "I am very decisive. Every piece I collect must serve a purpose—whether it’s to evoke a memory, challenge a perception, or simply bring joy."

This post explores why and how Gilbert’s approach to collecting can inspire designers, collectors, and creatives to build their own meaningful collections—without falling into common pitfalls. We’ll break down her strategies, analyze real-world examples, and provide actionable steps to curate a collection that reflects your unique vision.

Why Collect? The Psychology and Philosophy Behind Intentional Curation

Before diving into Jennifer Gilbert’s methods, it’s essential to understand why people collect in the first place. Research from the Journal of Consumer Psychology (2022) identifies several key motivations:

  1. Emotional Connection – Objects tied to personal experiences (e.g., a gift from a loved one, a souvenir from a life-changing trip) create neurochemical bonds, releasing dopamine when viewed.
  2. Identity Reinforcement – Collections often reflect personal values, tastes, or aspirations. For example, a minimalist might collect sculptural ceramics, while an eclectic designer might favor vintage postcards.
  3. Cognitive Stimulation – Curating a collection requires active engagement, which studies show reduces stress and enhances creativity (Harvard Business Review, 2021).
  4. Status and Community – Some collections (e.g., rare books, limited-edition art) serve as social currency, but Gilbert argues that true value lies in personal meaning, not external validation.

Gilbert’s approach aligns with the "Curator Mindset"—a concept popularized by designer Susan Kare, who once said:

"A collection is not just a group of objects; it’s a conversation between the past, present, and future."

Jennifer Gilbert’s Collecting Philosophy: 10 Actionable Strategies

Gilbert’s method is deliberate, selective, and deeply personal. Below, we dissect her 10 core strategies—each backed by real-world examples and practical tips for implementation.

1. Define Your "Why" Before You Start

Gilbert’s first rule: "If you don’t know why you’re collecting, you’ll end up with a cluttered closet." Before acquiring anything, ask:

  • What emotion or memory does this object represent?
  • How does it align with my personal or professional identity?
  • Will I use, display, or interact with it meaningfully?

Example: Gilbert’s collection of mid-century modern lamps isn’t just about aesthetics—each piece was chosen for its ergonomic innovation (e.g., a George Nelson "Bubble Lamp" for its playful yet functional design) or its connection to a designer she admires.

Actionable Tip:

  • Write a one-page manifesto outlining:
    • Your collecting goals (e.g., "I collect objects that inspire my work").
    • Your budget and constraints (e.g., "I’ll spend no more than $500 per item").
    • Your display philosophy (e.g., "I’ll rotate pieces seasonally").

2. Prioritize "Slow Collecting" Over Impulse Buys

In a 2023 study by McKinsey, 78% of impulse buyers later regretted their purchases, citing lack of alignment with personal values. Gilbert practices "slow collecting"—a method where she:

  • Waits 24–48 hours before making a purchase.
  • Researches the object’s history (e.g., provenance, materials, designer).
  • Considers its "lifespan"—will it hold value in 5, 10, or 20 years?

Example: Instead of buying a trendy ceramic vase that might go out of style, Gilbert sought out artisan-crafted pieces with timeless techniques, such as Japanese kintsugi (gold-repaired pottery) or Danish Funkis furniture.

Actionable Tip:

  • Implement the "10-10-10 Rule" (Suzy Welch):
    • How will I feel about this in 10 days? 10 months? 10 years?
  • Use a physical or digital "waiting list" (e.g., a notebook or Trello board) to track potential acquisitions.

3. Seek Objects with "Hidden Stories"

Gilbert believes the most compelling collections tell narratives. She avoids generic, mass-produced items in favor of pieces with:

  • Historical significance (e.g., a 1960s Eames chair with a known provenance).
  • Personal anecdotes (e.g., a travel mug from a place that changed her life).
  • Cultural context (e.g., a vintage African mask with tribal lore).

Example: Her collection includes a 19th-century French commode (a chest of drawers) that once belonged to a Parisian seamstress. The piece isn’t valuable for its rarity, but because it connects her to a craftswoman’s life.

Actionable Tip:

  • When acquiring an object, ask:
    • Who made it? Where did it come from? What was its original purpose?
  • Document the story in a collection journal (Gilbert uses a Leuchtturm1917 notebook).

4. Balance Rarity with Accessibility

While rare items can be exciting, Gilbert warns against overpaying for exclusivity. Instead, she looks for:

  • "Golden Ratio" objects—not too common, not too rare (e.g., a limited-edition ceramic by Bernard Leach).
  • Affordable alternatives with high emotional value (e.g., a handmade leather journal over a designer one).

Example: She owns a first-edition The Elements of Style by Strunk & White, but also a vintage typewriter from a local antique shop—both serve different purposes in her creative process.

Actionable Tip:

  • Use the "5-Year Test"—if you can’t imagine using or loving the object in five years, reconsider.
  • Explore lesser-known artisans (e.g., Etsy sellers, local potters, or indie designers) for unique finds.

5. Curate for "Mood" and Atmosphere

Gilbert’s spaces are intentional mood boards. She doesn’t just collect objects—she designs experiences. Her approach:

  • Color psychology (e.g., terracotta ceramics for warmth, black metal for contrast).
  • Textural contrast (e.g., pairing smooth marble with rough linen).
  • Functional beauty (e.g., a vintage radio that doubles as a decorative piece).

Example: Her home office features:

  • A 1930s Bakelite radio (for nostalgia).
  • A Japanese wabi-sabi stone lantern (for minimalist elegance).
  • A hand-painted Chinese porcelain bowl (for cultural depth).

Actionable Tip:

  • Before buying, visualize the object in your space. Ask:
    • Does it enhance the room’s energy?
    • Would I feel joy or discomfort around it?
  • Use digital tools like Roomstyler or Planner 5D to mock up arrangements.

6. Embrace "Anti-Collecting" (Curating Gaps)

Gilbert’s collection isn’t just about what she owns, but what she chooses not to own. She calls this "anti-collecting"—a strategy where she:

  • Declines trends (e.g., she avoids overly maximalist decor).
  • Lets go of duplicates (e.g., she sold a second Eames chair to make room for a unique Danish design).
  • Curates "negative space"—intentional emptiness in her home.

Example: She once owned three vintage clocks, but realized they competed for attention. She kept one (a 1920s French pendule) and donated the others to a local design museum.

Actionable Tip:

  • Conduct a "reverse audit"—ask:
    • What objects do I own that no longer serve me?
    • What gaps in my collection could I fill meaningfully?
  • Follow the "One In, One Out" rule (Marie Kondo-style).

7. Collect for "Future You"

Gilbert often asks: "Will this object matter to me in 10 years?" She curates for long-term meaning, not short-term satisfaction. Her strategies:

  • Invest in heirloom-quality pieces (e.g., hand-forged ironwork over cheap reproductions).
  • Document her collection (photos, notes, receipts) so future generations can understand its significance.
  • Choose objects with educational value (e.g., a vintage anatomy model for her design studio).

Example: She owns a **19th-century morphological skull collection (used for medical study), which she displays in her design research archive. It’s not decorative—it’s a tool for inspiration.

Actionable Tip:

  • Create a "Legacy Collection" folder (digital or physical) with:
    • Photos of each piece.
    • Its story, origin, and significance.
    • Any related documents (e.g., certificates of authenticity).

8. Mix High and Low Culture

Gilbert rejects the idea that collections must be either elite or everyday. Instead, she blends high art with everyday objects to create unexpected harmony. Examples:

  • A Dali-inspired ceramic teapot next to a vintage enamel mug.
  • A modern abstract painting above a handmade ceramic tile.
  • A vintage typewriter next to a contemporary sculpture.

Example: Her kitchen features:

  • A custom-made copper range hood (high-end).
  • A vintage enamel coffee pot (everyday, but with character).
  • A hand-painted Mexican talavera tile (cultural, not mass-produced).

Actionable Tip:

  • Pair one "statement piece" with three functional, low-key objects.
  • Rotate high/low pairings to keep your collection fresh.

9. Use Collecting as a Creative Tool

For Gilbert, collecting isn’t just a hobby—it’s a design research method. She uses her collection to:

  • Inspire problem-solving (e.g., studying vintage furniture joints for her own projects).
  • Test design principles (e.g., observing how light interacts with different materials).
  • Stay culturally relevant (e.g., collecting indigenous textiles to understand global design trends).

Example: She once recreated a 19th-century Japanese tokonoma (a small alcove for display) in her home, using objects from her collection to study composition and balance.

Actionable Tip:

  • Dedicate a "research corner" in your home or studio.
  • Take notes on how objects interact with light, space, and function.

10. Know When to Let Go

Even the most intentional collectors must edit their collections. Gilbert’s rules for releasing objects:

  • The "No Joy" Test – If an object doesn’t bring immediate joy, it’s time to go.
  • The "Space Test" – If it’s physically or mentally cluttering, donate or sell it.
  • The "Purpose Test" – If it no longer serves a functional or emotional role, reconsider.

Example: She once owned a vintage Persian rug that had no sentimental value—just aesthetic appeal. After years of not enjoying it, she sold it to fund a piece that truly resonated.

Actionable Tip:

  • Schedule a quarterly "edit session" where you:
    • Remove one object that no longer serves you.
    • Donate three items to a cause you believe in.
    • Sell one piece that could benefit someone else.

Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most passionate collectors make errors. Here are five pitfalls—and how Gilbert avoids them.

1. Collecting for Status, Not Meaning

Mistake: Buying expensive items to impress others rather than enrich your life. Gilbert’s Fix:

  • Ask: "Would I buy this if no one ever saw it?"
  • Focus on objects that make you feel something, not those that make others feel something.

2. Overcollecting Without a System

Mistake: Accumulating items without a clear vision, leading to visual chaos. Gilbert’s Fix:

  • Use the "Rule of Three"—limit collections to three categories (e.g., ceramics, books, vintage tools).
  • Display intentionally—group similar objects in curated clusters.

3. Ignoring Provenance and Ethics

Mistake: Buying controversial or unethically sourced items (e.g., looted art, conflict minerals). Gilbert’s Fix:

  • Research the origin of materials (e.g., fair-trade wood, recycled metals).
  • Support artisans and small businesses over mass producers.

4. Collecting Without a Budget

Mistake: Overspending on impulse buys, leading to financial stress. Gilbert’s Fix:

  • Set a monthly or yearly budget (e.g., "$200 per quarter").
  • Use the "24-Hour Rule"—wait before purchasing.

5. Failing to Enjoy the Process

Mistake: Treating collecting as a chore rather than a joyful activity. Gilbert’s Fix:

  • Make it social—join collecting groups, attend auctions, or visit museums.
  • Document your journey (a blog, Instagram, or journal).
