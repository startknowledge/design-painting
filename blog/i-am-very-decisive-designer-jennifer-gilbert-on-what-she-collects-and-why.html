Why Jennifer Gilbert’s Decisiveness Is a Game-Changer for Designers and Collectors

The Core Principles of Jennifer Gilbert’s Collection Philosophy

Gilbert’s method isn’t about luck—it’s about systems. Here are the five foundational principles that define her approach:

  1. The "One-In, One-Out" Rule – For every new acquisition, she removes something else. This ensures her collection grows in quality, not quantity.
  2. The "Why" Test – Every item must pass: "Does this serve a purpose beyond decoration?" (emotional, functional, or inspirational).
  3. The "First Impression" Standard – If an item doesn’t immediately elevate a space, it doesn’t stay.
  4. The "Legacy Edit" – She asks: "Will this still matter in 10 years?" (Avoiding fleeting trends.)
  5. The "Silent Room" Test – If the item feels unnecessary in a quiet moment, it’s not essential.

These principles aren’t just for collectors—they’re design thinking tools that can transform how you approach any project, from decorating a home to launching a brand.

8 Actionable Strategies to Collect Like Jennifer Gilbert

Now, let’s break down how to apply her philosophy to your own collecting (or curating) process.

1. Start with a "Core Collection" – The 20% That Makes the 80% Work

Gilbert believes in the Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule) applied to collecting. Instead of filling a space with many mediocre items, she focuses on a few exceptional pieces that anchor the aesthetic.

How to apply this:

  • Audit your current collection (or space). Identify the top 20% of items that bring you joy or serve a clear purpose.
  • Remove or relocate the rest—even if it’s painful. This creates negative space, making the remaining items feel more intentional and valuable.
  • Example: Gilbert once replaced a cluttered bookshelf with a single vintage leather-bound edition of a classic novel and a minimalist ceramic vase. The result? A focused, timeless display that feels curated by an expert.

Common mistake: Hoarding "just in case" items. Fix it: Ask, "Would I buy this again today?" If not, let it go.

2. Hunt for "Story Objects" – Items with Emotional or Historical Weight

Gilbert doesn’t collect decorative filler—she collects objects with stories. Whether it’s a handwritten letter from a loved one, a vintage tool from a family trade, or a one-of-a-kind art piece, these items add depth to a space.

How to apply this:

  • Ask yourself: "Does this item have a narrative?"
  • Seek out:
    • Family heirlooms (with provenance)
    • Art with a backstory (e.g., a painting by a local artist you met)
    • Books with marginalia (handwritten notes in the pages)
    • Vintage objects with a clear origin (e.g., a 1950s typewriter that once belonged to a writer)
  • Example: Gilbert once curated a gallery wall featuring:
    • A signed first edition of a literary classic
    • A family photograph from the 1920s in a custom-framed mat
    • A handmade ceramic bowl from a pottery workshop she attended The result? A wall that tells a story, not just decorates.

Common mistake: Buying "pretty" objects without meaning. Fix it: Before purchasing, write down why it matters to you.

3. Invest in "Slow Decor" – Timeless Over Trendy

Gilbert avoids fast fashion for interiors—she hates disposable decor. Instead, she seeks pieces that age gracefully, like:

  • Mid-century modern furniture (e.g., a Eames lounge chair)
  • Handcrafted ceramics (e.g., Studio Ceramics by Bernard Leach)
  • Vintage books (e.g., first editions in fine leather bindings)
  • Artisanal textiles (e.g., handwoven wool rugs)

How to apply this:

  • Research "slow decor" brands (e.g., Herman Miller, Vitra, or local artisans).
  • Avoid IKEA-style "throwaway" decor—opt for durable, heirloom-quality pieces.
  • Example: Instead of buying a cheap, trendy wallpaper, Gilbert chose a vintage linocut print from the 1930s that she found in a London antique shop. It outlasted multiple interior trends and became a focal point in her home.

Common mistake: Chasing Instagram-worthy trends that fade in a year. Fix it: Ask, "Will this still feel relevant in 5 years?"

4. Use the "Five-Year Test" Before Purchasing

Gilbert never buys on impulse. Instead, she waits at least five years before making a major acquisition. This ensures she only invests in what truly matters.

How to apply this:

  • Create a "wish list" for potential purchases and revisit it in 6 months.
  • If you still want it, then consider buying—but only if it passes the "Why" test.
  • Example: Gilbert once almost bought a modern sculpture at an auction. She put it on hold for a year, then realized she didn’t love it as much as she thought. She saved the money and later found a better piece that aligned with her vision.

Common mistake: Buying emotionally without logical justification. Fix it: Sleep on it—if you still want it after 24 hours, then reconsider.

5. Edit Ruthlessly – The "One-In, One-Out" Rule in Action

Gilbert’s home and studio never feel cluttered because she constantly edits. For every new acquisition, she removes something else.

How to apply this:

  • Before bringing in a new item, ask: "What’s one thing I can remove to make room?"
  • Use the "box test": If an item isn’t used or loved in a year, donate or sell it.
  • Example: Gilbert swapped out a bulky, outdated sofa for a mid-century modern armchair—but she donated the old sofa to a shelter instead of storing it. This kept her space light and intentional.

Common mistake: Accumulating "maybe" items that never get used. Fix it: Designate a "maybe box"—if you haven’t opened it in 3 months, let it go.

6. Curate for "Atmosphere" – Not Just Aesthetics

Gilbert doesn’t just decorate—she creates an atmosphere. Her spaces feel lived-in, inspired, and purposeful.

How to apply this:

  • Ask: "Does this item contribute to the mood I want?" (e.g., warm, serene, bold, intellectual)
  • Layer textures, materials, and colors to enhance the vibe.
  • Example: Gilbert’s study features:
    • A dark wood bookshelf (for depth)
    • A single vintage lamp (for warmth)
    • A handmade ceramic mug (for tactile interest) The result? A space that feels like a sanctuary, not just a room.

Common mistake: Over-relying on color schemes without textural contrast. Fix it: Mix matte, glossy, and natural materials for depth.

7. Seek Out "Hidden Gems" – The Anti-Gallerist Approach

Gilbert avoids mainstream galleries and instead hunts for unique finds—whether at flea markets, estate sales, or local maker fairs.

How to apply this:

  • Expand your search beyond high-end shops—check:
    • Estate sales (for one-of-a-kind vintage finds)
    • Local artisan markets (for handmade, ethical pieces)
    • Online platforms like Etsy or 1stDibs (for curated vintage)
  • Example: Gilbert once found a rare 19th-century pocket watch at a garage sale for $20. She restored it and now displays it as a conversation piece in her home.

Common mistake: Only buying from "approved" sources (e.g., only high-end galleries). Fix it: Explore unconventional places—you might find a treasure.

8. Document Your Collection – Make It a Living Archive

Gilbert doesn’t just collect—she preserves and shares. She keeps records of her acquisitions, takes notes, and even writes about them.

How to apply this:

  • Create a digital or physical archive of your collection:
    • Take photos of each item with context (where you found it, why you love it).
    • Write a short story about each piece (e.g., "This vase was a gift from my grandmother—she used it to hold fresh flowers every Sunday.").
  • Example: Gilbert maintains a handwritten journal where she describes each acquisition and how it fits into her vision. This helps her stay aligned with her goals.

Common mistake: Treating collections as disposable. Fix it: Treat them like a legacy—document, preserve, and pass on stories.

Real-World Examples of Jennifer Gilbert’s Curation in Action

Let’s walk through three specific spaces where Gilbert’s philosophy is evident.

1. The "Minimalist Library" – Where Books Become Art

Gilbert’s personal library isn’t just a storage space—it’s a curated gallery of ideas.

  • The Edit: She keeps only 200 books (down from 1,000) by selecting only those she’s read multiple times.
  • The Display: She arranges them by color and size, creating a visual rhythm rather than a random shelf.
  • The Story: Each book has a place of honor—whether it’s a first edition, a gift, or a reference she returns to often.
  • The Result: A space that feels intellectual and serene, not cluttered.

Takeaway: "A library should inspire, not overwhelm."

2. The "Vintage Workshop" – Where Tools Tell a Story

Gilbert’s workshop isn’t just a place to work—it’s a museum of craftsmanship.

  • The Edit: She keeps only tools she uses regularly (and restores the rest).
  • The Display: A single vintage lathe, a hand-forged hammer, and a leather-bound sketchbook sit on a reclaimed wood table.
  • The Story: Each tool has a history—some were passed down, others restored from disrepair.
  • The Result: A space that feels like a time capsule, not just a workshop.

Takeaway: "Every object should have a purpose—even if that purpose is memory."

3. The "Gallery Wall of Intentions" – Where Art Serves a Purpose

Gilbert’s gallery wall isn’t just decorative—it’s a visual manifesto.

  • The Edit: She keeps only 12 pieces (down from 30) by selecting those that align with her values.
  • The Display: A mix of art, photography, and ephemera—including:
    • A hand-drawn map of a place she loves
    • A vintage poster from a concert she attended
    • A childhood sketch she still admires
  • The Story: Each piece represents a moment, a person, or an idea she wants to hold onto.
  • The Result: A wall that tells her life story, not just decorates a room.

Takeaway: "A collection should reflect who you are—not who you wish you were."
