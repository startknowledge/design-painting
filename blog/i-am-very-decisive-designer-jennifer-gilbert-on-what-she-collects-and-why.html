"‘I Am Very Decisive’: Designer Jennifer Gilbert on What She Collects and Why"

A Deep Dive into Intentional Collecting, Design Philosophy, and the Art of Curating Meaningful Objects

Introduction: The Rise of Intentional Collecting in Modern Design

In an era where consumption habits are evolving, more people are shifting from mindless accumulation to mindful collecting—a practice rooted in intentionality, emotional resonance, and aesthetic purpose. According to a 2023 report by McKinsey & Company, 68% of millennials and Gen Z consumers now prioritize meaningful ownership over disposable goods, with 42% actively curating collections that reflect their identity, values, or passions.

One of the most compelling voices in this movement is designer Jennifer Gilbert, whose work and personal collecting philosophy blend functional design, emotional storytelling, and a deep appreciation for craftsmanship. In a recent interview with The New York Times, Gilbert shared her approach to collecting, emphasizing decision-making, authenticity, and the power of objects to shape our lives. Her perspective isn’t just about aesthetics—it’s about how we engage with the world through what we surround ourselves with.

This post explores:

  • Why Jennifer Gilbert’s collecting philosophy matters in today’s design landscape.
  • The psychology behind intentional collecting and how it differs from hoarding or clutter.
  • 8 actionable strategies to build a meaningful collection, inspired by Gilbert’s methods.
  • Real-world examples of curated collections that tell a story.
  • Common mistakes in collecting and how to avoid them.
  • FAQs to help you refine your own approach.

By the end, you’ll have a clear, structured framework for collecting objects that enhance your life, inspire your creativity, and reflect your unique perspective.

Why Jennifer Gilbert’s Approach to Collecting Stands Out

Jennifer Gilbert, a London-based designer known for her work in furniture, ceramics, and spatial design, doesn’t just collect objects—she curates experiences. Her philosophy aligns with the growing trend of "slow living", where objects are chosen not for fleeting trends but for their ability to evoke memory, emotion, and purpose.

Key Principles Behind Her Collecting Philosophy

  1. Decisiveness Over Indecision Gilbert has stated that she is "very decisive" when it comes to collecting. Unlike many who struggle with "collection paralysis" (the fear of making the wrong choice), she follows a clear, intentional process:

    • Does this object serve a purpose? (Functional or emotional)
    • Does it align with my aesthetic or values?
    • Will I use it, love it, or pass it on?

    This mindset prevents clutter and ensures that every piece in her collection earns its place.

  2. A Focus on Craftsmanship and Storytelling Gilbert prioritizes handmade, artisanal, and culturally significant objects. Whether it’s a Japanese wabi-sabi teacup or a mid-century Danish chair, she seeks pieces that carry a history, a maker, or a narrative.

    "An object without a story is just a thing," she once remarked. "But when it has a past, it becomes part of your own."

  3. The Role of Emotional Connection She doesn’t collect for status or trends—she collects for how an object makes her feel. A vintage French lamp might remind her of a trip to Provence, or a handwoven Moroccan rug might evoke the warmth of a friend’s home.

    This emotional anchor is what makes collections lasting, not just temporary.

  4. Curating, Not Hoarding Gilbert’s approach is selective and intentional. She doesn’t fill spaces with objects just because they’re "pretty"—she thoughtfully arranges them to create harmony and meaning.

    This is where she diverges from collectors who hoard, who often accumulate without purpose or organization.

The Psychology of Intentional Collecting: Why It Works

Before diving into strategies, it’s helpful to understand why intentional collecting resonates so deeply.

1. The Dopamine of Meaningful Ownership

Studies in neuroeconomics (e.g., a 2021 Journal of Consumer Psychology study) show that owning objects tied to personal meaning triggers higher dopamine levels than owning trendy, disposable items. This is why:

  • A grandmother’s silver spoon feels more valuable than a mass-produced kitchen gadget.
  • A first edition book holds more emotional weight than a new paperback.

2. The Reduction of Decision Fatigue

Psychologist Barry Schwartz (author of The Paradox of Choice) argues that too many options lead to paralysis. Gilbert’s decision-first approach reduces this by:

  • Limiting choices (e.g., only buying from trusted makers).
  • Setting clear criteria (e.g., "I only collect objects that tell a story").
  • Accepting imperfection (not every piece needs to be "perfect").

3. The Therapeutic Power of Curating

Research from the University of California (2020) found that organizing and curating physical spaces reduces stress by 23%. Gilbert’s method turns collecting into a creative, meditative practice—not a chore.

8 Actionable Strategies to Build a Meaningful Collection (Inspired by Jennifer Gilbert)

Now, let’s break down how you can apply Gilbert’s philosophy to your own collecting journey.

Strategy 1: Define Your "Why" Before You Start

Gilbert doesn’t collect randomly—she starts with intent. Ask yourself:

  • What does this collection represent? (e.g., travel, craftsmanship, nostalgia)
  • How will it enhance my life? (e.g., daily inspiration, conversation starter, emotional comfort)
  • What values does it align with? (e.g., sustainability, artisanal, vintage)

Example: If you love travel, your collection might include:

  • A postcard from every country you’ve visited.
  • A vintage suitcase that holds mementos.
  • Local ceramics from each place you’ve stayed.

Action Step: Write a one-sentence mission statement for your collection. Example: "I collect handmade ceramics from around the world to celebrate cultural craftsmanship and the stories behind each piece."

Strategy 2: Set Hard Limits to Avoid Clutter

Gilbert is disciplined about space. She doesn’t fill her home with too many objects—she curates intentionally.

How to Apply This:

  • The 12-Item Rule: For a small space (e.g., a shelf), limit yourself to 12 items max. If you add one, you must remove another.
  • The "Would I Buy This Again?" Test: If you wouldn’t repurchase it today, it doesn’t belong.
  • Designate "No-Go" Zones: Certain areas (e.g., your bed, dining table) should remain clutter-free.

Real-World Example: A friend of mine collects vintage typewriters. Instead of letting them pile up, she:

  • Displays 3 on a wall (each with a small plaque explaining its history).
  • Stores the rest in a closet, rotating them every few months.
  • Sells duplicates to keep her collection manageable and meaningful.

Strategy 3: Prioritize Objects with a Story

Gilbert seeks objects with heritage, craftsmanship, or personal significance. Here’s how to find them:

Where to Look:

  • Flea markets & antique shops (for hidden gems with history).
  • Local artisans & makers (supporting craftsmanship).
  • Family heirlooms & secondhand stores (for emotional weight).
  • Online platforms like Etsy or 1stDibs (for curated, meaningful pieces).

How to Spot a Story:

  • Look for imperfections (a chipped teacup from 1920, a hand-sewn quilt with patches).
  • Ask about the maker (e.g., "Who made this? Where was it made?").
  • Consider the object’s journey (e.g., a WWII-era compass passed down through generations).

Action Step: Before buying, ask: ✅ "Does this object have a past?" ✅ "Can I imagine it in my future?" ✅ "Does it make me feel something?"

Strategy 4: Follow the "One In, One Out" Rule

Gilbert doesn’t believe in endless accumulation. She follows a strict exchange system:

  • For every new object, she removes an old one.
  • If she can’t part with anything, she expands her space (e.g., adds a shelf).

Why It Works:

  • Prevents clutter buildup.
  • Keeps your collection fresh and evolving.
  • Encourages mindful consumption.

How to Implement:

  • Start small: If you collect books, donate one for every new book.
  • Use a "waiting period": If you impulse-buy, wait 30 days before deciding if it stays.
  • Sell or donate duplicates (e.g., if you have three identical vases, keep one).

Strategy 5: Curate for Functionality, Not Just Aesthetics

Gilbert’s collections serve a purpose. Whether it’s a daily-used ceramic mug or a display piece that sparks conversation, every object has a role.

How to Apply This:

  • Ask: "Will I use this daily, weekly, or only occasionally?"
  • Mix display pieces with functional items (e.g., a beautiful tray that holds keys + a book).
  • Consider multi-functional objects (e.g., a ladle that doubles as a decorative piece).

Real-World Example: A Japanese designer collects bamboo kitchen tools—not just for cooking, but because:

  • The handcrafted chopsticks feel luxurious.
  • The wooden rice paddle has generational significance.
  • They enhance the dining experience, making meals more intentional.

Strategy 6: Embrace Imperfection (Wabi-Sabi Mindset)

Gilbert appreciates flaws in objects—they tell a story of use, time, and care.

What is Wabi-Sabi? A Japanese aesthetic that celebrates: ✔ Imperfection (chips, cracks, wear). ✔ Impermanence (objects change over time). ✔ Incompleteness (nothing is "perfect").

How to Apply This:

  • Seek "characterful" objects (e.g., a vintage leather chair with faded upholstery).
  • Repurpose broken items (e.g., turn an old wooden crate into a shelf).
  • Accept that some pieces will age beautifully (e.g., a handmade ceramic vase that develops patina over years).

Action Step: Next time you’re shopping, look for objects with "history"—not just "perfection."

Strategy 7: Create a "Collection Journal"

Gilbert doesn’t just collect—she documents. Keeping a journal helps you:

  • Track acquisitions (what you bought, why, where).
  • Reflect on emotional connections.
  • Plan future additions.

What to Include:

  • Photos (if digital) or sketches of each piece.
  • The story behind it (who made it? where was it found?).
  • How it fits into your space.
  • Why you love it.

Example Entry: "Found this 1950s Danish teak chair at a flea market. The seller said it was in a family’s home since the 1960s. The grain of the wood is beautiful, and it reminds me of my grandmother’s old chairs. I’ll place it in my reading nook—it feels like a piece of history now."

Strategy 8: Share Your Collection (It Grows with Connection)

Gilbert believes collections thrive when shared. Whether through:

  • Hosting a "collection night" with friends.
  • Writing about it (like this blog post!).
  • Donating to a museum or archive (if it has historical value).

Why It Works:

  • Strengthens your relationship with the objects.
  • Creates community around your passions.
  • Keeps your collection dynamic.

Action Step:

  • Invite someone to see your collection and ask for their thoughts.
  • Start a blog or Instagram series documenting your journey.
  • Donate a piece that no longer resonates—passing it on gives it new life.
