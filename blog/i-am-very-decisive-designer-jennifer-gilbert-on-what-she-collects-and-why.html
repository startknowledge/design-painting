"‘I Am Very Decisive’: Designer Jennifer Gilbert on What She Collects and Why"

A Deep Dive into the Mind of a Visionary Collector and How Her Approach Can Transform Your Own Design Aesthetic

Introduction: The Rise of Intentional Collecting in Modern Design

In an era where 78% of millennials and Gen Z consumers prioritize personalization in their purchases (McKinsey, 2023), the way we collect objects—whether for functional, emotional, or artistic value—has evolved beyond mere accumulation. Jennifer Gilbert, a celebrated designer whose work bridges contemporary aesthetics with curated collections, embodies this shift. Her approach to collecting isn’t just about acquiring items; it’s about intentionality, narrative, and the power of objects to shape identity.

Gilbert’s philosophy, as articulated in her interviews and design projects, reveals a methodical yet intuitive process: "I am very decisive," she once said, "because every piece I bring into my space must serve a purpose—whether it’s a spark of conversation, a nod to history, or a piece of my own evolution." This statement isn’t just a personal quirk; it’s a blueprint for how designers, collectors, and enthusiasts can curate spaces that reflect their values, stories, and future aspirations.

This post explores why Jennifer Gilbert’s collecting habits matter, how her strategies can inspire your own design journey, and practical steps to build a collection that feels as intentional as it does beautiful. Whether you’re a designer, a homeowner, or simply someone who loves the art of gathering, this guide will help you avoid common pitfalls and create a collection that stands the test of time.

Why Jennifer Gilbert’s Collecting Philosophy Matters

Before diving into actionable strategies, it’s essential to understand why Gilbert’s approach resonates so deeply in today’s design landscape. Her method isn’t about chasing trends or filling spaces with "pretty" objects—it’s about meaningful engagement with the world around us.

1. The Psychology of Intentional Collecting

Research from the Journal of Consumer Psychology (2022) suggests that people who collect with purpose experience higher levels of satisfaction and emotional connection to their belongings. Gilbert’s decisiveness aligns with this finding: she doesn’t hoard; she selects.

  • Emotional resonance: Each item in her collection tells a story—whether it’s a vintage lamp that reminds her of a trip to Paris or a modern sculpture that reflects her love for abstract art.
  • Functional harmony: Unlike many collectors who treat their spaces as museums, Gilbert ensures her items serve a dual purpose—they’re beautiful and useful.
  • Future-proofing: She avoids fleeting trends, opting instead for timeless pieces that evolve with her tastes.

2. The Business of Curated Collections

Gilbert’s approach isn’t just personal—it’s commercially savvy. In an industry where 63% of consumers are willing to pay more for brands with a clear story (Forbes, 2023), her method of weaving narrative into design is a masterclass in branding.

  • Storytelling through objects: Whether she’s designing a client’s home or curating her own space, Gilbert ensures every item has a backstory. This creates a rich, immersive experience for viewers.
  • Limited-edition appeal: By favoring unique, one-of-a-kind pieces over mass-produced decor, she taps into the desire for exclusivity—a key driver in luxury markets.
  • Sustainability as a selling point: Her preference for vintage, repurposed, or ethically sourced items aligns with the growing demand for eco-conscious design (Nielsen, 2021).

3. The Influence of Jennifer Gilbert’s Work

While Gilbert is best known for her interior design and furniture collaborations, her collecting habits have subtly shaped broader design trends:

  • The rise of "slow decorating": Instead of rapid turnover, many designers now advocate for long-term, meaningful collections.
  • Hybrid aesthetics: Gilbert’s ability to blend industrial, organic, and minimalist elements has inspired a wave of eclectic yet cohesive design movements.
  • The democratization of luxury: By focusing on quality over quantity, she’s made high-end design more accessible to those who appreciate craftsmanship without the price tag.

10 Actionable Strategies to Build a Collection Like Jennifer Gilbert

Now that we understand why Gilbert’s approach works, let’s break down how you can apply her principles to your own collecting and design journey.

Strategy 1: Define Your "Why" Before You Start

Gilbert doesn’t collect randomly—she starts with a clear intention. Ask yourself:

  • What emotions or memories do I want my collection to evoke?
  • Does this piece align with my personal or professional identity?
  • Will this item still resonate with me in five years?

Real-world example: A client of Gilbert’s wanted a collection that reflected her love for travel and history. Instead of filling her living room with generic decor, she curated:

  • A vintage globe from the 1920s (purchased at an antique fair).
  • A hand-painted map of her favorite cities.
  • A collection of postcards from her travels, displayed in a custom wooden frame.

Result: The space became a visual diary of her life, not just a room.

Common mistake: Buying items "just because they’re pretty." Fix it: Write a manifesto—a few sentences on what your collection represents.

Strategy 2: Master the Art of the "One-In, One-Out" Rule

Gilbert is disciplined about space. She follows a strict rule: For every new item, she removes an old one. This prevents clutter and ensures her collection remains intentional.

How to implement it:

  • Before adding a new piece, physically remove an existing item that no longer serves its purpose.
  • Use the "12-month test": If you haven’t used or loved an item in a year, consider donating or selling it.

Real-world example: A friend of Gilbert’s struggled with a growing collection of thrifted vases. She realized most were visually similar and didn’t contribute to the room’s narrative. By applying the rule, she swapped out duplicates for a single statement piece—a hand-blown glass vase from a local artisan—which became the room’s focal point.

Common mistake: Letting "maybe someday" items linger. Fix it: Set a deadline—e.g., "If I don’t love this in 30 days, it’s gone."

Strategy 3: Seek Out "Story Objects"

Gilbert believes the best collections tell a story. These aren’t just decorative—they’re narrative anchors.

How to find them:

  • Thrift stores & flea markets: Look for items with visible wear, unique labels, or historical significance.
  • Local artisans & makers: Support creators who can personalize pieces for you.
  • Secondhand platforms: Etsy, eBay, and Facebook Marketplace often have one-of-a-kind finds.

Real-world example: A client wanted a dining room centerpiece that reflected her family’s heritage. Gilbert suggested:

  • A family recipe book from the 1950s (found at a library sale).
  • A hand-carved wooden spoon from her grandmother’s kitchen.
  • A vintage china plate with her maiden name.

Result: The table became a conversation starter, not just a place to eat.

Common mistake: Choosing items based solely on aesthetics. Fix it: Ask "Why does this matter to me?" before buying.

Strategy 4: Mix High and Low (But Thoughtfully)

Gilbert’s spaces balance luxury and affordability. She believes in investing in key pieces while filling gaps with budget-friendly finds.

How to do it:

  • Identify your "anchor pieces" (e.g., a statement sofa, a vintage mirror).
  • Fill in with affordable, high-impact items (e.g., thrifted books, DIY art, or small plants).

Real-world example: Gilbert designed a client’s apartment on a tight budget. She:

  • Invested in a custom-made sideboard (a one-time splurge).
  • Filled shelves with thrifted books, vintage postcards, and handmade ceramics (low-cost, high-impact).

Result: The space felt luxurious without being ostentatious.

Common mistake: Skimping on essentials to save money. Fix it: Prioritize quality over quantity—even in small purchases.

Strategy 5: Embrace "Slow Collecting"

In a world of fast fashion and disposable decor, Gilbert advocates for slow collecting—a deliberate, patient approach.

How to practice it:

  • Set a collection goal (e.g., "I want to build a collection of 10 meaningful objects over two years").
  • Resist impulse buys—wait at least a week before purchasing.
  • Focus on experiences over objects (e.g., instead of buying a generic lamp, take a lighting design workshop).

Real-world example: A young designer friend wanted to build a collection of vintage cameras. Instead of buying multiple at once, she:

  • Joined a photography club to learn about lenses.
  • Attended auctions slowly, adding one camera per month.
  • Repurposed old cameras into wall art when they became obsolete.

Result: Her collection grew organically and meaningfully.

Common mistake: Collecting too quickly, leading to regret or clutter. Fix it: Slow down—your future self will thank you.

Strategy 6: Curate by "Themes" (Not Just Aesthetics)

Gilbert organizes her collection by themes—not just colors or styles. This creates cohesion and depth.

Theme ideas:

  • Travel: Maps, postcards, vintage suitcases.
  • Nature: Pressed flowers, driftwood, botanical prints.
  • Technology: Old typewriters, retro electronics, sci-fi books.
  • Craftsmanship: Hand-forged tools, embroidered textiles, woodwork.

Real-world example: A client wanted a home office that reflected her love for writing. Gilbert curated:

  • A vintage typewriter (restored and displayed).
  • A collection of first editions of her favorite books.
  • A handmade leather-bound journal (a gift from a mentor).

Result: The space became a sanctuary for creativity, not just a workspace.

Common mistake: Collecting randomly without a unifying theme. Fix it: Pick 1-3 themes and stick to them.

Strategy 7: Invest in "Multi-Functional" Pieces

Gilbert’s collections serve a purpose beyond decoration. Many items are functional, interactive, or experiential.

Examples:

  • A coffee table that doubles as a chalkboard.
  • A bookshelf with hidden storage.
  • A wall-mounted bike rack that also holds plants.

Real-world example: Gilbert designed a small apartment where space was limited. She included:

  • A fold-down desk that transformed into a dining table.
  • A bench with storage for coats and shoes.
  • A mirror that also functioned as a TV stand.

Result: The space was both beautiful and practical.

Common mistake: Buying decor that doesn’t serve a real need. Fix it: Ask "How will this improve my life?"

Strategy 8: Rotate Your Collection Seasonally

Gilbert keeps her collection dynamic by rotating items. This prevents stagnation and keeps her space fresh.

How to rotate:

  • Store off-season items (e.g., winter blankets in summer).
  • Swap out decor every few months (e.g., seasonal centerpieces).
  • Host "collection parties" where friends can see new arrangements.

Real-world example: A client had a collection of vintage cameras. Instead of displaying all at once, Gilbert suggested:

  • Rotating 3-4 cameras at a time in a gallery wall.
  • Using the others for photography projects (e.g., shooting film with them).
  • Storing the rest in a climate-controlled box to preserve them.

Result: The collection felt new and exciting every time she entered the room.

Common mistake: Keeping everything out all the time. Fix it: Edit regularly—like a fashion closet.

Strategy 9: Learn from the Masters (But Make It Your Own)

Gilbert doesn’t just admire other collectors—she studies them and adapts their styles to her own taste.

How to do it:

  • Visit museums, galleries, and antique shops to inspire you.
  • Follow designers and collectors on Instagram/Pinterest (but don’t copy—interpret).
  • Experiment with "mood boards" to visualize your ideal collection.

Real-world example: Gilbert admired the minimalist collections of Japanese designers but wanted to add warmth and texture. She:

  • Kept the clean lines of minimalism.
  • Added handwoven textiles, ceramic vases, and natural wood for softness.
  • Used a neutral palette but incorporated one bold accent color (e.g., terracotta).

Result: A unique hybrid style that was distinctly hers.

Common mistake: Trying to replicate someone else’s aesthetic. Fix it: Take inspiration, not imitation.

Strategy 10: Document Your Collection

Gilbert keeps a journal of her collection, noting where each piece came from, why she loved it, and how it evolved in her space.

How to document:

  • Take photos of each item before and after placement.
  • Write a short story about each piece (e.g., "This lamp was a gift from my grandmother; she used it in her studio").
  • Update your collection "bible" every few months.

Real-world example: A client wanted to build a collection of vintage records. Gilbert suggested:

  • Taking photos of each sleeve before display.
  • Noting the artist and year in a ledger.
  • Creating a playlist that matched the records’ vibe.

Result: The collection became a living archive, not just a decorative display.

Common mistake: Forgetting the story behind your items. Fix it: Keep a record—your future self will appreciate it.

Common Mistakes in Collecting (And How to Avoid Them)

Even the most intentional collectors make mistakes. Here’s how to spot and fix the most common pitfalls:

Mistake 1: Collecting Without a Plan

Problem: Buying items "because they’re cute" leads to disorganized, emotionally draining collections. Solution: Define your "why" first (see Strategy 1).

Mistake 2: Overvaluing Trends Over Timelessness

Problem: Chasing fast fashion decor trends (e.g., maximalism, hyper-minimalism) can make your space feel dated quickly. Solution: Invest in neutral, high-quality basics and use trends as accents.

Mistake 3: Hoarding "Maybe Somedays"

Problem: Keeping items just in case clutters your space and mind. Solution: Apply the "one-in, one-out" rule (Strategy 2).

Mistake 4: Ignoring Functionality

Problem: Decor that doesn’t serve a purpose feels like a burden. Solution: Ask "How does this improve my life?" (Strategy 7).
