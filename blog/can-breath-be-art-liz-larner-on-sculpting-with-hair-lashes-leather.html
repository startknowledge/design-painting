Can Breath Be Art? Liz Larner’s Radical Approach to Sculpting with Hair, Lashes, and Leather

Imagine stepping into a gallery where the air itself feels like a work of art. Where the scent of leather mingles with the delicate texture of human hair, and where every piece seems to breathe—literally. This isn’t a scene from a futuristic film; it’s the world of Liz Larner, a contemporary artist whose work challenges the very definition of sculpture. For Larner, art isn’t confined to marble or bronze. Instead, she sculpts with hair, lashes, leather, and even breath, transforming ephemeral and often overlooked materials into profound, immersive experiences.

Larner’s practice, which spans the late 1990s to the present, has earned her recognition as a pioneer in conceptual and material-based art. Her work has been exhibited at prestigious institutions like the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), the Tate Modern, and the Whitney Museum of American Art. Yet, despite her acclaim, Larner’s work remains unclassifiable—it defies easy categorization, blending sculpture, installation, performance, and even biological art.

So, how does an artist redefine sculpture using materials that are typically associated with fashion, intimacy, or craft? And why does Larner insist that breath is an artistic medium? This article dives deep into Larner’s innovative approach, exploring her techniques, influences, and the broader implications of her work for contemporary art. Whether you're an artist, a collector, or simply curious about the boundaries of creativity, this exploration will challenge your understanding of what art can be.

The Evolution of Liz Larner’s Practice: From Hair to Breath

Liz Larner’s journey into art began in the late 1990s, a time when postmodernism was giving way to a more experimental, material-driven art scene. Unlike many of her contemporaries who focused on digital or conceptual works, Larner was drawn to the tactile and biological. Her early works often featured human hair—a material rich in cultural and personal significance—used to create sculptures that were at once beautiful and unsettling.

In her 2001 exhibition at the PS1 Contemporary Art Center, Larner presented a series of works titled "Hair Works". These pieces consisted of thousands of human hairs meticulously arranged into delicate, almost organic forms. The hairs were sourced from donors, each strand carrying its own history and meaning. Larner’s process involved washing, drying, and weaving the hairs into intricate patterns, often incorporating leather straps or fabric to give structure to the ephemeral material.

What made these works striking was not just their visual appeal but their tactile and sensory experience. Viewers were encouraged to touch the hair, to feel its texture, and to consider the ethical and personal implications of using human hair as art. This early work laid the foundation for Larner’s later explorations into breath, sound, and even the human body itself.

Breath as a Medium: The Radical Shift

By the mid-2000s, Larner began experimenting with breath as an artistic medium. In her 2007 installation "Breathing", displayed at the Saatchi Gallery, she created a space where viewers were invited to inhale and exhale through a series of tubes and filters. The installation was designed to visually and physically alter the air around the viewer, making them an active participant in the artwork.

Larner’s use of breath was not merely symbolic; it was a physical and conceptual intervention. She argued that breath is both a biological and an artistic act, one that connects the viewer to the materiality of their own body. This work was part of a broader trend in contemporary art where artists began to explore the body as both subject and medium, a movement that included figures like Marcel Duchamp and Vincent Wanloo.

Larner’s approach to breath was also influenced by her interest in science and biology. She often collaborated with researchers and scientists to explore how breath could be measured, recorded, and transformed into artistic forms. For example, in her 2012 project "Respiration", she worked with a team to capture and analyze the breath patterns of viewers, then used this data to generate soundscapes and visual projections. The result was an immersive experience where the viewer’s breath became part of the artwork itself.

Lashes and Leather: The Intersection of Craft and Concept

While hair and breath were central to Larner’s practice, she also incorporated lashes and leather into her work, further blurring the lines between craft, fashion, and fine art.

In her 2015 series "Lash Works", Larner used human eyelashes—another material often associated with intimacy and beauty—to create delicate, almost fragile sculptures. Like her hair works, these pieces were highly tactile, inviting viewers to consider the ethical and aesthetic implications of using such personal materials. Larner often sourced lashes from donors, much like she did with hair, creating a sense of shared experience and collective identity.

Leather, on the other hand, represented a shift toward durability and structure. In works like "Leather Breath" (2018), Larner combined leather straps, breath tubes, and human hair to create installations that were both sturdy and ephemeral. The leather provided a physical framework, while the hair and breath added layers of sensory and conceptual depth. This juxtaposition highlighted Larner’s ability to balance opposing forces—the permanent and the transient, the biological and the man-made.

Why Hair, Lashes, and Leather? The Philosophy Behind the Materials

Larner’s choice of materials is not arbitrary; each carries deep cultural, biological, and philosophical significance. Understanding why she selects these materials helps us grasp the broader message of her work.

1. Hair: The Story of the Self

Human hair is one of the most personally and culturally charged materials in existence. It carries memories, identities, and histories. For Larner, hair is more than just a biological byproduct—it’s a vehicle for storytelling.

In her 2003 work "Hair Stories", Larner collected hair from donors and wove it into tapestries that told fragmented narratives. Each strand represented a unique individual, and the collective work became a metaphor for community and shared experience. This approach aligns with Larner’s broader interest in how art can mediate personal and collective identities.

Additionally, hair is ephemeral yet enduring. It grows, falls out, and regenerates, much like the cycles of life itself. Larner’s use of hair forces viewers to confront the transience of existence while also celebrating the resilience of the human form.

2. Lashes: The Fragility of Beauty

Eyelashes, like hair, are highly personal and symbolic. They are often associated with femininity, beauty, and vulnerability. Larner’s use of lashes in her work challenges these associations, recontextualizing them as artistic materials.

In "Lash Fields" (2016), Larner arranged lashes into delicate, almost floral patterns. The fragility of the material contrasted with the precision of the arrangement, creating a sense of tension between beauty and impermanence. This work also touched on themes of gender and identity, as lashes are often tied to cosmetic and cultural expectations.

By using lashes, Larner invites viewers to question the narratives surrounding beauty and to see these materials as legitimate artistic mediums rather than just tools of adornment.

3. Leather: The Weight of History

Leather is a material steeped in history and craftsmanship. It has been used for centuries in clothing, furniture, and tools, carrying with it the marks of human labor and industry. For Larner, leather represents durability and structure, providing a counterpoint to the ephemeral nature of hair and breath.

In works like "Leather Breath", Larner combined leather with other materials to create installations that were both functional and conceptual. The leather straps held the breath tubes in place, while the hair and lashes added layers of sensory and emotional depth. This juxtaposition highlighted Larner’s ability to integrate opposing forces—the permanent and the transient, the biological and the man-made.

Additionally, leather is a material that ages and changes over time. It develops patinas, cracks, and textures that tell a story of its own. Larner’s use of leather in her work reflects her interest in how materials evolve and carry meaning over time.

Technical Approaches: How Does Liz Larner Sculpt with Breath, Hair, and Leather?

Larner’s work is not just conceptual; it’s also highly technical. Each material requires a unique approach, and her methods are as innovative as her ideas. Below, we explore the practical techniques she employs to transform these materials into art.

1. Working with Hair

Larner’s process with hair involves several key steps:

  1. Collection: She sources hair from donors, often through public calls or personal networks. Each strand is treated as a unique element with its own history.
  2. Cleaning and Preparation: The hair is washed, dried, and sometimes bleached or dyed to standardize its appearance. This step ensures that the material is consistent and ready for sculpting.
  3. Weaving and Arrangement: Larner uses traditional weaving techniques, often incorporating leather straps or fabric to give structure to the hair. She arranges the strands into delicate patterns, tapestries, or three-dimensional forms.
  4. Installation: The final piece is often displayed in a way that encourages interaction. Viewers are invited to touch, smell, and engage with the hair, making the artwork a sensory experience.

One of Larner’s most famous hair works is "Hair Fields" (2005), where she created a massive, immersive installation using thousands of strands of hair. The piece was designed to fill a gallery space with a sea of hair, creating a sensory and emotional experience for viewers.

Larner’s approach to breath is both conceptual and technical. She often collaborates with engineers and scientists to develop systems that capture, filter, and transform breath into artistic forms.
