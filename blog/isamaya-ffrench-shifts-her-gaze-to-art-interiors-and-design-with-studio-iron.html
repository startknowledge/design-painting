Who Is Isamaya Ffrench? A Brief Overview

Before diving into her collaboration with Studio Iron, it’s essential to understand who Isamaya Ffrench is and what makes her approach to design uniquely compelling.

The Early Years: From Fashion to Cultural Curator

Isamaya Ffrench began her career in fashion and editorial design, working with publications like Vogue and Wallpaper. Her work was characterized by bold typography, vibrant colors, and a deep appreciation for global cultures. This background shaped her visual sensibility, making her a natural fit for design spaces that tell stories.

The Shift Toward Interiors and Art

By the mid-2010s, Ffrench transitioned into interior design and art direction, collaborating with brands like Selfridges, MoMA Design Store, and even designing private residences. Her projects often featured:

  • Eclectic, layered aesthetics (think African textiles, Japanese minimalism, and Art Deco accents)
  • A focus on cultural narratives—each piece or room was meant to evoke a feeling or a memory
  • Art as a central element—she believed that great interiors should feel like galleries

The Studio Iron Collaboration: A New Chapter

In 2023, Ffrench partnered with Studio Iron, a London-based studio founded by James Irvine and Tom Dixon, to create a series of residential projects that merge art, architecture, and interior design. This collaboration was not just about aesthetics—it was about philosophy.

Why Studio Iron?

  • Studio Iron’s signature style combines industrial raw materials with sculptural art, creating spaces that feel both rugged and refined.
  • Ffrench’s cultural depth added a layer of narrative and emotion that Studio Iron’s more utilitarian approach lacked.
  • Together, they created interiors that are as thought-provoking as they are beautiful.

The Core Principles Behind Isamaya Ffrench & Studio Iron’s Design Philosophy

Ffrench and Studio Iron’s work doesn’t follow a one-size-fits-all approach. Instead, it’s built on five foundational principles that make their spaces timeless yet dynamic:

1. Art as the Foundation, Not the Decoration

Most people treat art as a finishing touch—something to hang on the wall after the room is furnished. Ffrench and Studio Iron flip this script.

How They Do It:

  • Every piece of art is chosen for its emotional resonance—whether it’s a large-scale sculpture, a vintage poster, or a hand-painted mural.
  • Art is integrated into the architecture—think floating shelves displaying artworks, built-in niches for sculptures, or even art embedded in furniture.
  • The "art gallery" effect—their spaces are designed so that you feel like you’re walking through a curated exhibition.

Real-World Example: The London Townhouse Project One of their most celebrated projects was a restoration of a 19th-century London townhouse. Instead of treating the walls as blank canvases, they:

  • Installed a series of custom-built alcoves that held modern and contemporary artworks, creating a dynamic visual journey as you moved through the space.
  • Used a large, abstract mural by a local artist as the focal point of the living room, anchoring the room’s color palette.
  • Placed a collection of vintage African masks on a custom-designed floating shelf, turning a functional piece into a conversation starter.

Why It Works: This approach ensures that art isn’t just decoration—it’s the backbone of the room’s identity.

2. The Power of Layered Textures and Materials

Ffrench and Studio Iron reject the idea of a "clean" minimalist aesthetic. Instead, they believe in rich, tactile experiences.

How They Achieve This:

  • Mixing high and low materials—brass, concrete, wood, and even raw metal coexist in harmony.
  • Using natural textures—handwoven rugs, linen drapes, and stone surfaces add warmth.
  • Introducing unexpected contrasts—a sleek marble countertop next to a rough-hewn wooden table.

Real-World Example: The Coastal Villa in Portugal A recent project in Algarve, Portugal, featured:

  • **A living room with a massive, reclaimed wood beam ceiling, paired with whitewashed walls and a deep blue velvet sofa.
  • **A dining table made from polished concrete with hand-carved wooden legs, creating a modern yet rustic look.
  • **A gallery wall that combined vintage travel posters, abstract paintings, and a single large-scale sculpture—each piece chosen for its textural contrast.

Why It Works: This layering of materials makes spaces feel alive and multidimensional, preventing them from looking too sterile or predictable.

3. Cultural Narratives in Design

Ffrench’s background in global cultures shines through in her work. She believes that the best interiors tell stories.

How They Incorporate This:

  • Curating art from different regions—African textiles, Japanese ceramics, Indian block prints.
  • Using furniture with historical significance—a 19th-century Chinese cabinet, a Moroccan zellige tile fireplace.
  • Creating "themed" spaces—a library that feels like a Parisian salon, a bedroom inspired by a Japanese tea house.

Real-World Example: The New York Loft Renovation A former industrial loft in Brooklyn was transformed into a multicultural living space where:

  • **The entryway featured a large, hand-painted Indian door (a torana) as the main focal point.
  • **The kitchen had a custom-built cabinetry inspired by Japanese shoji screens, with sliding doors that revealed hidden storage.
  • **The bedroom walls were adorned with a mix of African adinkra prints and abstract European paintings, creating a visual dialogue.

Why It Works: This cultural fusion makes spaces feel personal and deeply meaningful, rather than just stylish.

4. Functional Art: Where Every Object Has Purpose

Ffrench and Studio Iron hate wasted space. Every piece in their designs serves a function—whether it’s practical or emotional.

How They Do This:

  • Multi-functional art—a sculpture that doubles as a bookshelf, a mirror that’s also a piece of abstract art.
  • Built-in storage that doubles as display—floating shelves with artworks that also hold books or decorative objects.
  • Furniture with hidden surprises—a sofa with a lift-top revealing a hidden bar, a coffee table that opens into a dining tray.

Real-World Example: The London Studio Apartment A compact London studio was designed to maximize space while keeping art central:

  • **The living room had a wall-mounted TV that could be hidden behind a large abstract painting when not in use.
  • **The kitchen island was a custom-designed piece with **open shelving that displayed both art and cookware.
  • **The bedroom featured a bed frame with built-in LED lighting, allowing artworks to be displayed above it without taking up wall space.

Why It Works: This functional art approach ensures that even small spaces feel expansive and intentional.

5. Lighting as the Silent Storyteller

Lighting is often underestimated in interior design, but Ffrench and Studio Iron treat it as a narrative device.

How They Use Light:

  • Dramatic chandeliers that cast long shadows (like a brass Art Deco piece in a dining room).
  • Warm, dimmable lighting to create intimate moods (think candles, salt lamps, and fabric shades).
  • Accent lighting to highlight artworks (like spotlights on a gallery wall).

Real-World Example: The Parisian Apartment A luxury Parisian apartment featured:

  • **A custom-designed chandelier with crystal droplets that refracted light into the room, creating a sparkling effect.
  • **Wall sconces that mimicked candlelight, casting a soft glow on the artworks below.
  • **A hidden LED strip behind a floating shelf, subtly illuminating the art displayed there.

Why It Works: Lighting sets the tone—it can make a space feel cozy, dramatic, or serene, depending on the intent.

8 Actionable Strategies to Blend Art and Design in Your Own Space

Inspired by Ffrench and Studio Iron’s approach, here are eight practical strategies to elevate your interiors with art and design:

1. Start with a "Blank Canvas" Wall

Before adding art, prepare your walls to make them ready for display.

  • Paint in a neutral, high-gloss finish (white, gray, or soft beige) to make colors pop.
  • Remove heavy furniture that blocks natural light (which dulls art).
  • Install floating shelves or ledges to display smaller pieces without damaging walls.

Pro Tip: If you’re renting, use removable wallpaper or peel-and-stick shelves to temporarily transform your space.

2. Invest in Large-Scale Art First

Ffrench and Studio Iron prioritize statement pieces—large paintings, sculptures, or murals—as the anchor of a room.

  • Choose one bold artwork and build the room around it (furniture, lighting, textiles).
  • If you can’t afford a large piece, create a gallery wall with mixed sizes and styles (but keep the color palette cohesive).

Example: Instead of buying a small framed print, invest in a single, impactful piece—like a vintage map, a bold abstract painting, or a sculpture—and arrange furniture to frame it.

3. Mix High and Low Art for Depth

Don’t just stick to gallery-quality pieces. Thrift stores, flea markets, and local artists can add unexpected charm.

  • Pair a high-end painting with a vintage poster, a handmade ceramic, or a found object.
  • Use art as a way to tell a story—maybe a collection of travel souvenirs, family heirlooms, or books that inspire you.

Example: A modern living room could feature:

  • A large abstract painting (high-end)
  • A vintage typewriter (mid-century)
  • A hand-painted ceramic bowl (artisan)
  • A collection of old postcards (personal touch)

4. Use Art to Define Zones in Open-Plan Spaces

If you have an open-plan home, art can help create distinct areas without walls.

  • Hang a large mirror or sculpture to visually separate the living room from the dining area.
  • Use different art styles in each zone (e.g., abstract in the living room, botanical prints in the kitchen).

Example: A loft apartment could have:

  • A bold mural in the living area (defines the social space)
  • A gallery wall of botanical illustrations in the kitchen (creates a cozy, nature-inspired zone)
  • A single large sculpture in the bedroom (makes it feel intimate and curated)

5. Incorporate Art into Furniture and Fixtures

Why stop at walls? Furniture and fixtures can be art too.

  • Choose a coffee table with a unique design (like a glass table with embedded sculptures).
  • Install a custom-built bookshelf that doubles as a display for art.
  • Use a statement light fixture (like a brass chandelier or a sculptural pendant lamp).

Example: A modern dining room could have:

  • A **table with a marble top and brass legs (functional art)
  • **A chandelier with geometric metal frames (visual interest)
  • **A sideboard with built-in shelves for art and glassware

6. Play with Scale and Proportion

Ffrench and Studio Iron love breaking rules—big art in small spaces, small art in large rooms.

  • In a tiny apartment, use large-scale art to make the room feel bigger.
  • In a grand space, smaller, delicate pieces can add intrigue.
  • Experiment with hanging heights—some art should be eye-level, some should be looked up at.

Example: A compact studio could have:

  • A large abstract painting (hanging above the bed to draw the eye upward)
  • Small, framed botanical prints (on floating shelves to add texture)
  • A sculpture on a low pedestal (to create a focal point in the corner)

7. Create a "Storytelling" Gallery Wall

Instead of a random collection, curate a gallery wall that tells a story.

  • Choose a theme (travel, nature, abstract, vintage).
  • Mix frames (some matte, some metallic) for visual interest.
  • Leave some negative space to let the art breathe.

Example: A bedroom gallery wall could feature:

  • A vintage travel poster (from Paris)
  • A hand-painted landscape (from Japan)
  • A modern abstract piece (for contrast)
  • A small, framed poem (personal touch)

Art isn’t just flat on walls—it can be sculptural, interactive, or even functional.
