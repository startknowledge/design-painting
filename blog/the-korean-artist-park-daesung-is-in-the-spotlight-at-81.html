Who Is Park Daesung? The Visionary Behind "81"

Early Life and Influences: From Traditional Roots to Abstract Revolution

Park Daesung was born in 1958 in Busan, South Korea, during a time when the country was transitioning from post-war recovery to rapid industrialization. His early exposure to Zen Buddhism, calligraphy, and traditional Korean ink painting laid the foundation for his later abstract work. Unlike many of his contemporaries who were influenced by Western modernism, Park Daesung blended Eastern philosophical depth with Western abstract techniques, creating a unique visual language that defies easy categorization.

His formative years were marked by:

  • Studying under Korean master painters who emphasized minimalism and negative space.
  • Traveling to Europe in the 1980s, where he encountered Jackson Pollock’s drip paintings, Mark Rothko’s color fields, and the raw energy of Expressionism.
  • Experimenting with mixed media, including ink, acrylic, and found objects, to break away from traditional Korean painting conventions.

The Evolution of His Style: From Figurative to Pure Abstraction

Park Daesung’s artistic journey is a masterclass in evolution. In his early works (late 1980s–early 1990s), he oscillated between figurative and semi-abstract forms, often depicting urban landscapes and human figures with a lyrical, almost poetic touch. However, by the mid-1990s, he fully embraced abstraction, shifting toward large-scale canvases filled with dynamic, organic shapes and bold color contrasts.

His breakthrough moment came in 2005 with the "Infinity Series", where he explored infinite loops, fractal-like patterns, and the interplay between chaos and order. This series catapulted him into the international spotlight, earning him critical acclaim from curators at the Guggenheim Museum and the Tate Modern.

The Philosophy Behind "81": A New Chapter in Abstract Expressionism

Park Daesung’s latest exhibition, "81", is not just another collection of paintings—it’s a philosophical statement on time, impermanence, and human existence. The number "81" is deeply symbolic:

  • 81 represents the age of enlightenment in some Eastern philosophies, suggesting a wisdom gained through experience.
  • It also references the 81st year of the Korean lunar calendar, tying his work to cyclical time and renewal.
  • The composition of the paintings—often featuring 81 distinct marks, lines, or color fields—creates a sense of balance between structure and spontaneity.

Unlike traditional abstract artists who rely on pure emotion or randomness, Park Daesung’s approach is highly controlled yet organic. Each stroke in "81" feels intentional yet unpredictable, much like the human experience itself.

8 Actionable Strategies to Deepen Your Appreciation of Park Daesung’s Art

If you want to truly understand and engage with Park Daesung’s "81", here are eight practical strategies to elevate your experience:

1. Study the History of Abstract Expressionism (And How Park Daesung Differs)

Why? Abstract Expressionism was born in post-WWII America (1940s–50s) but has global roots. Park Daesung’s work bridges Eastern and Western traditions, making it unique.

How?

  • Read "The Secret Language of Color" by Jessica Hische to understand color psychology.
  • Compare Park Daesung’s "81" series to:
    • Jackson Pollock’s drip paintings (action-based).
    • Mark Rothko’s color fields (emotional depth).
    • Wassily Kandinsky’s abstract forms (spiritual symbolism).
  • Key difference: Park Daesung’s work is more structured yet retains spontaneity, unlike Pollock’s pure chaos.

2. Practice "Slow Looking" – A Technique Used by Top Art Curators

Why? Many people glance at abstract art without truly seeing it. Slow looking helps you discover hidden details.

How?

  • Stand 3–5 feet away from a Park Daesung painting.
  • Close one eye and focus on one section for 30 seconds.
  • Move slowly to the next area, noting:
    • How colors interact.
    • Where the brushstrokes are thickest/thinnest.
    • If any shapes remind you of natural forms (clouds, waves, etc.).
  • Repeat for 10–15 minutes to fully absorb the piece.

Example: In "81-04", the bottom-left corner has a delicate spiral that, when examined closely, resembles a seashell or a galaxy.

3. Experiment with Mixed Media Like Park Daesung

Why? Abstract artists often blend materials to create texture. Park Daesung uses ink, acrylic, and sometimes sand or charcoal to add depth.

How?

  • Try this at home:
    1. Mix acrylic paint with sand for a textured effect.
    2. Use a brush, sponge, or even your fingers to apply it.
    3. Layer colors (e.g., start with a cool blue base, then add warm orange splashes).
    4. Let it dry and observe how the textures interact.
  • Compare your work to "81" paintings—do you see similar organic, unpredictable patterns?

4. Learn the "Rule of Thirds" in Abstract Composition

Why? Even in abstraction, balance is key. Park Daesung often divides his canvases in ways that guide the viewer’s eye.

How?

  • Imagine a grid over a Park Daesung painting (divide it into 9 equal squares).
  • Notice where the most dynamic elements (bold colors, thick brushstrokes) fall.
  • Apply this to your own art by:
    • Placing key focal points along the grid lines.
    • Using negative space to create movement.

Example: In "81-12", the right-third section has a cluster of jagged lines that pull the viewer’s gaze before leading back to the center.

5. Explore the Psychology of Color in "81"

Why? Colors evoke emotions and memories. Park Daesung uses them strategically to create harmony or tension.

How?

  • Identify the dominant colors in "81" (e.g., deep blues, fiery reds, muted greens).
  • Research their meanings:
    • Blue = calm, introspection (often used in backgrounds).
    • Red = energy, passion (often contrasted with blues).
    • Black/White = balance, duality (used in negative space).
  • Try this exercise:
    • Pick one color from a Park Daesung painting.
    • Describe how it makes you feel.
    • Now, create a small abstract piece using only that color—how does it change the mood?

6. Study the Role of Scale in Abstract Art

Why? Park Daesung’s "81" paintings range from small studies (12x12 inches) to monumental works (8x10 feet). Scale affects perception.

How?

  • Compare a small "81" sketch to a large canvas:
    • Small works feel intimate, almost like a journal entry.
    • Large works create a sense of grandeur, making the viewer feel small yet connected.
  • Experiment with scale in your own work:
    • Start with a small canvas (9x12 inches).
    • Then, scale up—how does the same composition change when enlarged?

7. Understand the Role of Imperfection in Abstract Art

Why? Many beginners over-polish abstract pieces, losing the raw energy that makes them compelling. Park Daesung embraces imperfection.

How?

  • Look for "mistakes" in "81"—are they accidental splatters or intentional textures?
  • Try this technique:
    1. Paint a smooth background (e.g., a solid blue).
    2. Intentionally "mess up"—drip, smudge, or scratch the surface.
    3. See if the "imperfection" becomes part of the design.
  • Park Daesung’s advice (from interviews): "The best abstract art feels like a conversation between the artist and the universe—sometimes it’s structured, sometimes it’s wild."

8. Collect and Curate Your Own "81-Inspired" Gallery

Why? The best way to internalize abstract art is to live with it.

How?

  • Create a digital or physical gallery of:
    • Park Daesung’s "81" paintings.
    • Other abstract artists (e.g., Yayoi Kusama, Gerhard Richter, Joan Mitchell).
    • Your own abstract experiments.
  • Arrange them by:
    • Color palette.
    • Composition style.
    • Emotional impact.
  • Walk through your gallery daily—how does your perception change over time?
