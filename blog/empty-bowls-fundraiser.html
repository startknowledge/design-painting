Empty Bowls Fundraiser: A Creative & Impactful Way to Raise Awareness & Funds for Your Community

Introduction: The Power of Empty Bowls in Community Building & Philanthropy

In recent years, the Empty Bowls Fundraiser has emerged as one of the most meaningful and visually striking community service events. Originating in 1990 at the Ceramics Department of the University of Wisconsin–Stout, this simple yet profound concept has grown into a global movement, raising millions of dollars for local charities, food banks, and social causes.

According to Feeding America, over 40 million Americans experienced food insecurity in 2023—a 12% increase from 2020 (Feeding America, 2023). Meanwhile, Kiva.org reports that small-scale community fundraisers like Empty Bowls have collectively funded over $100 million in microloans and local initiatives since 2005. This proves that when creativity meets compassion, even the smallest gestures can create lasting change.

At Design-Painting, we believe that art and philanthropy should go hand in hand. Whether you're an artist, a nonprofit organizer, or a community leader, hosting an Empty Bowls Fundraiser is a fantastic way to: ✔ Support local food banks and hunger relief programs ✔ Showcase handmade ceramics and artistic talent ✔ Foster community engagement and unity ✔ Raise funds without traditional fundraising barriers

In this comprehensive guide, we’ll break down everything you need to know—from planning and execution to marketing strategies and common pitfalls—to ensure your Empty Bowls event is a resounding success.

Why Empty Bowls? The Philosophy Behind the Movement

Before diving into logistics, it’s essential to understand why Empty Bowls resonates so deeply with communities worldwide.

1. A Symbol of Unity & Shared Struggle

Each bowl represents one meal—a simple yet powerful reminder of the basic human need for nourishment. When participants eat from handmade bowls, they symbolically share a meal, reinforcing the idea that no one should go hungry.

2. Art as a Catalyst for Change

Unlike traditional fundraisers that rely solely on donations, Empty Bowls combines creativity with philanthropy. Artists contribute their time and skills, while donors experience the emotional connection of supporting a cause through beautiful, handcrafted pieces.

3. Low-Cost, High-Impact Fundraising

Unlike expensive galas or auctions, Empty Bowls requires minimal budget—just clay, kilns, and community effort. This makes it accessible for small towns, schools, and grassroots organizations.

4. A Memorable Experience for Participants

People remember events they feel part of, not just those they attend. Empty Bowls creates lasting memories—whether it’s the satisfaction of eating from a handmade bowl or the joy of knowing their donation helped someone in need.

Step-by-Step Guide: How to Organize a Successful Empty Bowls Fundraiser

Hosting an Empty Bowls event requires careful planning, but with the right strategies, you can turn it into a community highlight. Below, we’ll walk through 10 actionable steps to ensure your fundraiser is well-organized, engaging, and financially successful.

Step 1: Define Your Mission & Choose a Charity

Before anything else, decide who will benefit from your fundraiser.

How to Choose the Right Charity:

✅ Local Food Banks – Many communities have underfunded food banks that rely on grassroots support. ✅ Homeless Shelters & Soup Kitchens – These organizations often need ongoing funding for meals and supplies. ✅ School & Youth Programs – If you’re working with students, consider feeding programs in schools. ✅ Disaster Relief Funds – If a recent crisis (like a wildfire or hurricane) affected your area, a portion of proceeds can go toward recovery.

Example: The Portland Empty Bowls Fundraiser (Oregon) partners with Oregon Food Bank, donating over $100,000 annually to combat hunger in the region. Their event features local potters, sculptors, and ceramic artists, making it both an artistic and philanthropic success.

Pro Tip:

  • Reach out to multiple charities to see which one aligns best with your community’s needs.
  • Ask for a donation receipt template to provide to donors for tax deductions.

Step 2: Assemble Your Planning Team

No single person can handle everything—delegate responsibilities to ensure smooth execution.

Key Roles to Assign:

🔹 Event Coordinator – Oversees the entire fundraiser (budget, timeline, logistics). 🔹 Artistic Director – Works with artists to ensure consistent quality and themes. 🔹 Marketing Lead – Handles social media, flyers, and press outreach. 🔹 Volunteer Manager – Recruits and schedules helpers for setup, cleanup, and day-of support. 🔹 Fundraising Lead – Tracks donations, manages payment processing, and ensures transparency.

Example: The Empty Bowls Fundraiser at the University of Wisconsin–Stout (the original event) was organized by faculty, students, and local potters. Their team structure included:

  • A ceramics professor overseeing artistic standards.
  • Student volunteers handling registration and donations.
  • A local nonprofit liaison ensuring funds went directly to the food bank.

Common Mistake & Fix: ❌ Assuming one person can do everything → Leads to burnout and missed details. ✅ Assign clear roles and regular check-ins to keep everyone on track.

Step 3: Recruit Artists & Set Guidelines

The heart of Empty Bowls lies in the art—but quality and consistency matter.

How to Attract Artists:

🎨 Local Ceramic Studios & Pottery Classes – Partner with instructors to get students involved. 🎨 Art Schools & Universities – Many have ceramic departments eager to participate. 🎨 Community Art Groups – Facebook groups, Meetup, or local craft fairs often have talented volunteers. 🎨 Professional Potters – Some may donate their time for exposure or charity.

Guidelines to Provide Artists:

✔ Theme & Style – Decide if bowls should be unified (same color, design) or diverse (each artist’s unique style). ✔ Size & Material – Standardize bowl sizes (e.g., 6-8 inches in diameter) for easy eating. ✔ Pricing – Artists can set their own prices (e.g., $20–$100 per bowl), but encourage affordable options. ✔ Deadlines – Give artists 4–6 weeks to complete bowls before the event. ✔ Packaging – Provide sturdy boxes or bags to protect bowls during transport.

Example: The San Francisco Empty Bowls event features dozens of artists, including established potters and emerging talent. Their guidelines ensure:

  • All bowls are food-safe (glazed properly).
  • A mix of functional and decorative pieces to appeal to different buyers.
  • A "People’s Bowl" option (a $10 bowl made by a local student) to encourage participation.

Pro Tip:

  • Offer a "Silent Auction" option where artists can bid on their own work (with proceeds going to charity).
  • Provide a small stipend for artists who can’t donate their time but want to contribute.

Step 4: Plan the Event Logistics

Where, when, and how will your fundraiser take place?

Venue Considerations:

📍 Indoor Spaces (Best for Large Groups):

  • Community centers
  • Art galleries
  • School cafeterias
  • Ceramic studios

📍 Outdoor Spaces (Best for Warm Weather):

  • Parks with pavilions
  • Farmers' markets
  • University quad areas

Date & Time:

  • Weekend evenings (6–9 PM) work best for high attendance.
  • Avoid holidays when people may be traveling.
  • Give artists enough time (at least 4–6 weeks to create bowls).

Seating & Layout:

  • Round tables (to encourage conversation).
  • Signage labeling each artist’s work.
  • A donation station with clear instructions (cash, Venmo, PayPal, etc.).

Example: The Austin Empty Bowls event takes place in Zilker Park during the fall, attracting hundreds of attendees. Their setup includes:

  • A central stage for a live artist demonstration.
  • Tables arranged in a circular pattern to mimic a shared meal.
  • A "Donation Tree" where people hang tags with their contributions.

Common Mistake & Fix: ❌ Choosing a venue too small → Leads to crowding and discomfort. ✅ Book a space that can accommodate 1.5–2x your expected attendance.

Step 5: Set Pricing & Donation Structure

How will people pay for bowls and donate extra?

Pricing Strategies:

💰 Tiered Pricing – Offer $10, $20, $50, and $100+ bowls to appeal to different budgets. 💰 "People’s Bowl" – A $10 bowl made by a local student or beginner artist. 💰 Artist-Signed vs. Unsigned – Some artists may charge more for signed pieces.

Donation Options:

💳 Cash Box – Simple and effective. 💳 Venmo/PayPal – For digital donations. 💳 Square Reader – For credit/debit card payments. 💳 Honor System – A donation jar with suggested amounts (e.g., "Donate $5–$20 to feed a family").

Example: The Denver Empty Bowls event uses:

  • A mix of cash and digital payments (via Square and Venmo).
  • "Adopt-a-Bowl" donations where people can sponsor a bowl in someone else’s name.
  • A "Silent Auction" table where rare or high-end bowls are bid on.

Pro Tip:

  • **Display a clear breakdown of where money goes (e.g., "80% to food bank, 20% to event costs").
  • Offer a "Tax-Deductible Receipt" for donors (if applicable).

Step 6: Market Your Event Like a Pro

Even the best fundraiser won’t succeed without promotion. Here’s how to maximize attendance and donations.

1. Social Media Strategy (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok)

  • Post teaser videos of artists at work.
  • Share behind-the-scenes content (e.g., "Meet the potters!").
  • Use hashtags like #EmptyBowls #CommunityFundraiser #ArtForACause.
  • Run a countdown leading up to the event.

2. Local Press & Community Outreach

  • Pitch to newspapers, radio stations, and TV news (highlight the local impact).
  • Partner with influencers (local food bloggers, artists, or activists).
  • Send press releases to nonprofit and community boards.

3. Email & Direct Mail Campaigns

  • Collect emails from past attendees or local art groups.
  • Send a "Save the Date" email with RSVP details.
  • Mail postcards to senior centers, schools, and businesses.

4. Collaborate with Local Businesses

  • Sponsorships – Ask restaurants, breweries, or art supply stores to sponsor a table or donate prizes.
  • Cross-Promotion – Partner with a local café to host a pre-event tasting night.

Example: The Seattle Empty Bowls event gained massive traction by:

  • Featuring a live stream of the event on Facebook and Instagram.
  • **Partnering with The Stranger (a local newspaper) for a feature story.
  • Offering a "Sponsor a Bowl" package where businesses could brand a bowl for $200.

Common Mistake & Fix: ❌ Relying only on social media → Misses older demographics. ✅ **Use a multi-channel approach (email, press, word-of-mouth).

Step 7: Day-of-Event Execution

The big day is here—keep things running smoothly with these tips.

Pre-Event Checklist:

✅ Confirm artist arrivals (have a backup plan if someone cancels). ✅ Set up tables, signs, and donation stations. ✅ Test sound equipment (if there’s live music or announcements). ✅ **Have a first-aid kit and extra napkins/utensils. ✅ **Assign a photographer (or ask a volunteer to document the event).

During the Event:

🎤 Welcome Speech – Thank artists, volunteers, and donors. 🍽️ Demonstration – Have an artist show how bowls are made (engages kids and first-timers). 💰 Donation Drive – Encourage impulse donations ("Every $20 feeds a family for a week!"). 📸 Social Media Wall – Set up a hashtag board (#YourCityEmptyBowls) for real-time updates.

Example: The Chicago Empty Bowls event includes:

  • **A live pottery wheel demo by a local artist.
  • A "Donation Match" challenge where a sponsor doubles the first $1,000 raised.
  • **A photo booth with props (bowls, aprons, "I Fed Someone" signs).

Pro Tip:

  • **Have a designated person handle donation tracking (use a spreadsheet or app like GoFundMe or Classy).
  • **Offer a light snack (water, fruit, or cookies) to keep energy up.

Step 8: Post-Event Follow-Up & Impact Reporting

The event is over—but your work isn’t done yet.

1. Thank You Notes

  • Email artists & volunteers with a thank-you and photos.
  • **Send a personalized receipt to donors (if tax-deductible).
  • **Post a recap video on social media.

2. Financial Transparency

  • Share the total raised and how much went to charity.
  • Provide a breakdown (e.g., "75% to food bank, 15% to event costs, 10% to artist stipends").
