Gerhard Richter’s Hexagonal Doha Pavilion: How His Strip Paintings Inspired a Revolutionary Architectural Masterpiece

In the heart of the Middle East, where tradition meets modernity, a striking architectural marvel emerged in 2021: the Hexagonal Doha Pavilion for Expo 2020. Designed by the renowned studio BIG (Bjarke Ingels Group), this pavilion was not just a temporary structure—it was a bold statement, a fusion of art and architecture that left visitors in awe. But what many might not realize is that its geometric form was deeply inspired by the abstract paintings of one of the most celebrated artists of our time: Gerhard Richter.

Richter, a German painter whose work spans abstract expressionism, photorealism, and conceptual art, has long been a bridge between the visual arts and broader cultural discourse. His Strip Paintings, created between 1965 and 1974, are a series of large-scale canvases characterized by their horizontal stripes of varying colors and widths. These works, which Richter described as "a kind of visual shorthand," have now found an unexpected but profound second life in the geometric language of the Doha Pavilion. This article dives deep into the fascinating intersection of Richter’s art and this architectural icon, exploring the creative process, the significance of the pavilion, and what it means for the future of art-infused design.

Whether you're an art enthusiast, an architecture lover, or simply curious about how creativity transcends disciplines, this exploration will reveal how Richter’s abstract vision became the blueprint for a structure that redefined Expo 2020.

The Genesis of the Doha Pavilion: A Vision for Expo 2020

Expo 2020, originally scheduled for 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic, was a global celebration of human achievement with the theme "Connecting Minds, Creating the Future." Held in Dubai, the event featured pavilions from 192 countries, each designed to reflect its nation’s identity, culture, and aspirations. Among these, the Qatar Pavilion stood out—not just for its striking design but for its collaboration with a world-renowned artist.

The pavilion was designed by BIG (Bjarke Ingels Group), a Copenhagen-based architecture firm known for its innovative and playful approach to urban design. BIG’s founder, Bjarke Ingels, has often emphasized the importance of joy and playfulness in architecture, and the Doha Pavilion was no exception. However, what made this project truly unique was the decision to incorporate the geometric language of Gerhard Richter’s Strip Paintings into its structural form.

At first glance, the pavilion appears to be a hexagonal prism, a geometric shape that evokes both strength and fluidity. But closer inspection reveals a more complex pattern: the exterior is composed of hexagonal modules arranged in a way that mimics the rhythmic repetition of Richter’s stripes. This wasn’t just a decorative choice—it was a deliberate homage to Richter’s work, transforming his abstract art into a tangible architectural experience.

Gerhard Richter’s Strip Paintings: The Artistic Foundation

To understand how Richter’s art influenced the Doha Pavilion, it’s essential to explore his Strip Paintings in detail. Created during Richter’s early abstract phase, these works are part of a series that also includes his Color Charts and Blast Paintings. The Strip Paintings are particularly notable for their monochromatic or near-monochromatic horizontal bands, which Richter created using a technique he called "hand-painting."

Richter described his process as follows:

"I paint with my hands, not with my head. The stripes are not about precision—they are about the emotional charge of the color and the way it interacts with the space around it."

These paintings are not just visually striking; they also carry symbolic weight. Richter has spoken about how the stripes represent the passage of time, memory, and the subconscious. For example, his 1968 Strip Painting (now in the collection of the Tate Modern) features a single horizontal stripe of red against a white background, a stark contrast that evokes both simplicity and intensity.

The Strip Paintings were created between 1965 and 1974, a period when Richter was experimenting with abstraction after initially gaining fame for his photorealistic works. This shift marked a turning point in his career, as he sought to explore the boundaries of perception and emotion through color and form.

How Richter’s Art Translated into Architecture

The connection between Richter’s Strip Paintings and the Doha Pavilion is not immediately obvious—after all, one is a work of art, and the other is a structural design. However, the collaboration between BIG and Richter’s estate (managed by Gerhard Richter Archive) was rooted in a shared appreciation for geometric abstraction and the power of repetition.

Here’s how the translation happened:

  1. Geometric Abstraction as a Language: Both Richter’s Strip Paintings and the pavilion rely on repetitive geometric patterns. Richter’s stripes are horizontal, while the pavilion’s hexagons are three-dimensional, but the rhythmic repetition of forms creates a similar visual and emotional impact.
  2. Color as a Unifying Element: Richter often used limited color palettes in his Strip Paintings, creating a sense of harmony and balance. The Doha Pavilion’s exterior features a neutral color scheme (primarily white and gray), which allows the geometric form to dominate while subtly echoing Richter’s use of monochromatic bands.
  3. Emotional Resonance: Richter’s stripes are not just decorative—they carry emotional weight. Similarly, the pavilion’s hexagonal form is designed to evoke a sense of wonder and contemplation, inviting visitors to engage with the space in a way that feels both intellectual and intuitive.
  4. Functional Aesthetics: The pavilion’s hexagonal modules are not purely decorative—they serve a structural and functional purpose. The repetition of hexagons allows for efficient use of materials while maintaining a visually striking design. This aligns with Richter’s approach, where form and function often intersect in unexpected ways.

Bjarke Ingels has described the collaboration as an example of "art as a catalyst for architecture". By incorporating Richter’s geometric language, the pavilion becomes more than just a building—it becomes a visual dialogue between two disciplines.

The Creative Process: From Canvas to Pavilion

The journey from Richter’s Strip Paintings to the Doha Pavilion was a collaborative one, involving architects, artists, and engineers. Here’s a breakdown of the key steps:

Step 1: Initial Inspiration and Research

BIG’s team began by studying Richter’s Strip Paintings, particularly his works from the 1960s and early 1970s. They focused on:

  • The rhythmic repetition of stripes.
  • The emotional contrast between colors and backgrounds.
  • The sense of movement created by the horizontal bands.

They also explored Richter’s Color Charts, which use gradients and transitions to create a sense of depth. These works influenced the pavilion’s gradual shifts in scale and proportion as visitors move through the space.

Step 2: Translating 2D to 3D

One of the biggest challenges was translating Richter’s two-dimensional stripes into a three-dimensional structure. BIG’s solution was to:

  • Use hexagonal modules instead of stripes, creating a tessellating pattern that mimics the repetition of Richter’s bands.
  • Incorporate light and shadow to evoke the depth and texture of Richter’s paintings.
  • Design the pavilion’s interior to guide visitors through a visual journey, much like how Richter’s stripes draw the eye across the canvas.

Step 3: Material and Structural Considerations

The pavilion’s exterior is clad in white steel panels, which reflect light and create a shifting appearance depending on the angle of view. This choice was influenced by Richter’s use of matte and glossy surfaces in his paintings, where texture plays a crucial role in the overall effect.

The hexagonal form also allowed for efficient load distribution, ensuring structural integrity while maintaining the desired aesthetic. Engineers worked closely with BIG to ensure that the pavilion could withstand the extreme temperatures and winds of the Middle East.

Step 4: Finalizing the Design

Before finalizing the design, BIG and the Richter estate conducted virtual reality simulations to test how the pavilion would appear from different angles. This step was crucial in ensuring that the geometric language of Richter’s art was faithfully represented in the architecture.

The result was a pavilion that feels both familiar and unfamiliar—a structure that visitors recognize as geometric yet also uniquely inspired by Richter’s abstract vision.

The Significance of the Doha Pavilion: Why This Collaboration Matters

The Doha Pavilion is more than just an architectural feat—it represents a new paradigm in art and design collaboration. Here’s why this project is significant:

1. Bridging Art and Architecture

For decades, art and architecture have existed in separate spheres, each with its own language and techniques. The Doha Pavilion proves that these disciplines can—and should—collaborate. By integrating Richter’s abstract art into the pavilion’s design, BIG created a hybrid experience that engages visitors on both an intellectual and emotional level.

2. Celebrating Contemporary Art in Public Spaces

Expo 2020 was a global stage for cultural exchange, and the Doha Pavilion’s collaboration with Richter elevated the event’s artistic profile. It demonstrated how contemporary art can inspire public spaces, making them more than just functional structures—they become cultural landmarks.

3. Exploring the Power of Geometric Abstraction

Richter’s Strip Paintings are a testament to the universal appeal of geometric abstraction. The pavilion’s hexagonal form shows how this language can be scaled up from a canvas to a building, proving that abstraction is not limited to the art world—it has real-world applications in design and urban planning.

4. Setting a Precedent for Future Collaborations

This project has inspired other architects and artists to explore cross-disciplinary collaborations. Since the pavilion’s completion, there have been discussions about similar partnerships for future events, such as the 2025 Expo in Osaka and the 2030 World Expo in Dubai. The Doha Pavilion has shown that art can be a driving force in architecture, not just a decorative element.
